# 凶 — `ominous`

**58 times in the judgments and line texts, and absent from 26 hexagrams.** The formulae are 貞凶
10 and 征凶 10 ("setting out: ominous"), 有凶 3 and 終凶 1. It is the partner of 吉, and
the two stand in one line seven times (3.5, 6.J, 8.J, 31.2, 32.5, 44.1, 49.6).

**Its place in the set.** It is the other half of 繫辭上 ch 2's 吉凶者，失得之象也, "吉 and 凶 are
the images of losing and getting." So it takes a word from the same register as 吉's: **a sign that
the way ahead is bad**, not the harm itself. See [`吉-ji.md`](吉-ji.md) and
[`WORKLIST-CLOSED.md` § A4](../WORKLIST-CLOSED.md#a4--the-verdict-vocabulary--done).

## The evidence

**The graph is a pit.** 說文: 惡也。象地穿交陷其中也, "bad. A picture of the ground broken open,
with something crossed fallen into it." The 陷 there is 坎's action in 說卦 ch 7, falling in. Meet
the image: the graph of 凶 shows something caught in a hole in the ground.

**凶 is an outcome, and it is not a fault.** 28.6 reads 過涉滅頂，凶，无咎, "wading over, the water
closes over the crown: ominous; no blame." 47.2 has 征凶，无咎 too. The 小象傳 at 28.6 says so
outright: 過涉之凶，不可咎也, "the ominousness of wading over cannot be blamed." So a moral word
for 凶 says something the text denies twice, in two of its layers.

**The two English witnesses split, and neither gives the reading.** Across the 38 lines whose only
verdict graph is 凶:

| | renders 凶 as |
|---|---|
| Legge | *evil* 37 of 38 |
| McClatchie | *unlucky* 30, *evil* 1, nothing legible in 7 |
| de Harlez | *funeste* 6, *malheur* 4, *mauvais* 4, *mal* 3, *désastre* 2, and nothing in 16 |

Legge's word is moral, and 28.6 rules it out. McClatchie's is chance, and 繫辭下 ch 9 rules it out
(愛惡相攻而吉凶生: 吉 and 凶 arise from love and hate attacking each other). De Harlez scatters
across five words, and that scatter is his denial of the oracle showing.

## The ruling — `ominous`, Shalom's call, 2026-10-20

**It pairs with *auspicious* without being its negation.** The Chinese sets 吉 and 凶 as opposites,
so the English may follow it, but *inauspicious* defines 凶 as the absence of 吉. The graph is not
an absence. It is a pit with something in it. *Ominous* is the plain, strong word, and it holds at
the lines where 凶 falls hardest: 迷復，凶 (24.6), "a lost return: ominous."

**It keeps 凶 off every outcome noun the text needs for something else.** 災 occurs five times in
the core (24.6, 25.3 twice, 56.1, 62.6), 眚 six and 害 once. 24.6 has 凶 and 災眚 in one line.
*Disaster*, *calamity* and *harm* belong to those graphs.

## What is ruled out, and why

- **"evil"** puts a moral verdict where the Chinese has an outcome. That is
  [`no-verdict-the-chinese-lacks`](../method.md#6-the-principles) in one word, and 28.6 shows the
  text denying it.
- **"unlucky"** imports chance, against 繫辭下 ch 9. It is the same objection as *lucky* for 吉.
- **"disaster", "misfortune", "calamity"** are not on the `forbidden:` list, because they are the
  plain English for 災 and 眚, which stand in the same lines (24.6, 62.6). They are ruled out for
  凶 because they would collide with those graphs.
- **"inauspicious"** has no defect but a weak one: it names 凶 by 吉.

## The seam

**For the Tao Te Ching side.** The parent renders 凶 *disaster* (chs 16, 30) and *inauspicious*
(ch 31, 凶事). Its *disaster* is also its word for 禍 (chs 46, 58, 69). That is two characters on one
word over there, and it is reported here rather than fixed.

**And *ominous* shares a root with *omen*, where the Chinese shares none.** The parent's *ill omen*
is 不祥 (ch 31), and 祥 occurs once in this core text (10.6 視履考祥, "looking at the treading,
examining the signs"). An English reader may hear a kinship between 凶 and 祥 that the Chinese
does not have. The notes at 10 should say so.
