# 吉 — `auspicious`

**The commonest verdict in the book: 147 times in the judgments and line texts, in 60 of the 64
hexagrams.** Only 20, 23, 29 and 43 never use it. It mostly comes in formulae: 貞吉 34 times (the
fork in 貞 is A6), 元吉 14, 終吉 10 ("in the end, auspicious"), 大吉 5 ("greatly auspicious", on the
lock that makes 大 *great*), 吉无不利 4. A word chosen here is chosen 147 times, and 139 more in
the Wings.

**Its place in the set.** 繫辭上 ch 2 pairs it with 凶: 吉凶者，失得之象也, "吉 and 凶 are the
images of losing and getting." The Wings read the pair as **signs**, images (象) read off the figure,
and not as the outcome itself. 繫辭下 ch 9 says where those signs come from: 愛惡相攻而吉凶生, "love
and hate attack each other, and 吉 and 凶 arise." So they are not chance. The Chinese sets 吉 and 凶
as opposites, so the English may follow it ([`README.md`](../README.md#the-polarity-is-in-the-lines)).
**auspicious · ominous** follows it as a pair in one register, the register of signs, and not as a
word and its negation. The whole set is laid out in [`WORKLIST-CLOSED.md` § A4](../WORKLIST-CLOSED.md#a4--the-verdict-vocabulary--done).

## The evidence

**The graph.** 說文: 善也。从士、口, "good; from 士 and 口." The graph says *good*, and that is
exactly why the English cannot be *good*. The parent's lock on 善 keeps *good* for where a text
names the category ([`sources/locks/`](../sources/locks/README.md)), and 吉 would spend that word
147 times on a different character.

**The three translators converge, and the convergence is on the meaning.** Across the 95 lines
whose only verdict graph is 吉:

| | renders 吉 as |
|---|---|
| Legge | *good fortune* 74, *great good fortune* 13, *fortunate* 6, *success* 2 |
| McClatchie | *luck* / *lucky* 95 of 97 |
| de Harlez | *heureux* / *bonheur* 41 of 86, *bien* 9, and nothing at all in 27: he strips the formula, as he says the restored text should |

All three read a favourable outcome, and that reading is well supported
([`convergence-is-evidence`](../principles/convergence-is-evidence.md)). *Auspicious* keeps that
reading. It departs from their **word**, for two reasons that are both properties of the words.

## The ruling — `auspicious`, Shalom's call, 2026-10-20

**First, *fortune* is 福's.** 福 occurs four times in the core text (11.3, 35.2, 48.3, 63.5). The
Tao Te Ching translation renders it *fortune* (ch 58) and *good fortune* (ch 65). At 35.2 the two
characters stand in one line: 貞吉。受茲介福, "holding firm: auspicious. Receiving this great
fortune." Render 吉 as *good fortune* and that line says fortune twice, for two graphs.

**Second, the Wings read 吉 as a sign, and *auspicious* is a sign word.** Read off the figure, it
says the way ahead is favourable. It does not report luck. The Tao Te Ching already uses the same
word for the same character: 吉事尚左 (ch 31), "auspicious occasions honour the left." So the two
books agree here.

## What is ruled out, and why

- **"lucky"** imports chance. 繫辭下 ch 9 says 吉 and 凶 arise from conduct, from love and hate
  attacking each other, and a word for chance cannot say that. McClatchie's *lucky* / *unlucky* is
  the cleanest pair any of the three made, and this is the one thing wrong with it.
- **"good fortune"** collides with 福 inside 35.2. It is not on the `forbidden:` list, because 福
  itself may want it, and the check would then bar 福's English from every file that cites this entry.
- **"good"** is 善's, by lock.

## The seam

**The graph says *good*; the English says *favourable sign*.** *Auspicious* is the Wings' framing
(失得之象), and that is the declared lens ([`README.md`](../README.md#the-lens)). The plain
goodness of 說文's 善 is not in the English. Where a line plainly means "this goes well" and
nothing more, the notes may say so.
