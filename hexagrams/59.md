## The signature — a reading

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-24. This reads the figure through the Wings — the declared lens
([`README.md`](../README.md#the-lens)) — and it is a reading, not a ruling. The name still comes from
the lines ([`read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums`](../principles/read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums.md)).*

**The signature.** Within: **Water** (坎 · yang · sinking in). Without: **Wind** (巽 · yin · entering).
**Across** — water ↔ fire and thunder ↔ wind, yang within and yin without.

**What the tradition reads.** The 大象 (*dà xiàng*): 風行水上，渙；先王以享于帝立廟 (*fēng xíng shuǐ shàng, huàn; xiān wáng yǐ xiǎng yú dì lì miào*), "wind moves over the water: 渙; the former rulers made offerings to the god (帝, *dì*) and set up temples." The 彖 (*tuàn*): 剛來而不窮 (*gāng lái ér bù qióng*), "the firm comes and does not run out", and 利涉大川，乘木有功也 (*lì shè dà chuān, chéng mù yǒu gōng yě*), "it is favourable to cross the great river: riding on wood gets results" — 說卦 (*shuōguà*) makes wind wood, 巽為木 (*xùn wéi mù*). The tradition reads a dispersal answered twice: by gathering at the temple, and by crossing on a boat.

**What the polarity implies.** Water within is firm at its centre and sinks in: it is what has settled, pooled, gone down into the pit. Wind without is open at its base and enters from above, so the figure lays the wind's open underside straight onto the water. Wind does not stop water; it breaks the surface, drives it apart in ripple and spray, and dries what was wet. That is the 渙 of the lines — what had pooled or set is loosened: 渙汗其大號 (*huàn hàn qí dà hào*), "his great cry pours out like sweat"; 渙其群 (*huàn qí qún*), "he scatters his flock", the figure's best omen; 渙其血，去逖出 (*huàn qí xuè, qù tì chū*), "he scatters his blood; goes, keeps far, gets out." Water is the danger trigram (陷, *xiàn*, "sinking in"), and the structure suggests that 渙 is how danger is left: not climbed out of but lifted off from above, as wind lifts water from a pool. Because the pole within is yang, what gets scattered has a firm core — a crowd, a clot, a hoard — and the 彖's 剛來而不窮 says the firm survives it. The form breaks; the substance goes on.

**For the name.** *Scattering* — the signature supports it and says why it is transitive and why it is good. **Candidates the signature suggests:** *Dissolving* — survives: 渙其群 "dissolving his flock", 渙奔其机 (*huàn bēn qí jī*) "in the dissolving, he runs to his bench", though it softens 渙其血, which is not gentle; *Thawing* — fails the lines: "thawing his flock" (渙其群) and "thawing his blood" (渙其血) make the water image the name and lose the verb's object.

## The heart of it

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-25 — an interpretive reading, not a translation. Each claim is marked by
layer: the core text (the judgment and the six lines, Western Zhou), the Wings (Warring States to
Han), 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*, 3rd century), and echoes of the Tao Te Ching in this project's own translation.
The formula words — 吉, 凶, 无咎, 亨, 孚, 貞 — carry working glosses until WORKLIST A4 and A6 rule on
them ([`hexagram-names.md`](../hexagram-names.md)).*

### Essence

**What has massed is broken up and carried off — the flock, the self, the hoard, the blood — and
the scattering, rightly done, is how a larger gathering begins.** The judgment sets 渙 (*huàn*)
beside its opposite in the same breath: 王假有廟 (*wáng gé yǒu miào*, "the ruler comes to the
temple"), the place where the scattered are gathered again. The fourth line says it outright:
scatter the flock and 渙有丘 (*huàn yǒu qiū*, "the scattering makes a mound"). 說文 (*shuōwén*)
gives the graph as 流𢿱也 (*liú sàn yě*, "flowing apart"), water with 奐 (*huàn*) for its sound.
And the figure is built on the hazard trigram, yet no line in it is ominous. Scattering is the way
out.

### The polarities in it

Water within, wind without. Water is firm at its centre and sinks in: what has pooled, settled,
clotted. Wind is open at its base and enters from above: it lays its open side straight onto the
water. The 大象 (*dà xiàng*) says it: 風行水上 (*fēng xíng shuǐ shàng*, "wind moves over the water").
Wind does not stop water or drain it; it breaks its surface and carries it off in spray and vapour.
The 彖 (*tuàn*) adds what the figure keeps: 剛來而不窮 (*gāng lái ér bù qióng*, "the firm comes and
does not run out"). The shape that held is loosened; the substance goes on.

### In nature

說卦 (*shuōguà*) gives the wind its work: 風以散之 (*fēng yǐ sàn zhī*, "wind scatters them"). Watch a
pond on a morning that starts still. The first breath comes as a dark patch moving across the
surface, and where it passes, the reflection breaks into a thousand pieces. Watch washing dry on a
line: in a steady wind it dries far faster than in still sun. Wind over water carries the water
away.

And watch what the scattered makes. After a snowfall in wind, the open field is scoured almost
bare, and all that snow lies heaped in the lee of a hedge or a wall, sometimes higher than a person.
Sand does the same on a beach and builds dunes. The wind that took the snow off the field made the
drift. That is 渙有丘 exactly: scattering that makes a mound, 匪夷所思 (*fěi yí suǒ sī*, "not what the
ordinary would think of").

The fifth line's sweat is the same thing in a body. 說文's 汗 (*hàn*) is 人液也 (*rén yè yě*, "the
body's liquid"). When a fever breaks, heat leaves as sweat, drying on the skin: water scattered by
air, and the crisis passes.

### The movement of the lines

**The judgment.** 渙：亨。王假有廟，利涉大川，利貞 (*huàn: hēng. wáng gé yǒu miào, lì shè dà chuān, lì
zhēn*) — "Scattering *(proposed)* gets through. The ruler comes to the temple. Advantage in crossing
the great river; advantage in holding firm." Two answers to scattering: gather at the shrine, and
cross over. The 彖 has the crossing ride on wood, 乘木有功也 (*chéng mù yǒu gōng yě*, "riding on
wood gets results") — the boat.

**Line 1** gets out early. 用拯馬壯，吉 (*yòng zhěng mǎ zhuàng, jí*, "use it to rescue: the horse is
strong — auspicious"). The same words stand at 36.2. 王弼: at the start the break is not yet deep,
so one can still ride clear.

**Line 2** runs to what holds it up. 渙奔其机，悔亡 (*huàn bēn qí jī, huǐ wáng*, "in the scattering,
running to one's bench; regret goes"). 王弼: 机承物者也 (*jī chéng wù zhě yě*, "the bench is what
bears things") — the low support to lean on.

**Line 3** lets the self go. 渙其躬，无悔 (*huàn qí gōng, wú huǐ*, "scattering one's own body — no
regret"). The 小象 (*xiǎo xiàng*): 志在外也 (*zhì zài wài yě*, "the intent is outside"). What is
loosened now is self-concern.

**Line 4** is the turn, and the best omen. 渙其群，元吉。渙有丘，匪夷所思 (*huàn qí qún, yuán jí.
huàn yǒu qiū, fěi yí suǒ sī*, "scattering the flock — auspicious from the source. The scattering makes a
mound: not what the ordinary would think of"). 說文 builds 羣 (*qún*, "flock") on sheep, and makes
丘 (*qiū*) 土之高也，非人所爲也 (*tǔ zhī gāo yě, fēi rén suǒ wéi yě*, "a height of earth, not made by
people"). Break up the faction, and a hill no one built rises.

**Line 5** pours out. 渙汗其大號，渙王居，无咎 (*huàn hàn qí dà hào, huàn wáng jū, wú jiù*, "the great
cry pours out like sweat; the ruler's dwelling is opened — no blame"). 說文's 號 (*hào*) is a call,
built with the tiger. 渙王居 (*huàn wáng jū*) reads two ways: the ruler's stores given out, or 王弼's
reading, the ruler alone holding the centre of the scattering. Either way the cry, once out, like
sweat, does not go back in.

**Line 6** goes far. 渙其血，去逖出，无咎 (*huàn qí xuè, qù tì chū, wú jiù*, "scattering the blood;
go, keep far, get out — no blame"). 說文's 血 (*xuè*) is sacrificial blood; 王弼 reads it as
grief and harm dispersed. The 小象: 遠害也 (*yuǎn hài yě*, "keeping far from harm").

### In a life now

This is the figure for breaking up what has hardened: a faction in a workplace, a clique in a
family, a hoard that should be given out, a grievance that has clotted, a fever that has to break.
It is also the figure for what follows: a household scattered by a death, which gathers at the
funeral (the judgment's temple); a people dispersed, who keep a shrine or a festival to come back
to. The text counsels getting out early while it is easy (line 1), leaning on what really bears
weight (line 2), letting self-concern go (line 3), and trusting that dissolving the small group
can make a larger one (line 4). It says that the leader's word, once given, is out like sweat (line
5), and that sometimes the right move is simply distance (line 6).

The 大象 turns it to practice: 先王以享于帝立廟 (*xiān wáng yǐ xiǎng yú dì lì miào*, "the former
rulers made offerings to the god and set up temples"). When things scatter, build the place they
can come back to.

### An echo from the Laozi

**The same graph, in a different grammar.** The Laozi's chapter 15 describes the ancients who were
masterful: *"Loosening, like ice about to melt."* The word is 渙 (*huàn*), and Shalom's *loosening*
answers to it; the ice beside it is the image of 說文's 流𢿱 (*liú sàn*, "flowing apart") coming true
in spring. Both books see a hard thing come apart and take it as good. **But they part.** The
Laozi's 渙 is a quality of a person, gentle and without object. 59's 渙 is something done — to a
flock, a body, a ruler's stores, blood — and at its top it is not gentle at all. The signature
reading set aside *Thawing* for the name for this reason; the Laozi's ice does not come back in by
the side door.
