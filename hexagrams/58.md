## The signature — a reading

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-24. This reads the figure through the Wings — the declared lens
([`README.md`](../README.md#the-lens)) — and it is a reading, not a ruling. The name still comes from
the lines ([`read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums`](../principles/read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums.md)).*

**The signature.** Within: **Lake** (兌 · yin · releasing). Without: **Lake** (兌 · yin · releasing).
**Doubled** — mountain ↔ lake, the open pole twice.

**What the tradition reads.** The 彖 (*tuàn*) glosses the name with the action and places it: 兌，說也。剛中而柔外 (*duì, yuè yě. gāng zhōng ér róu wài*), "兌 is 說 — delight, release, speech; firm at the centre and yielding outside." It follows delight into public use: 說以先民，民忘其勞 (*yuè yǐ xiān mín, mín wàng qí láo*), "when delight goes before the people, the people forget their toil." The 大象 (*dà xiàng*) reads the doubling as company: 麗澤，兌；君子以朋友講習 (*lì zé, duì; jūnzǐ yǐ péngyǒu jiǎng xí*), "lakes joined together: 兌; the noble one talks things over and practises with friends."

**What the polarity implies.** A lake is firm beneath and open at the top; its mouth is its surface. Doubled, there is no mountain anywhere in the figure — nothing that holds — so what keeps release from simply spilling cannot be a rim. The 彖 finds it underneath: the firm lines sit at the bottom and centre of each trigram (剛中), so this is delight with a bed under it, not a surface without depth. And since nothing restrains it, the lines sort delight by its source instead: 和兌 (*hé duì*), "in harmony", auspicious; 孚兌 (*fú duì*), with 孚 (*fú*, its English pending), auspicious; 來兌 (*lái duì*), "that comes to you", ominous; 商兌 (*shāng duì*), "bargained over", 未寧 (*wèi níng*), "not yet settled"; 引兌 (*yǐn duì*), "drawn out", with no verdict at all. The doubling itself is an exchange. 說卦 (*shuōguà*) gives the lake the mouth, 兌為口 (*duì wéi kǒu*), "兌 is the mouth", and says 說言乎兌 (*yuè yán hū duì*), "in 兌 things are glad and speak." Two lakes are two mouths: the 大象's 講習. The structure suggests the three senses of 說 are one act seen three ways — opening, being glad of it, saying it — and that this is the one figure where the act happens between two parties. Its danger is the fifth line's 孚于剝 (*fú yú bō*): 孚 given to what strips away — gladness and speech trusted where they should not be.

**For the name.** *Delight* — the signature strains it slightly: the doubling makes the figure an exchange, and *Delight* is felt by one party. **Candidates the signature suggests:** *Talk* — survives: 和兌 "harmonious talk", 來兌 "talk that comes to you", 商兌，未寧 "bargaining talk, not yet settled", 引兌 "talk drawn out" — every line reads, and 商 (*shāng*, "to bargain, to weigh") reads better than under *Delight*; *Opening* — fails the lines: 孚兌 and 商兌 as "an opening with 孚" and "a bargained opening" lose what the modifiers qualify. (*Release*, the action's own word, is taken by 40 解.)

## The heart of it

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-25 — an interpretive reading, not a translation. Each claim is marked by
layer: the core text (the judgment and the six lines, Western Zhou), the Wings (Warring States to
Han), 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*, 3rd century), and echoes of the Tao Te Ching in this project's own translation.
The formula words — 吉, 凶, 无咎, 亨, 孚, 貞 — carry working glosses until WORKLIST A4 and A6 rule on
them ([`hexagram-names.md`](../hexagram-names.md)).*

### Essence

**An opening at the surface — gladness, speech, a breath let go — and the six lines ask one
question of it: where does it come from?** The judgment is almost bare, 兌：亨。利貞 (*duì: hēng. lì
zhēn*, "gets through; advantage in holding firm"). The lines do the work: each sets 兌 (*duì*) after
a word that names its source — answering, confident, coming to court, bargained, drawn out — and
the fortune follows the source, not the gladness. 說文 (*shuōwén*) glosses 兌 with 說 (*yuè / shuō*),
and glosses 說 in turn as 釋也 (*shì yě*, "to loosen, let go") with a second sense, 一曰談說 (*yī
yuē tán shuō*, "also: talk"). Release, delight and speech are one opening in this graph. The lower
part of 兌 is a standing person, 儿 (*rén*); modern readers see breath leaving a mouth above it,
where 說文 sees only a sound.

### The polarities in it

Lake within, lake without: each trigram two firm lines under an open one — a bed below, a mouth at
the top. There is no mountain anywhere, so nothing in the figure keeps the opening shut; whatever
governs gladness here must come from underneath. The 彖 (*tuàn*) says so: 剛中而柔外 (*gāng zhōng ér
róu wài*, "firm at the centre, yielding outside"). The lines bear it out, mostly. The firm lines
(1, 2, 4) find gladness that holds; the two open lines, the mouths themselves, are gladness all
surface — at 3 courting (ominous), at 6 drawn out (no verdict at all). **But line 5 is firm, central
and upright, and in peril.** Here structure and text part, and the text wins: the 小象 (*xiǎo
xiàng*) even says 位正當也 (*wèi zhèng dāng yě*, "the position is right and fitting") — a right
position is exactly what makes misplaced confidence dangerous.

### In nature

The 大象 (*dà xiàng*) image is 麗澤 (*lì zé*, "lakes joined together"): not one lake but a chain,
water passing from one basin to the next. Look at a string of lakes in a glaciated valley or a run
of mill-ponds: each is still at the surface, and each is fed and emptied by the others. A lake is
never only itself; its surface is where it trades — with the air, as the mist that lifts off it on
a cold morning, with the sky it gives back, with the birds that land on it.

說卦 (*shuōguà*) gives 兌 mid-autumn, 兌，正秋也 (*duì, zhèng qiū yě*, "兌 is full autumn"), when
萬物之所說也 (*wàn wù zhī suǒ yuè yě*, "the countless things are glad"). Watch a lake then. In a
temperate climate, as autumn cools the surface, the colder water sinks and the whole lake turns
over, top water down to the bed and bed water up to the air. The opening and the depth are not two
things; in autumn they change places. Notice it on a still October morning, when the surface steams.

### The movement of the lines

**The judgment.** 兌：亨。利貞 — "Delight *(proposed)* gets through; advantage in holding firm." The
opening is good; it needs a firm bed.

**Line 1** answers. 和兌，吉 (*hé duì, jí*, "answering gladness — auspicious"). 說文 makes 和 (*hé*)
相譍也 (*xiāng yìng yě*, "voices responding to each other"), and builds it on the mouth, 口 (*kǒu*): this is gladness in
the sense of speech, call and reply. The 小象: 行未疑也 (*xíng wèi yí yě*, "its going is not yet
doubted").

**Line 2** has confidence. 孚兌，吉，悔亡 (*fú duì, jí, huǐ wáng*, "confident gladness — auspicious;
regret goes"). Delight in its plainest sense, between people who can rely on each other; the 小象
has 信志也 (*xìn zhì yě*, "the intent is trusted").

**Line 3** comes courting. 來兌，凶 (*lái duì, xiōng*, "gladness that comes to get — ominous"). 王弼:
來求說者也 (*lái qiú yuè zhě yě*, "one who comes seeking to please"), and calls it flattery. The open
line at the lower lake's mouth, out of place: gladness sought rather than given.

**Line 4** weighs. 商兌，未寧，介疾有喜 (*shāng duì, wèi níng, jiè jí yǒu xǐ*, "gladness bargained
over, not yet at rest; screened from the sickness, there is joy"). 說文's 商 (*shāng*) is 从外知内
(*cóng wài zhī nèi*, "knowing the inside from outside"); its 介 (*jiè*) is a drawn line, and it adds 人各有介
(*rén gè yǒu jiè*, "every person has a boundary"). Here 說 is talk as weighing, and the line that draws a boundary
against the flatterer below comes through to joy.

**Line 5** trusts the wrong thing. 孚于剝，有厲 (*fú yú bō, yǒu lì*, "confidence in what strips away
— peril"). 剝 (*bō*) is 說文's 裂也 (*liè yě*, "to split, to flay"), and the name of hexagram 23.
王弼 reads the ruler as glad of the open line above rather than the firm one below.

**Line 6** is drawn out. 引兌 (*yǐn duì*, "gladness drawn out"). 說文's 引 (*yǐn*) is 開弓也 (*kāi gōng
yě*, "drawing a bow"): the pull held and not loosed. No fortune is given. The 小象: 未光也 (*wèi
guāng yě*, "not yet shining").

### In a life now

This is the figure for conversation, friendship, charm, persuasion, and every kind of pleasure
that passes between people. It does not warn against delight; it gets through. It asks where the
delight is coming from. Talk that answers (line 1) and gladness between people who rely on each
other (line 2) are good. Charm that comes to get something (line 3) is ominous, whether it is a
seller's warmth, a flatterer at work, or an app built to please. Weighing an offer before being
glad of it (line 4) is uneasy and ends in joy. The sharpest line is 5: the person in the right
place, doing well, who gives their confidence to what is quietly stripping them. And line 6 is
pleasure drawn out and kept at full stretch — the bow held, the evening that will not end.

The 大象 makes it practice: 君子以朋友講習 (*jūnzǐ yǐ péngyǒu jiǎng xí*, "the noble one talks things
over and practises with friends"). 說文's 講 (*jiǎng*) is 和解也 (*hé jiě yě*, "reconciling"): talk
that loosens a knot. Two lakes, two mouths, joined.

### An echo from the Laozi

**The same graph, and opposite counsel.** The Laozi uses 兌 twice (chapters 52 and 56), and
Shalom's English answers to it: *"Block the openings, close the doors."* Chapter 52 goes on:
*"Open the openings, multiply your affairs, and to the end of your days you are beyond saving,"*
and chapter 56 begins *"Those who know do not speak."* In both books 兌 is the opening, and 說卦's
兌為口 (*duì wéi kǒu*, "兌 is the mouth") joins them. **But they part completely.** The Laozi shuts
the opening; 58 says 亨 (*hēng*, "gets through") and its 大象 sends the noble one to talk with
friends. This reading does not soften either: the Laozi's closed mouth and the I Ching's open one
are two counsels, and the difference is what makes each clear.
