## The signature — a reading

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-24. This reads the figure through the Wings — the declared lens
([`README.md`](../README.md#the-lens)) — and it is a reading, not a ruling. The name still comes from
the lines ([`read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums`](../principles/read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums.md)).*

**The signature.** Within: **water** (坎 · yang · sinking in). Without: **sky** (乾 · yang · vigour).
**Across** — water ↔ fire meets sky ↔ earth, yang on both sides, the odd line at the middle within
and none without.

**What the tradition reads.** The 彖 (*tuàn*): 訟，上剛下險，險而健，訟 (*sòng, shàng gāng xià xiǎn,
xiǎn ér jiàn, sòng*), "訟 (*sòng*): firm above, hazard below; hazard, and vigour: 訟." It adds
終凶，訟不可成也 (*zhōng xiōng, sòng bù kě chéng yě*), "the end is ominous: a dispute cannot be
carried through." The 大象 (*dà xiàng*, the great image): 天與水違行，訟；君子以作事謀始 (*tiān yǔ
shuǐ wéi xíng, sòng; jūnzǐ yǐ zuò shì móu shǐ*), "sky and water go opposite ways: 訟; the noble one,
by it, plans at the start of an undertaking." The tradition reads a quarrel that should be settled
early or not pursued.

**What the polarity implies.** This is 5 turned over, and the turn reverses the direction: now the
sinking pole is below and the rising pole above, so they draw apart — the 大象's 違行 (*wéi xíng*,
"go opposite ways") is the structure stated as an image. Within is a hazard with a firm line closed
up at its centre: a grievance lodged in the self, hard and hidden. Without is all firm and rising
away: a counterpart that does not bend and does not come down. No amount of pressure from below
closes a gap that widens by the figure's own motion. So the lines reward those who let go — 初六
(*chū liù*) 不永所事 (*bù yǒng suǒ shì*), "does not prolong the affair"; 九二 (*jiǔ èr*) 不克訟，歸而逋
(*bù kè sòng, guī ér bū*), "cannot win the dispute, goes home and slips away" — and punish the one who
wins: the top line is given a belt and 終朝三褫之 (*zhōng zhāo sān chǐ zhī*), "has it stripped three
times in a morning."

The one line where 訟 is auspicious is 九五 (*jiǔ wǔ*), the centre of the sky: the dispute resolved
from above, by a third party — which is why the judgment says 利見大人 (*lì jiàn dà rén*), "favourable
to see the great person." In 5 the vigour inside waits and is fed; here the hazard inside presses
and is stripped. Same two trigrams; what differs is which one is the self.

**For the name.** **Dispute** — supported and deepened: two forces moving apart explain why the
dispute 不可成 (*bù kě chéng*, "cannot be carried through") and why winning it is empty; *dispute*
keeps the sense that it is argued before someone, not fought out. **Candidates the signature
suggests:** *Grievance* — the hazard within; survives the lines, if stiffly: 不克訟 (*bù kè sòng*)
"cannot carry the grievance", 訟，元吉 (*sòng, yuán jí*) "the grievance: auspicious from the source" —
readable only as the grievance heard. *Going Apart* — from 違行 (*wéi xíng*, "go opposite ways");
fails the lines at 不克訟 (*bù kè sòng*), "cannot win the —", since a going-apart is not won or lost.

## The heart of it

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-25 — an interpretive reading, not a translation. Each claim is marked by
layer: the core text (the judgment and the six lines, Western Zhou), the Wings (Warring States to
Han), 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*, 3rd century), and echoes of the Tao Te Ching in this project's own translation.
The formula words — 吉, 凶, 无咎, 亨, 孚, 貞 — carry working glosses until WORKLIST A4 and A6 rule on
them ([`hexagram-names.md`](../hexagram-names.md)).*

### Essence

**A dispute cannot be carried through to its end: stop it early, or have it heard — and do not
count on keeping what you win.** 說文 (*shuōwén*) defines 訟 (*sòng*) in one word, 爭也 (*zhēng
yě*, "contending"), and builds it from 言 (*yán*, "speech") with 公 (*gōng*) for its sound. The
core text uses it as a thing that can be won or lost — 不克訟 (*bù kè sòng*, "cannot win the
dispute") twice — and its judgment turns on one hinge: 中吉，終凶 (*zhōng jí, zhōng xiōng*, "midway,
auspicious; to the end, ominous"). Every line that lets go does well. The one line that wins is
stripped.

### The polarities in it

Within is water: a firm line shut inside two open ones, a hardness held out of sight — the
grievance one is sure of. Without is sky, firm through and through, and it goes up while water
goes down. The 大象 (*dà xiàng*) makes that the image: 天與水違行 (*tiān yǔ shuǐ wéi xíng*, "sky and
water go opposite ways"). Pressing from below does not close a gap that the figure itself is
widening. This is 5 turned over: there the vigour was within and waited on the hazard, and was
fed; here the hazard is within and pushes at the vigour, and gets nowhere.

### In nature

Water goes down, always, by the shortest way it can find; the weather above it goes where it
goes. Stand by a river on a windy day and watch the surface ruffle upstream while the current
runs down underneath — the two moving past each other, neither yielding. That is the 大象's
picture.

The 彖 (*tuàn*) has a second, sharper one. It explains why the judgment says 不利涉大川 (*bù lì shè
dà chuān*, "not advantageous to cross the great river"): 入于淵也 (*rù yú yuān yě*, "it enters the
deep pool"). 說文 gives 淵 (*yuān*) as 回水也 (*huí shuǐ yě*, "water that turns back on itself") —
an eddy. Look below any rock in a fast stream: a patch of water circling for hours, carrying the
same leaf and the same foam round and round, going nowhere while the river goes past. There is no
better picture of a quarrel pursued to the end.

And 序卦 (*xùguà*) places the hexagram where anyone who has watched animals would: 飲食必有訟
(*yǐn shí bì yǒu sòng*, "where there is eating and drinking there will be dispute"). After 5's
meal, the squabble over it — gulls over a scrap, sparrows at a feeder, siblings at a table.

### The movement of the lines

**The judgment.** 訟：有孚，窒，惕，中吉，終凶。利見大人，不利涉大川 (*sòng: yǒu fú, zhì, tì,
zhōng jí, zhōng xiōng. lì jiàn dà rén, bù lì shè dà chuān*) — "Dispute. There is confidence, and it
is blocked; be wary. Midway, auspicious; to the end, ominous. Advantageous to see the great person;
not advantageous to cross the great river." 窒 (*zhì*) is in 說文 塞也 (*sè yě*, "stopped up") —
a 穴 (*xué*, "hole") with something lodged in it. You may be right, and it will still not go
through.

**Line 1** stops at the start. 不永所事，小有言，終吉 (*bù yǒng suǒ shì, xiǎo yǒu yán, zhōng jí*,
"does not prolong the affair: a little talk; in the end, auspicious"). 永 (*yǒng*) is in 說文 the
long veins of flowing water. Do not let it run.

**Line 2** is firm, at the lower centre — strong enough to fight, and beneath the whole sky. 不克訟，
歸而逋，其邑人三百戶无眚 (*bù kè sòng, guī ér bū, qí yì rén sān bǎi hù wú shěng*, "cannot win the
dispute; goes home and slips away; the three hundred households of the town suffer no blight").
說文 gives 眚 (*shěng*) as a film growing over the eye. The retreat is what spares everyone else.

**Line 3**, open, between firm lines, does not dispute at all. 食舊德，貞厲，終吉。或從王事，无成
(*shí jiù dé, zhēn lì, zhōng jí. huò cóng wáng shì, wú chéng*, "lives on the old integrity; holding
firm is peril; in the end, auspicious. Perhaps it follows the ruler's affairs: no completion").
It lives on what it already has, claims nothing, and comes through.

**Line 4**, at the seam, fails to win as line 2 did — and changes. 不克訟，復即命渝，安貞吉 (*bù kè
sòng, fù jí mìng yú, ān zhēn jí*, "cannot win the dispute; turns back, goes to the allotted course,
and changes; settled, holding firm: auspicious"). 王弼 reads 命 (*mìng*) the other way round —
變前之命 (*biàn qián zhī mìng*, "changing the earlier order") — the claim itself withdrawn. Either
way the line's weight is on 渝 (*yú*, "changes").

**Line 5** is the only place 訟 (*sòng*) is auspicious. 訟，元吉 (*sòng, yuán jí*, "the dispute: from the
source, auspicious"). This is the sky's centre — the dispute brought before someone who can hear
it. 王弼 calls this line 訟之主 (*sòng zhī zhǔ*, "the master of the dispute"), who uses the centre
以㫁枉直 (*yǐ duàn wǎng zhí*, "to decide the crooked and the straight"; 㫁 is an old form of 斷, *duàn*, "decide"). It is why the judgment
says to see the great person.

**Line 6** wins. 或錫之鞶帶，終朝三褫之 (*huò xī zhī pán dài, zhōng zhāo sān chǐ zhī*, "perhaps
granted a great leather belt; before the morning is out, stripped of it three times"). 說文's entry
for 鞶 (*pán*) quotes this very line; 褫 (*chǐ*) is 奪衣 (*duó yī*, "stripping the clothes"). The
prize and the humiliation arrive in the same morning.

Notice the pattern: lines 2 and 4, the two firm lines that fail to win, both end well; line 6, the
one that succeeds, is the one the text shames.

### In a life now

The inheritance quarrel between siblings; the grievance at work that has become a file; the
boundary fence; the argument online that will not end; the lawsuit that costs more than the thing
fought over. The judgment does not say you are wrong — it grants the confidence — only that it is
**blocked**, and that pressing it to the end is ominous. It counsels four things: **stop early**
(line 1); **retreat, if the fight would take others down with you** (line 2); **take it before
someone fair, rather than across the great river** (the judgment and line 5); and **do not start
anything large while it is live**. What is won by quarrel is not kept (line 6).

The 大象 moves the whole matter earlier: 君子以作事謀始 (*jūnzǐ yǐ zuò shì móu shǐ*, "the noble one,
in undertaking affairs, plans at the start"). 王弼 says why: 契之不明，訟之所以生也 (*qì zhī bù
míng, sòng zhī suǒ yǐ shēng yě*, "when the tally is unclear, that is where dispute is born").
Settle the terms before the work, and there is less to fight about after.

### An echo from the Laozi

**Here the Laozi is not an echo the reader brings — 王弼 put it into this hexagram himself.** In his
note on the 大象 he writes 故有德司契而不責於人 (*gù yǒu dé sī qì ér bù zé yú rén*, "so the one with
integrity keeps the tally, and presses no claim on people") — two phrases from the Laozi's chapter
79, joined. In this project's translation: *"Those with integrity keep the tally."* The layer is
王弼, not the core: 契 (*qì*, "tally") is nowhere in the judgment or the lines.

The two books share the conviction that the dispute is best dealt with before it exists. They part
over what happens once it does. Chapter 79 opens *"Settle a great grudge / and some grudge always
remains. / How could that count as masterful?"* — repair is second-best, and the Laozi does not
praise it. 訟's fifth line does: the dispute heard at the centre is 元吉 (*yuán jí*), "from the source,
auspicious." The I Ching keeps a judge; the Laozi would rather no one needed one. **That difference
stays open here.** (A second contact, only thematic: 說文 glosses 訟 (*sòng*) with 爭 (*zhēng*), the Laozi's locked
*contend* — *"Because they do not contend, no one in the world can contend with them,"* ch 22 —
but 爭 is 說文's word and 王弼's, not the core text's.)
