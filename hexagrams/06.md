## The signature — a reading

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-24. This reads the figure through the Wings — the declared lens
([`README.md`](../README.md#the-lens)) — and it is a reading, not a ruling. The name still comes from
the lines ([`read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums`](../principles/read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums.md)).*

**The signature.** Within: **water** (坎 · yang · sinking in). Without: **sky** (乾 · yang · vigour).
**Across** — water ↔ fire meets sky ↔ earth, yang on both sides, the odd line at the middle within
and none without.

**What the tradition reads.** The 彖 (*tuàn*): 訟，上剛下險，險而健，訟 (*sòng, shàng gāng xià xiǎn,
xiǎn ér jiàn, sòng*), "訟 (*sòng*): firm above, hazard below; hazard, and vigour: 訟." It adds
終凶，訟不可成也 (*zhōng xiōng, sòng bù kě chéng yě*), "the end is ominous: a dispute cannot be
carried through." The 大象 (*dà xiàng*, the great image): 天與水違行，訟；君子以作事謀始 (*tiān yǔ
shuǐ wéi xíng, sòng; jūnzǐ yǐ zuò shì móu shǐ*), "sky and water go opposite ways: 訟; the noble one,
by it, plans at the start of an undertaking." The tradition reads a quarrel that should be settled
early or not pursued.

**What the polarity implies.** This is 5 turned over, and the turn reverses the direction: now the
sinking pole is below and the rising pole above, so they draw apart — the 大象's 違行 (*wéi xíng*,
"go opposite ways") is the structure stated as an image. Within is a hazard with a firm line closed
up at its centre: a grievance lodged in the self, hard and hidden. Without is all firm and rising
away: a counterpart that does not bend and does not come down. No amount of pressure from below
closes a gap that widens by the figure's own motion. So the lines reward those who let go — 初六
(*chū liù*) 不永所事 (*bù yǒng suǒ shì*), "does not prolong the affair"; 九二 (*jiǔ èr*) 不克訟，歸而逋
(*bù kè sòng, guī ér bū*), "cannot win the dispute, goes home and slips away" — and punish the one who
wins: the top line is given a belt and 終朝三褫之 (*zhōng zhāo sān chǐ zhī*), "has it stripped three
times in a morning."

The one line where 訟 is auspicious is 九五 (*jiǔ wǔ*), the centre of the sky: the dispute resolved
from above, by a third party — which is why the judgment says 利見大人 (*lì jiàn dà rén*), "favourable
to see the great person." In 5 the vigour inside waits and is fed; here the hazard inside presses
and is stripped. Same two trigrams; what differs is which one is the self.

**For the name.** **Dispute** — supported and deepened: two forces moving apart explain why the
dispute 不可成 (*bù kě chéng*, "cannot be carried through") and why winning it is empty; *dispute*
keeps the sense that it is argued before someone, not fought out. **Candidates the signature
suggests:** *Grievance* — the hazard within; survives the lines, if stiffly: 不克訟 (*bù kè sòng*)
"cannot carry the grievance", 訟，元吉 (*sòng, yuán jí*) "the grievance: auspicious from the source" —
readable only as the grievance heard. *Going Apart* — from 違行 (*wéi xíng*, "go opposite ways");
fails the lines at 不克訟 (*bù kè sòng*), "cannot win the —", since a going-apart is not won or lost.

## The heart of it

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-25 — an interpretive reading, not a translation. Each claim is marked by
layer: the core text (the judgment and the six lines, Western Zhou), the Wings (Warring States to
Han), Wang Bi (the 3rd-century commentator), and echoes of the Tao Te Ching in this project's own
translation. The formula words — 吉 ("auspicious"), 凶 ("ominous"), 无咎 ("no blame"), 亨 ("gets
through"), 孚 ("confidence"), 貞 ("holding firm") — carry working glosses until WORKLIST A4 and A6
rule on them ([`hexagram-names.md`](../hexagram-names.md)).*

### Essence

**A dispute cannot be carried through to its end: stop it early, or have it heard — and do not count
on keeping what you win.** The Shuowen, the c. 100 CE character dictionary, defines 訟 ("Dispute") in
one word, 爭也 ("contending"), and builds it from 言 ("speech") with 公 ("impartial") for its sound. The
core text uses it as a thing that can be won or lost — 不克訟 ("cannot win the dispute") twice — and
its judgment turns on one hinge: 中吉，終凶 ("midway, auspicious; to the end, ominous"). Every line that
lets go does well. The one line that wins is stripped.

### The polarities in it

Within is water: a firm line shut inside two open ones, a hardness held out of sight — the grievance
one is sure of. Without is sky, firm through and through, and it goes up while water goes down. The
Great Image, the Wing on the whole figure, makes that the image: 天與水違行 ("sky and water go opposite
ways"). Pressing from below does not close a gap that the figure itself is widening. This is 5
turned over: there the vigour was within and waited on the hazard, and was fed; here the hazard is
within and pushes at the vigour, and gets nowhere.

### In nature

Water goes down, always, by the shortest way it can find; the weather above it goes where it goes.
Stand by a river on a windy day and watch the surface ruffle upstream while the current runs down
underneath — the two moving past each other, neither yielding. That is the Great Image's picture.

The Tuan, the Wing on the judgment, has a second, sharper one. It explains why the judgment says
不利涉大川 ("not advantageous to cross the great river"): 入于淵也 ("it enters the deep pool"). The Shuowen
gives 淵 ("deep pool") as 回水也 ("water that turns back on itself") — an eddy. Look below any rock in a
fast stream: a patch of water circling for hours, carrying the same leaf and the same foam round and
round, going nowhere while the river goes past. There is no better picture of a quarrel pursued to
the end.

And the Xugua, the Wing on the sequence, places the hexagram where anyone who has watched animals
would: 飲食必有訟 ("where there is eating and drinking there will be dispute"). After 5's meal, the
squabble over it — gulls over a scrap, sparrows at a feeder, siblings at a table.

### The movement of the lines

**The judgment.** 訟：有孚，窒，惕，中吉，終凶。利見大人，不利涉大川 ("Dispute. There is confidence, and it is blocked; be
wary. Midway, auspicious; to the end, ominous. Advantageous to see the great person; not
advantageous to cross the great river"). 窒 ("blocked") is in the Shuowen 塞也 ("stopped up") — a 穴
("hole") with something lodged in it. You may be right, and it will still not go through.

**Line 1** stops at the start. 不永所事，小有言，終吉 ("does not prolong the affair: a little talk; in the end,
auspicious"). 永 ("long-lasting") is in the Shuowen the long veins of flowing water. Do not let it
run.

**Line 2** is firm, at the lower centre — strong enough to fight, and beneath the whole sky.
不克訟，歸而逋，其邑人三百戶无眚 ("cannot win the dispute; goes home and slips away; the three hundred households of
the town suffer no blight"). The Shuowen gives 眚 ("blight") as a film growing over the eye. The
retreat is what spares everyone else.

**Line 3**, open, between firm lines, does not dispute at all. 食舊德，貞厲，終吉。或從王事，无成 ("lives on the old
integrity; holding firm is peril; in the end, auspicious. Perhaps it follows the ruler's affairs: no
completion"). It lives on what it already has, claims nothing, and comes through.

**Line 4**, at the seam, fails to win as line 2 did — and changes. 不克訟，復即命渝，安貞吉 ("cannot win the
dispute; turns back, goes to the allotted course, and changes; settled, holding firm: auspicious").
Wang Bi reads 命 ("the allotted course") the other way round — 變前之命 ("changing the earlier order") —
the claim itself withdrawn. Either way the line's weight is on 渝 ("changes").

**Line 5** is the only place 訟 ("Dispute") is auspicious. 訟，元吉 ("the dispute: from the source,
auspicious"). This is the sky's centre — the dispute brought before someone who can hear it. Wang Bi
calls this line 訟之主 ("the master of the dispute"), who uses the centre 以㫁枉直 ("to decide the crooked
and the straight"; 㫁 ("decide") is an old form of 斷 ("decide")). It is why the judgment says to see
the great person.

**Line 6** wins. 或錫之鞶帶，終朝三褫之 ("perhaps granted a great leather belt; before the morning is out,
stripped of it three times"). The Shuowen's entry for 鞶 ("leather belt") quotes this very line; 褫
("strip") is 奪衣 ("stripping the clothes"). The prize and the humiliation arrive in the same morning.

Notice the pattern: lines 2 and 4, the two firm lines that fail to win, both end well; line 6, the
one that succeeds, is the one the text shames.

### In a life now

The inheritance quarrel between siblings; the grievance at work that has become a file; the boundary
fence; the argument online that will not end; the lawsuit that costs more than the thing fought
over. The judgment does not say you are wrong — it grants the confidence — only that it is
**blocked**, and that pressing it to the end is ominous. It counsels four things: **stop early**
(line 1); **retreat, if the fight would take others down with you** (line 2); **take it before
someone fair, rather than across the great river** (the judgment and line 5); and **do not start
anything large while it is live**. What is won by quarrel is not kept (line 6).

The Great Image moves the whole matter earlier: 君子以作事謀始 ("the noble one, in undertaking affairs,
plans at the start"). Wang Bi says why: 契之不明，訟之所以生也 ("when the tally is unclear, that is where
dispute is born"). Settle the terms before the work, and there is less to fight about after.

### An echo from the Laozi

**Here the Laozi is not an echo the reader brings — Wang Bi put it into this hexagram himself.** In
his note on the Great Image he writes 故有德司契而不責於人 ("so the one with integrity keeps the tally, and
presses no claim on people") — two phrases from the Laozi's chapter 79, joined. In this project's
translation: *"Those with integrity keep the tally."* The layer is Wang Bi, not the core: 契
("tally") is nowhere in the judgment or the lines.

The two books share the conviction that the dispute is best dealt with before it exists. They part
over what happens once it does. Chapter 79 opens *"Settle a great grudge / and some grudge always
remains. / How could that count as masterful?"* — repair is second-best, and the Laozi does not
praise it. The fifth line of 訟 ("Dispute") does: the dispute heard at the centre is 元吉 ("from the
source, auspicious"). The I Ching keeps a judge; the Laozi would rather no one needed one. **That
difference stays open here.** (A second contact, only thematic: the Shuowen glosses 訟 ("dispute")
with 爭 ("contend"), the Laozi's locked *contend* — *"Because they do not contend, no one in the
world can contend with them,"* ch 22 — but 爭 ("contend") is the Shuowen's word and Wang Bi's, not
the core text's.)
