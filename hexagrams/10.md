## The signature — a reading

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-24. This reads the figure through the Wings — the declared lens
([`README.md`](../README.md#the-lens)) — and it is a reading, not a ruling. The name still comes from
the lines ([`read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums`](../principles/read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums.md)).*

**The signature.** Within: **lake** (兌 · yin · releasing). Without: **sky** (乾 · yang · vigour).
**Across** — mountain ↔ lake under sky ↔ earth.

**What the tradition reads.** The 彖 (*tuàn*) reads the name as a relation between two kinds of
line — 柔履剛也 (*róu lǚ gāng yě*, "the yielding treads on the firm") — and says why the treader
survives: 說而應乎乾 (*yuè ér yìng hū qián*, "releasing, it answers 乾, the sky"), and so 履虎尾，
不咥人 (*lǚ hǔ wěi, bù dié rén*, "treads on the tiger's tail; it does not bite"). The 大象 (*dà
xiàng*) takes the figure as the plainest statement of rank, 上天下澤 (*shàng tiān xià zé*, "sky
above, lake below"), and the noble one 以辨上下，定民志 (*yǐ biàn shàng xià, dìng mín zhì*, "tells
above from below, and settles the people's aims").

**What the polarity implies.** 10 is 9 turned over, and the same fact shows from the other side.
Lake is sky with its top line opened, and it meets the sky with exactly that line: the figure's one
yielding line is line 3, the sole of the foot on the tiger. Everything above it is firm, and nothing
in the figure could shift it. What the lake has is an open face turned upward — 說 (*yuè*,
releasing, speech, delight), a mouth under a weight. The treader is almost what it treads on,
differing only at the surface that touches. The structure says the tread is safe while that surface
stays open, and the lines agree at the exact place where it pretends to be closed: line 3, 眇能視，
跛能履 (*miǎo néng shì, bǒ néng lǚ*, "the one-eyed can see, the lame can tread"), then 履虎尾，咥人，凶
(*lǚ hǔ wěi, dié rén, xiōng*, "treads on the tiger's tail; it bites; ominous"), and 武人為于大君
(*wǔ rén wéi yú dà jūn*, "a warrior acting as a great lord"). The bite falls on the lake's own line.
Against 9: there the soft line holds the firm from above; here it walks beneath it. The same single
open line, as a cover and as a footing.

**For the name.** **Treading** — supports: the figure is a soft surface in contact with a hard one
from below, which is what the word says; the signature adds that the tread keeps contact without
contest. **Candidates the signature suggests:** *Footing* — survives: 履道坦坦 (*lǚ dào tǎn tǎn*,
"treading the path, level and smooth", 10.2) and 視履考祥 (*shì lǚ kǎo xiáng*, "look at the
treading, examine the signs", 10.6) both read as footing; *Stepping Softly* — fails the lines: 夬履
(*guài lǚ*, "decisive treading", 10.5) is a hard step, and the line counts it 貞厲 (*zhēn lì*, "firm,
and in peril"), not wrong in kind.

## The heart of it

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-25 — an interpretive reading, not a translation. Each claim is marked by
layer: the core text (the judgment and the six lines, Western Zhou), the Wings (Warring States to
Han), Wang Bi (the 3rd-century commentator), and echoes of the Tao Te Ching in this project's own
translation. The formula words — 吉 ("auspicious"), 凶 ("ominous"), 无咎 ("no blame"), 亨 ("gets
through"), 孚 ("confidence"), 貞 ("holding firm") — carry working glosses until WORKLIST A4 and A6
rule on them ([`hexagram-names.md`](../hexagram-names.md)).*

### Essence

**To tread is to be in touch with what could kill you, and to live or die by the manner of the
step.** The Shuowen, the c. 100 CE character dictionary, gives 履 ("Treading") first as a thing, not
an act — 足所依也 ("what the foot rests on"), with 舟象履形 ("the boat-shape pictures the shoe"). A shoe,
then the step taken in it. The core text makes the ground a tiger: the judgment treads on its tail
and is not bitten, line 3 treads on it and is. Nothing in the figure removes the tiger. **Treading**
*(proposed)* is the whole of what can be chosen: the shoe, the road, the wariness, the look back.

### The polarities in it

Lake within, sky without. Everything above is firm; the one open line is at the top of the lower
trigram, the lake's face, turned up against the sky's underside (see *The signature*, above). The
treader is nearly what it treads on — differing only at the surface that touches. The Tuan, the Wing
on the judgment, says the soft treads on the firm and survives because 說而應乎乾 ("releasing, it answers
the sky"): it meets the weight with an open face rather than a braced one. **Here the text corrects
the structure.** The structure makes that open line the treader, and the core text has exactly that
line bitten. The tread that ends well is line 4's — a firm line, wary.

### In nature

A tiger at rest lies with its tail laid out behind it on the ground, and the tip twitches; that tip
is the one part of a resting tiger you could reach without being in front of it. The Shuowen gives 尾
("tail") as 微也 ("the slight, the tip"). Tigers were at home in the Zhou country, and like every big
cat they hunt from behind and from cover, so to come on one from behind is to come at it the way it
comes at others. The Shuowen, curiously, knows 咥 ("bite") only as 大笑也 ("to laugh aloud") — the mouth
opened wide. The core text's sense is the other opening of the mouth: the bite.

The Great Image, the Wing on the whole figure, sets the figure as 上天下澤 ("sky above, lake below").
Stand at a lake on a still morning. It is the lowest thing in the land, and its surface holds the
whole sky; at the far shore, for a moment, the eye cannot tell which is sky and which is water. The
Great Image's noble one does the opposite — 辨上下 ("tells above from below"). Treading begins with
knowing which way is down.

### The movement of the lines

**The judgment.** 履虎尾，不咥人，亨 ("Treading on the tiger's tail; it does not bite the person. It gets
through.") The hazard is stated before anything else, and survived before anything is explained.

**Line 1** 素履，往无咎 ("plain shoes; going on — no blame"). 素 ("plain") is the Shuowen's 白緻繒也 ("fine
white silk"), undyed. The first step is taken in undyed shoes: nothing added, nothing to catch the
eye.

**Line 2** 履道坦坦，幽人貞吉 ("treading the road, level, level; for the one who keeps out of sight, holding
firm is auspicious"). 幽 ("out of sight") is 隱也 ("hidden"). In the lower centre the road is smooth,
and the one who walks it unnoticed does well.

**Line 3** 眇能視，跛能履，履虎尾，咥人，凶。武人為于大君 ("one-eyed, and can see; lame, and can tread; treads on the
tiger's tail; it bites the person — ominous. A warrior acting as a great lord"). The Shuowen makes 眇
("one-eyed") 一目小也 ("one eye small") and 跛 ("lame") 行不正也 ("walking not straight"). The line is the
step taken on a capacity one does not have. The bite falls here, on the seam.

**Line 4** 履虎尾，愬愬終吉 ("treading on the tiger's tail — wary, wary; in the end auspicious"). The same
tail, the same step, and the opposite outcome. What differs is 愬愬 ("wary, wary"), the doubled
wariness. This is the judgment's tread, found in a line.

**Line 5** 夬履，貞厲 ("decisive treading; holding firm is peril"). 夬 ("decisive") is the Shuowen's 分決也
("to split, to cut through"). In the upper centre, the step that cuts a way through is right in kind
and still dangerous; Wang Bi says 履道惡盈而五處實是以危 ("the way of treading dislikes fullness, and 5 sits in
solidity — so, peril").

**Line 6** 視履考祥，其旋元吉 ("look at the treading, examine the signs; its turning round — from the source,
auspicious"). At the end the treader turns and looks back along the prints. 旋 ("turning round") has
疋 ("a foot") in its graph: the walk comes round.

### In a life now

This is the figure for working close to power you cannot match: a junior before a volatile chief, a
nurse on a ward with a difficult consultant, a small country beside a large one, anyone who must
raise the hard subject with a person who can hurt them. It is also the figure for anyone walking
where the ground itself is dangerous — the climber, the carer of a violent patient. The text does
not say keep away from the tiger. It says: go plainly (line 1); keep to the level road and out of
sight (line 2); do not step on the strength you wish you had (line 3); be wary every step (line 4);
know that even a right and decisive step is perilous (line 5); and afterwards look back at the
footprints and read them (line 6).

The Xugua, the Wing on the sequence, reads 履 ("Treading") as conduct — 物畜然後有禮 ("once things are
kept, there is propriety") — and the Great Image turns it to order: 君子以辨上下，定民志 ("the noble one tells
above from below, and settles the people's aims"). To tread well is to know where one stands.

### An echo from the Laozi

**The two books share a tiger, and disagree about it.** The Laozi's chapter 50: *"one who truly
tends life / can travel the land without meeting rhinos or tigers ... the tiger's claws have nowhere
to tear into ... Because they give death no foothold."* The graph is 虎 ("tiger"), in the core text
of 10 and in the Laozi alike, and both books ask what makes a person safe near it.

But the Laozi's walker does not meet the tiger at all; 10's steps on it. The Laozi gives death no
foothold; the I Ching is all foothold — it makes safety a matter of how the foot comes down, and
line 3 is bitten. **This reading keeps the tiger's teeth**, and does not soften them into the
Laozi's untouchable sage.
