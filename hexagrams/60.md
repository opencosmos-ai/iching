## The signature — a reading

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-24. This reads the figure through the Wings — the declared lens
([`README.md`](../README.md#the-lens)) — and it is a reading, not a ruling. The name still comes from
the lines ([`read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums`](../principles/read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums.md)).*

**The signature.** Within: **Lake** (兌 · yin · releasing). Without: **Water** (坎 · yang · sinking in).
**Across** — mountain ↔ lake and water ↔ fire, yin within and yang without.

**What the tradition reads.** The 大象 (*dà xiàng*): 澤上有水，節；君子以制數度，議德行 (*zé shàng yǒu shuǐ, jié; jūnzǐ yǐ zhì shù dù, yì dé xíng*), "water above the lake: 節; the noble one sets numbers and measures, and weighs integrity and conduct." The 彖 (*tuàn*): 說以行險 (*yuè yǐ xíng xiǎn*), "delighting, it goes through hazard", and 天地節而四時成 (*tiān dì jié ér sì shí chéng*), "sky and earth keep their joints and the four seasons come about." The tradition reads 節 as the joint of the 說文 (*shuōwén*) bamboo: division that lets a thing run long.

**What the polarity implies.** A lake is a hollow open at the top; water sinks. Put water over a lake and it pours in, and the lake's opening is what receives it. So the figure makes restraint a matter of capacity, not of force. A lake does not stop water the way a mountain would — that is 止 (*zhǐ*, "stopping"), and there is no mountain here. It holds water by being the shape it is, a rim of some height: too little and it is dry, too much and it floods. The lines are doors. 不出戶庭 (*bù chū hù tíng*), "not going out past the inner door", is 无咎 (*wú jiù*), "no fault", at the first line; 不出門庭 (*bù chū mén tíng*), "not going out past the gate", is 凶 (*xiōng*), "ominous", at the second — the same staying in, right at one moment and wrong at the next. The 小象 (*xiǎo xiàng*) says the first 知通塞 (*zhī tōng sè*), "knows getting through from being blocked." A lake with no outlet goes bitter: 苦節 (*kǔ jié*). The structure suggests that measure is what lets an open thing live under a sinking one: the self open, the world pouring in, the rim deciding how much. 雜卦 (*záguà*) adds a surprise, 節止也 (*jié zhǐ yě*), "節 is stopping" — the mountain's word for a figure with no mountain. The rim of a hollow does the work of a height.

**For the name.** *Restraint* — the signature strains it: restraint is force held back, and this figure's measure is a shape, not a grip; the lines' tastes — 苦 (*kǔ*, "bitter"), 甘 (*gān*, "sweet"), 安 (*ān*, "settled") — fit a measure better than a check. **Candidates the signature suggests:** *Measure* — survives: 甘節 "sweet measure", 不節若，則嗟若 (*bù jié ruò, zé jiē ruò*) "without measure, then lamenting", and 64's 小象 亦不知節也 (*yì bù zhī jié yě*) "nor does he know measure"; *Capacity* — fails the lines: 苦節 "bitter capacity" and 安節 "settled capacity" lose the act of keeping to it.

## The heart of it

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-25 — an interpretive reading, not a translation. Each claim is marked by
layer: the core text (the judgment and the six lines, Western Zhou), the Wings (Warring States to
Han), 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*, 3rd century), and echoes of the Tao Te Ching in this project's own translation.
The formula words — 吉, 凶, 无咎, 亨, 孚, 貞 — carry working glosses until WORKLIST A4 and A6 rule on
them ([`hexagram-names.md`](../hexagram-names.md)).*

### Essence

**A joint in the bamboo: the closing that lets a thing keep growing — and the text judges every
measure by its taste.** 說文 (*shuōwén*) gives 節 (*jié*) as 竹約也 (*zhú yuē yě*, "the bamboo's
binding"): the node where the hollow stem is closed across and the next section begins. The
judgment grants it 亨 (*hēng*, "gets through") and in the same breath sets the limit: 苦節不可貞
(*kǔ jié bù kě zhēn*, "bitter measure cannot be held firm"). The lines then taste it — settled,
sweet, bitter — and time it, door by door. This is not restraint as a grip. It is the right amount,
at the right moment, for as long as those who live inside it can bear it.

### The polarities in it

Lake within, water without. The lake is open at its top; the water above sinks in. So the figure is
an opening under an inflow, and the question it poses is not how to stop the water — there is no
mountain here to stop anything — but how much the opening can take. The 大象 (*dà xiàng*) image is
澤上有水 (*zé shàng yǒu shuǐ*, "water above the lake"); a lake's measure is its rim. The 彖 (*tuàn*)
finds the order in the lines: 剛柔分，而剛得中 (*gāng róu fēn, ér gāng dé zhōng*, "firm and yielding
are divided, and the firm holds the centres"). **But here the text overrules the structure.** Line 2
is firm and central, and it is ominous. The centre does not save a measure kept past its time.

### In nature

Cut a length of bamboo and look at it. Between two nodes it is a hollow tube; at each node a solid
wall closes it across, and there the leaves and branches start. The walls are what keep a tall,
hollow stem from buckling in wind. And watch a bamboo shoot come up in spring: it rises with all its
nodes already in it, packed close, and grows by lengthening every section at once. The joints are not
added afterwards to hold the growth in; they are there from the start, and the growth happens
between them.

The 彖 carries the image to the year: 天地節而四時成 (*tiān dì jié ér sì shí chéng*, "sky and earth
keep their joints and the four seasons are made"). The year has nodes — the longest day, the
shortest, the two even ones — and each season grows between them.

And taste the water. A lake with an outlet stays sweet, because what comes in also goes out. A lake
with no outlet keeps everything the rivers bring, and as the water leaves only by the air, its salts
build up until it turns bitter; there are lakes in the world named for it. 說文's 甘 (*gān*, "sweet")
is 从口含一 (*cóng kǒu hán yī*, "a mouth holding one thing"); its 苦 (*kǔ*, "bitter") is a bitter herb.

### The movement of the lines

**The judgment.** 節：亨。苦節不可貞 (*jié: hēng. kǔ jié bù kě zhēn*) — "Restraint *(proposed)* gets
through. Bitter measure cannot be held firm" (or, in the older sense of 貞, "cannot be the
inquiry"). Measure is good; the one kind the judgment names is the kind that cannot last.

**Line 1** stays in, rightly. 不出戶庭，无咎 (*bù chū hù tíng, wú jiù*, "not going out past the inner
door — no blame"). 說文: 半門曰戶 (*bàn mén yuē hù*, "half a gate is called a 戶"), the single
leaf. The 小象 (*xiǎo xiàng*): 知通塞也 (*zhī tōng sè yě*, "it knows what gets through and what is
blocked").

**Line 2** stays in, wrongly. 不出門庭，凶 (*bù chū mén tíng, xiōng*, "not going out past the gate —
ominous"). The 門 (*mén*) is the double gate to the world. The same staying-in, one threshold on, is
ominous; the 小象: 失時極也 (*shī shí jí yě*, "it misses the height of the time"). 王弼: the rule made
at the first line should be proclaimed at the second, and is hidden instead.

**Line 3** has no measure. 不節若，則嗟若，无咎 (*bù jié ruò, zé jiē ruò, wú jiù*, "without measure,
then lamenting — no blame"). The 小象: 又誰咎也 (*yòu shuí jiù yě*, "and whom is there to blame?"). The
lament is the lesson, and it is one's own.

**Line 4** is measure at rest. 安節，亨 (*ān jié, hēng*, "settled measure — gets through"). 說文's 安
(*ān*) is 靜也 (*jìng yě*, "stillness"). Measure no longer felt as effort.

**Line 5** is the height of it. 甘節，吉；往有尚 (*gān jié, jí; wǎng yǒu shàng*, "sweet measure —
auspicious; going on is honoured"). 王弼: 爲節而不苦，非甘而何 (*wéi jié ér bù kǔ, fēi gān ér hé*,
"measure that is not bitter — what is that but sweet?").

**Line 6** is measure taken to the extreme. 苦節，貞凶，悔亡 (*kǔ jié, zhēn xiōng, huǐ wáng*, "bitter
measure: holding firm, ominous; regret goes"). The two verdicts seem to clash. 王弼 separates them:
imposed on others, it is more than they can bear, and ominous; kept in one's own conduct, regret
goes.

### In a life now

This is the figure for budgets and rations, working hours, a diet, a household's rules, a day of
rest, the limits a parent sets, the discipline of any craft. It counsels measure and then tests it
two ways. **By time:** keeping a thing to yourself at first is right (line 1); keeping it in when
the moment has come to go out is ominous (line 2) — the plan never announced, the grief that has
closed the gate. **By taste:** a measure people can settle into (line 4), even savour (line 5),
gets through; a bitter one, imposed on others, fails however right it is (line 6). An austerity
that tastes of punishment will not hold.

The 大象: 君子以制數度，議德行 (*jūnzǐ yǐ zhì shù dù, yì dé xíng*, "the noble one sets numbers and
measures, and weighs integrity and conduct"). Set the measure plainly; then keep asking what it does
to the people inside it.

### An echo from the Laozi

**The same words, and a different clock.** The Laozi's chapter 47 opens: *"Without leaving home,
you know the whole world."* The Chinese is 不出戶 (*bù chū hù*, "not going out the door"), the words
of 60's first line, 不出戶庭; Shalom's *leaving home* answers to them at a small remove. Both books
find that staying in can be right, and knowing. **They part on time.** For the Laozi, not going out is
the sage's standing way. For 60 it is right at the inner door and ominous one line later at the gate.
The I Ching does not let a good measure become a rule for all seasons.
