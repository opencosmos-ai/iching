## The signature — a reading

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-24. This reads the figure through the Wings — the declared lens
([`README.md`](../README.md#the-lens)) — and it is a reading, not a ruling. The name still comes from
the lines ([`read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums`](../principles/read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums.md)).*

**The signature.** Within: **mountain** (艮 · yang · stopping). Without: **wind** (巽 · yin · entering).
**Across** — mountain ↔ lake and thunder ↔ wind; yang within, yin without.

**What the tradition reads.** The 大象傳 sees 山上有木 (*shān shàng yǒu mù*, "a tree on the mountain"):
巽 (*xùn*) read as wood, rooted on what does not move. The 彖傳 reads it as advance that keeps its footing: 進得位，往有功也
(*jìn dé wèi, wǎng yǒu gōng yě*, "advancing, it gains its place; going on, it achieves"). And it gives the
two actions outright: 止而巽，動不窮也 (*zhǐ ér xùn, dòng bù qióng yě*, "stopping, and entering: its
movement does not run out"). The judgment ties it to the proper order of a marriage, 女歸吉 (*nǚ guī jí*,
"the woman goes to her husband's house: good fortune").

**What the polarity implies.** Mountain within is closed at its top, the third line. Wind without is open
at its base, the fourth. The two odd lines meet at the centre of the figure: the lid of the hold lies
directly under the open floor of the entering. That is a tree's root on a rock, going in only where the
rock gives. Movement that starts from a hold does not run out, because it never overreaches its ground.
The lines are one wild goose landing by stages — 干 (*gān*, "shore"), 磐 (*pán*, "rock"), 陸 (*lù*, "dry land"), 木 (*mù*, "tree"), 陵 (*líng*, "hill") — and
the seam between the trigrams is where it goes wrong. At the third line, the top of the hold, the goose reaches dry
land and 夫征不復 (*fū zhēng bù fù*, "the husband marches off and does not come back"). The structure
suggests the reason. That is the one line that tries to go straight on from the mountain's closed top, instead
of stepping across into the open floor above. At the fourth line the goose, now in the tree, 或得其桷 (*huò dé qí jué*,
"may find a flat branch"). It does not land well; it lands enough. Turned over, the figure is 54: releasing
first, then moving. Here the order is right, stopping before entering, and the order is the whole of it.

**For the name.** **By Degrees** — the signature supports it: stopping under entering is advance measured by
the ground, and the name keeps the manner rather than the motion. **Candidates the signature suggests:**
*Gradual Advance* — survives: 鴻漸于陸 (*hóng jiàn yú lù*, "the wild goose advances gradually to the dry
land"); *Rooting* — fails the lines: the tree is the 大象傳's image, but 漸 (*jiàn*) in every line is the goose's
progress, not growth.

## The heart of it

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-25 — an interpretive reading, not a translation. Each claim is marked by
layer: the core text (the judgment and the six lines, Western Zhou), the Wings (Warring States to
Han), 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*, 3rd century), and echoes of the Tao Te Ching in this project's own translation.
The formula words — 吉, 凶, 无咎, 亨, 孚, 貞 — carry working glosses until WORKLIST A4 and A6 rule on
them ([`hexagram-names.md`](../hexagram-names.md)).*

### Essence

**Advance by stages, each one landed before the next is tried.** Every line of the core text opens
the same way, 鴻漸于 (*hóng jiàn yú*, "the wild goose advances by degrees to…"), and then names a
place, climbing from the water's edge to the high ground. The judgment sets a marriage beside the
bird: 女歸吉 (*nǚ guī jí*, "the woman's going to her new home — auspicious"), and 說文 (*shuōwén*)
gives 歸 (*guī*, "going home") as 女嫁也 (*nǚ jià yě*, "a woman marrying"). The name's graph does not
help: 說文 knows 漸 (*jiàn*, "by degrees") only as the name of a river, water (水) with 斬 (*zhǎn*)
for the sound. The sense is the lines', and one Wing, 坤's 文言 (*wényán*), uses the word exactly:
of frost before hard ice, 其所由來者漸矣 (*qí suǒ yóu lái zhě jiàn yǐ*, "where it came from, it came
by degrees"). **By Degrees** *(proposed)* is not about slowness. It is about order: the right place
reached at the right step.

### The polarities in it

Mountain within, wind without. The mountain stops at its closed top; the wind goes in through its
open base; and the two odd lines meet at the middle of the figure, so that what stops lies directly
under what enters (see *The signature*, above). The 彖 (*tuàn*) says it in six characters:
止而巽，動不窮也 (*zhǐ ér xùn, dòng bù qióng yě*, "stopping, and entering: its movement does not run
out"). Every advance here starts from ground already held. The text bears the structure out at the
seam, and not simply. Line 3, the mountain's closed top, is the one place the goose goes too far —
and the same line finds its one advantage in defending, not in marching. What stops has its own
work; only what stops can go on.

### In nature

The bird is 鴻 (*hóng*), a large wild goose — 說文 has 鴻鵠也 (*hóng hú yě*, "the great goose"), and
王弼 says 鴻水鳥也 (*hóng shuǐ niǎo yě*, "the goose is a water-bird"). Watch geese on migration,
in spring and again in autumn. They do not fly the whole distance at once. They come down at the
same shores, marshes and stubble fields year after year, feed for days, and go on. You hear a skein
before you see it, high and calling, each bird riding the air of the one ahead, and the line holds
its order. The six places of the lines are a naturalist's list, in the order a water-bird would
feel them: 干 (*gān*, "the shore"), 磐 (*pán*, "the rock" — 王弼: 磐山石之安者也, *pán shān shí zhī
ān zhě yě*, "a mountain stone that is steady"), 陸 (*lù*, "the high plain" — 說文 高平地, *gāo píng
dì*, "high, level ground"), 木 (*mù*, "the wood"), 陵 (*líng*, "the hill" — 說文 大𨸏也, *dà fù yě*,
"a great mound"). Each step away from water is a step into exposure.

Look hardest at the fourth. Geese are not perching birds: webbed feet cannot close round a branch.
So the line says 或得其桷 (*huò dé qí jué*, "it may find a flat branch"), and 說文 gives 桷 (*jué*)
as 椽方曰桷 (*chuán fāng yuē jué*, "a squared rafter"). A goose in a tree needs a branch as flat as
a beam. The text knew its bird.

The 大象 (*dà xiàng*) sees 山上有木 (*shān shàng yǒu mù*, "wood on the mountain"); 說卦 (*shuōguà*)
ch 11 gives the wind's trigram as 巽為木 (*xùn wéi mù*, "wind is wood") and also, tellingly, as
為進退 (*wéi jìn tuì*, "advancing and retreating"). Trees on a mountain grow slowly — thin soil,
wind, a short season — and their rings are tight, so the wood is dense and strong. At a tree line
you can see a pine no taller than a person that is two hundred years old.

### The movement of the lines

**The judgment.** 漸：女歸吉，利貞 (*jiàn: nǚ guī jí, lì zhēn*) — "By degrees: the woman's going to
her new home — auspicious; holding firm is advantageous." A marriage made in its proper steps is the
human form of the goose's stages. The 雜卦 (*záguà*) reads it so: 漸女歸待男行也 (*jiàn nǚ guī dài
nán xíng yě*, "by degrees: the woman's going waits for the man to act") — each step in its turn.

**Line 1** is the young bird at the water's edge. 鴻漸于干，小子厲，有言，无咎 (*hóng jiàn yú gān,
xiǎozǐ lì, yǒu yán, wú jiù*, "to the shore; the young one in peril, there is talk — no blame"). The
first stage is exposed and criticised, and it does no harm.

**Line 2** is the lower centre, and it rests. 鴻漸于磐，飲食衎衎，吉 (*hóng jiàn yú pán, yǐn shí kàn
kàn, jí*, "to the rock; eating and drinking, contented — auspicious"). 說文 gives 衎 (*kàn*) as
行喜皃 (*xíng xǐ mào*, "the look of going gladly"). The 小象 (*xiǎo xiàng*) adds 不素飽也 (*bù sù
bǎo yě*, "not fed for nothing"): the rest is earned.

**Line 3** is the seam, and the step too far. 鴻漸于陸，夫征不復，婦孕不育，凶；利禦寇 (*hóng jiàn yú
lù, fū zhēng bù fù, fù yùn bù yù, xiōng; lì yù kòu*, "to the high plain; the husband marches off and
does not come back, the wife conceives and does not bring the child up — ominous; advantageous to
ward off raiders"). From the mountain's top straight out onto open ground: the household breaks.
The same line, turned to holding, succeeds.

**Line 4** is out of its element and manages. 鴻漸于木，或得其桷，无咎 (*hóng jiàn yú mù, huò dé qí
jué, wú jiù*, "to the wood; it may find a flat branch — no blame"). Not a good landing; enough of one.

**Line 5** is the upper centre, and it waits. 鴻漸于陵，婦三歲不孕，終莫之勝，吉 (*hóng jiàn yú líng,
fù sān suì bù yùn, zhōng mò zhī shèng, jí*, "to the hill; the wife does not conceive for three
years; in the end nothing prevails against it — auspicious"). A long delay at the height, and then
what was due arrives.

**Line 6** returns to the same ground as line 3. 鴻漸于陸，其羽可用為儀，吉 (*hóng jiàn yú lù, qí yǔ
kě yòng wéi yí, jí*, "to the high plain; its feathers can be used in the rite — auspicious"). Many
later readers emend the graph; the received text repeats 陸 (*lù*, "the high plain"), and it is read
here as given. The same place is ominous when reached by a push from the middle and auspicious at the
end of the whole course. 說文 gives 儀 (*yí*, "the rite") as 度也 (*dù yě*, "a measure"): the bird that
arrived in order becomes the pattern others keep. The 小象: 不可亂也 (*bù kě luàn yě*, "it cannot be
thrown into disorder").

### In a life now

This is the figure for anything that can only be done in stages: an apprenticeship, a long
courtship, a garden, recovery after an injury, a child learning to read, a practice of any kind, a
reputation. It counsels a stance more than a pace. Land each stage before trying the next. When
there is food on the rock, eat (line 2). Do not go straight out onto the open plain from the top of
what you hold, leaving the household behind (line 3). When you find yourself in a tree, find the
flat branch and do not ask for more (line 4). When three years pass and nothing comes, the text
does not say you have failed (line 5). And the end, reached in order, is not private: the feathers
go into the rite.

The 大象 turns it to the long run: 君子以居賢德，善俗 (*jūnzǐ yǐ jū xián dé, shàn sú*, "the noble one
dwells in worthy integrity, and betters the common ways"). Custom changes the way a mountain tree
grows — slowly, and then it holds.

### An echo from the Laozi

**The two books share a graph and part over what it is for.** The Laozi's chapter 64: *"The tree the
width of both arms is born from a hair's tip. / The nine-tiered terrace rises from a heap of dirt."*
The word is 木 (*mù*, "tree", "wood"), present in 53's core (line 4) and its 大象, and in both books
the large thing is made of small steps. But chapter 64 is about acting before a thing has shape —
*"Handle it before it exists"* — and warns that *"those who handle it ruin it."* 漸 is not about the
small beginning, and it does not counsel leaving things alone. It counsels the order of steps once
under way, inside a social order — a marriage, a rite, a custom. If there is a bridge it is the
chapter's *"Be as careful at the end as at the beginning"*, which is what line 6's feathers are,
and that bridge is thematic only.

### Words kept

- 漸 (*jiàn*) — *by degrees*; **By Degrees** the proposed name (not yet approved); never *growth*: the lines are a bird's advance, not a plant's
- 木 (*mù*) — *wood* in the 大象 and at line 4, one word for both
- 歸 (*guī*) — *going to her new home*, a woman's marrying (說文 女嫁也); not *returning*
- 鴻 (*hóng*) — *the wild goose*
- 德 (*dé*) — *integrity*, never *virtue* (locked)
- 善 (*shàn*) — here a verb, *betters*; the lock's *masterful* names skill, and this use is for Shalom to rule on
- 亂 (*luàn*) — *disorder* (locked)
- 貞 · 吉 · 凶 · 无咎 · 厲 (*zhēn · jí · xiōng · wú jiù · lì*) — *holding firm · auspicious · ominous · no blame · peril*, working glosses (A4, A6)
