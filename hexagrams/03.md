## The signature — a reading

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-24. This reads the figure through the Wings — the declared lens
([`README.md`](../README.md#the-lens)) — and it is a reading, not a ruling. The name still comes from
the lines ([`read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums`](../principles/read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums.md)).*

**The signature.** Within: **thunder** (震 · yang · moving out). Without: **water** (坎 · yang ·
sinking in). **Across** — thunder ↔ wind meets water ↔ fire, yang on both sides, the odd line at the
base within and at the middle without.

**What the tradition reads.** The 彖 (*tuàn*): 剛柔始交而難生，動乎險中 (*gāng róu shǐ jiāo ér nán
shēng, dòng hū xiǎn zhōng*), "firm and yielding first meet and difficulty is born; it moves in the
middle of the hazard"; and 雷雨之動滿盈 (*léi yǔ zhī dòng mǎn yíng*), "the moving of thunder and rain
fills everything to the brim." The 大象 (*dà xiàng*, the great image) reads the upper trigram as
cloud, rain not yet fallen: 雲雷，屯；君子以經綸 (*yún léi, zhūn; jūnzǐ yǐ jīng lún*), "cloud and
thunder: 屯 (*zhūn*); the noble one, by it, lays the warp and twists the thread." The tradition reads
the first, crowded difficulty of things coming to be, and the work of ordering it.

**What the polarity implies.** Two yang trigrams, so two rulers — and they sit at different heights.
Within, the firm line is at the very bottom, pushing up and out; without, the firm line is held in
the middle of the water, sunk and closed round. Thunder rises, water descends: they move *into* each
other, which is why the 彖 finds everything full. But full is not open. The figure has exactly two
firm lines, and the lines say what each is doing: 初九 (*chū jiǔ*, the firm first line) 磐桓 (*pán
huán*), "circling round a great stone" — the push at the base cannot go yet; 九五 (*jiǔ wǔ*, the
firm fifth) 屯其膏 (*zhūn qí gāo*), "hoarding its fat" — the core above keeps its richness to itself.
The four open lines between are caught in the traffic, and three of them repeat 乘馬班如 (*chéng mǎ
bān rú*), "riding horses, milling about."

The structure suggests the difficulty here is not weakness but two initiatives not yet in touch:
a shoot with all its force at the tip, under a ground that has a hard, wet core. 4 turns this over —
the thunder at the base becomes a mountain at the top, *moving out* turned into *stopping*, and the
force that was pushing up is now sunk and capped.

**For the name.** **Sprouting** — the signature deepens it: a firm line at the base moving out,
beneath a firm core sinking in, is the sprout under the crust that 說文 (*shuōwén*) pictures, and it
says the struggle is the sprout's own force, not a lack. **Candidates the signature suggests:**
*Crowding* — from 滿盈 (*mǎn yíng*, "filled to the brim") and two firm lines with the open ones
jammed between; survives the lines, including 乘馬班如 ("riding horses, milling about") and 屯其膏
(*zhūn qí gāo*), which reads naturally as crowding in, hoarding. *Breaking Through* — from thunder's
moving out; fails the lines: no line breaks through, and the top one ends 泣血漣如 (*qì xuè lián
rú*), "weeping blood in streams."

## The heart of it

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-25 — an interpretive reading, not a translation. Each claim is marked by
layer: the core text (the judgment and the six lines, Western Zhou), the Wings (Warring States to
Han), Wang Bi (the 3rd-century commentator), and echoes of the Tao Te Ching in this project's own
translation. The formula words — 吉 ("auspicious"), 凶 ("ominous"), 无咎 ("no blame"), 亨 ("gets
through"), 孚 ("confidence"), 貞 ("holding firm") — carry working glosses until WORKLIST A4 and A6
rule on them ([`hexagram-names.md`](../hexagram-names.md)).*

### Essence

**Life at the moment it breaks ground, when everything is pushing and nothing is yet in its place.**
The Shuowen, the c. 100 CE character dictionary, draws 屯 ("Sprouting") exactly: 象艸木之初生 ("it pictures
plants first coming up"), 从屮貫一 ("a sprout piercing a line"), the line being the ground — and it adds
屯然而難 ("sprouting-like, and hard"). The difficulty is not something outside the thing. It is what
being born is. The lines are a scene of that — horsemen milling, a match delayed ten years, a deer
lost in the forest, fat hoarded, silent tears — and the counsel runs against the push: stay where
you are, set someone in place, do not chase.

### The polarities in it

Thunder within, water without — two trigrams each ruled by a firm line, at different heights.
Within, the firm line is at the base, pushing up and out; without, it sits in the middle of the
water, sunk and held round. The rising and the sinking move into each other, and the Tuan, the Wing
on the judgment, calls that fullness: 雷雨之動滿盈 ("the stirring of thunder and rain fills everything to
the brim"). The Xugua, the Wing on the sequence, says the same in two clauses: 屯者盈也，屯者物之始生也
("Sprouting is filling; Sprouting is things first being born"). But full is not open. **The force is
real, and it has not yet found its way through** — two firm lines not yet in touch, and four soft
ones caught between them.

### In nature

Watch a row of beans in the week after sowing. The seed sends its root down first — the Shuowen's 尾曲
("the tail curls") — and then the shoot comes up bent double, a hook with the tender tip tucked
behind it, shouldering through the soil. The arched necks break the surface before any leaf does.
Now add the Tuan's weather. A heavy thunder-rain on bare, fine soil beats it smooth, and it dries to
a crust; seedlings that were almost up stall beneath it, and a gardener goes along the row breaking
it with a finger. The rain that made the sprouting possible is what made the lid. Thunder below,
water above: the push and the lid are the same weather.

The Great Image, the Wing on the whole figure, catches the moment just before: 雲雷，屯 ("cloud and
thunder: Sprouting") — the storm with the rain not yet fallen. Towering cloud, thunder inside it,
air heavy and close, and nothing released. And line 3 has a creature, observed truly: a deer that is
chased does not run into the open. It goes for cover, into the thickest wood it can find, and
whoever follows without someone who knows the ground ends in the middle of the trees with nothing.

### The movement of the lines

**The judgment.** 屯：元亨，利貞。勿用有攸往，利建侯 ("Sprouting *(proposed)*: from the source, it gets through;
advantageous: holding firm. Do not set out anywhere; advantageous: to set up a lord"). The beginning
gets through — and still the instruction is not to go. What helps is a centre, someone set in place.
Wang Bi: 得主則定 ("once there is a master, things settle").

**Line 1** is the push itself, the firm line at the base, and it is told to stay. 磐桓，利居貞，利建侯 ("a
great stone, and circling; advantageous to stay and hold firm; advantageous to set up a lord"). The
Shuowen gives 桓 ("circling") as 亭郵表也 ("the post that marks a relay station") — a thing that stands
where it is. The Zagua, the Wing that pairs the hexagrams, sums the whole figure in this line:
屯見而不失其居 ("Sprouting shows itself and does not leave its place"). The Small Image, the Wing on each
line: 以貴下賤，大得民也 ("the honoured placed below the lowly greatly wins the people").

**Line 2** is the lower centre, and the waiting is long. 屯如邅如，乘馬班如，匪寇婚媾，女子貞不字，十年乃字 ("stuck, and
turning back; riding horses, milling; not raiders — a marriage party. The girl holds firm and does
not bear; ten years, and then she bears"). The riders who look like raiders are suitors; the answer
is still *not yet*. The Shuowen gives 字 ("bear") as 乳也 ("to bear and suckle"), a child under a roof.

**Line 3** is at the seam, soft in a firm seat, and the push becomes a chase. 即鹿无虞，惟入于林中，君子幾不如舍，往吝
("going after deer with no warden, only into the middle of the forest; the noble one, at the first
sign, does better to let it go; going on — stinting"). The Shuowen gives 幾 ("the first sign") as
微也。殆也 ("slight; on the verge"): the moment to stop is the faint one, before the trees close.

**Line 4** is the first line told to go. 乘馬班如，求婚媾，往，吉无不利 ("horses milling; seek the marriage; going
— auspicious, nothing not advantageous"). It answers the firm first line below. The difference from
line 3 is the object: a partner sought, not a quarry chased.

**Line 5** is the ruling centre, and here **the structure and the text disagree**. 屯其膏；小貞吉，大貞凶
("hoarding its fat: holding firm in small things, auspicious; in great ones, ominous"). The graph is
read in its other sense, "to hoard". A firm line in the ruler's seat should be the figure's
strength; the text gives it a hoard. The older sense of 貞 ("the inquiry") reads cleanly here — small
questions come out well, large ones badly. The Small Image: 施未光也 ("its giving has not yet spread").
The text wins: in a beginning, the centre that keeps its richness to itself is the blockage.

**Line 6** is the top of the water, and nothing answers it. 乘馬班如，泣血漣如 ("horses milling; weeping
blood, in ripples"). The Shuowen: 無聲出涕曰泣 ("tears without sound are called weeping"). No verdict,
only silent weeping; the Small Image asks 何可長也 ("how can this last?"). It cannot. Beginnings end.

### In a life now

This is the figure for every first stretch: the first year of a marriage, the first months with a
newborn, a business in its first winter, the first season on new land, a household that has just
crossed a border, a body relearning to walk after surgery. Everything is pushing and nothing is in
place, and the text does not promise speed. **It says stay put and set a post** — someone
responsible, a fixed point others can find. It says the answer may be *not yet* for a long time. It
says do not chase the deer into the forest without someone who knows the ground, and stop at the
first sign. It says seek the partner, not the quarry. And to whoever holds the centre: do not sit on
the richness. In a beginning, the hoard is what keeps the ground shut.

The Great Image turns it to the work of the hands: 君子以經綸 ("the noble one, by it, sets the warp and
twists the thread"). Before there is cloth there is a loom strung straight. The tangle of a
beginning is ordered one long thread at a time.

### An echo from the Laozi

**The two books share a graph here and disagree about it, plainly.** Chapter 63: *"Map out the
difficult while it is easy"* … *"The world's difficult affairs always begin easy."* The graph is 難
("difficult"). It is not in the core text of 屯 ("Sprouting"); it is in the Tuan, 剛柔始交而難生 ("firm and
soft first meet, and difficulty is born"), and in the Shuowen's 屯然而難 ("sprouting-like, and hard").
So the echo is at the Wings' layer, not the core's.

The Laozi says beginnings are easy, and that trouble should be met there, while it is small. The
Wings say the beginning is where difficulty is born. Both can be true, because they stand in
different places. The Laozi speaks to the one who acts early upon a thing still slight; 屯
("Sprouting") speaks from inside the thing being born, for whom the first push through the ground is
the hardest work it will ever do. **The echo teaches by its gap, and this reading does not close
it.**
