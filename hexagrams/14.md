## The signature — a reading

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-24. This reads the figure through the Wings — the declared lens
([`README.md`](../README.md#the-lens)) — and it is a reading, not a ruling. The name still comes from
the lines ([`read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums`](../principles/read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums.md)).*

**The signature.** Within: **sky** (乾 · yang · vigour). Without: **fire** (離 · yin · attaching).
**Across** — sky ↔ earth under water ↔ fire.

**What the tradition reads.** The 彖 (*tuàn*) finds the name in the fifth line:
柔得尊位，大中而上下應之 (*róu dé zūn wèi, dà zhōng ér shàng xià yìng zhī*, "the yielding gets the
honoured place, great at the centre, and above and below answer it"). Its integrity is
剛健而文明 (*gāng jiàn ér wén míng*, "firm and vigorous, and patterned with clarity"), and it
應乎天而時行 (*yìng hū tiān ér shí xíng*, "answers the sky and moves with the time"). The 大象 (*dà
xiàng*) sees 火在天上 (*huǒ zài tiān shàng*, "fire above the sky"), and the noble one
以遏惡揚善，順天休命 (*yǐ è è yáng shàn, shùn tiān xiū mìng*, "checks the bad and lifts the good,
going along with the sky's fine allotted course").

**What the polarity implies.** 13 turned over. There fire rose into the sky; here it stands on
top of it — the light of noon over everything beneath. The one yielding line has moved from the
heart of the self (13's line 2) to the ruler's place (line 5), and the five firm lines all answer
to it. What the figure has, it has by an open hand: fire owns nothing and lives on what it clings
to, and here it clings to the whole sky. The structure suggests that great having is the owner's
hollowness — the holder at the top is the one line that grips nothing. The lines agree: 匪其彭
(*fěi qí péng*, "not its swelling", 14.4), 厥孚交如，威如 (*jué fú jiāo rú, wēi rú*, "its 孚 — *fú*,
not yet ruled — goes both ways, and commands respect", 14.5), and 公用亨于天子 (*gōng yòng xiǎng yú
tiānzǐ*, "the lord makes an offering to the son of the sky", 14.3): the having is passed upward, and
the top line receives 自天佑之 (*zì tiān yòu zhī*, "from the sky, help for it"). Set against 9, the
other sky with a yielding trigram above: there a single open line at 4 stores and does not rain;
here it sits at 5 and shows everything.

**For the name.** **Great Possession** — strains: the figure has what it has by being open where
the owner sits, and *possession* leans toward a grip. **Candidates the signature suggests:** *Great
Having* — survives: 大車以載 (*dà chē yǐ zài*, "a great cart, loaded", 14.2) is having, not owning;
*Great Harvest* — survives: the loaded cart and the offering in 14.3 are a harvest year's, and the
sun above the sky is its season; *Great Light* — fails the lines: no line lights anything, and the
light is the image of the 大象 (*dà xiàng*, "the great image") alone. The name waits on what 有 (*yǒu*, "have, there is") is here.

## The heart of it

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-25 — an interpretive reading, not a translation. Each claim is marked by
layer: the core text (the judgment and the six lines, Western Zhou), the Wings (Warring States to
Han), 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*, 3rd century), and echoes of the Tao Te Ching in this project's own translation.
The formula words — 吉, 凶, 无咎, 亨, 孚, 貞 — carry working glosses until WORKLIST A4 and A6 rule on
them ([`hexagram-names.md`](../hexagram-names.md)).*

### Essence

**Great having is a full year kept moving — loaded, carried, offered up and never drummed — and
the one who holds it at the top holds with an open hand.** The judgment adds two characters to the
name and no warning: 大有：元亨 (*dà yǒu: yuán hēng*, "great having: from the source, gets through").
有 (*yǒu*) is the plain word, "to have, there is" — in this hexagram's own second line it is simply
有攸往 (*yǒu yōu wǎng*, "has somewhere to go"). The lines say what is had and what is done with it: a
great cart, loaded (line 2); an offering carried up to the son of the sky (3); help from the sky at
the end (6). What makes the having great is not the size of the heap but that it moves. The Wings
say who comes to it: 與人同者，物必歸焉 (*yǔ rén tóng zhě, wù bì guī yān*, "whoever is together with
people — things come home to them", 序卦), and 大有眾也 (*dà yǒu zhòng yě*, "great having is the
many", 雜卦).

說文 (*shuōwén*) casts a shadow on the word worth knowing. It defines 有 as 不宜有也 (*bù yí yǒu yě*,
"having what ought not to be had"), and its example is an eclipse: 日月有食之 (*rì yuè yǒu shí zhī*,
"the sun and moon had something eating them"). The core text never uses 有 that way, and this reading
does not lean on it. But the first line says something close: plenty begins by remembering hard
ground.

### The polarities in it

Sky within, fire without — 13 turned over. The firm, vigorous trigram is below, carrying; fire is
on top, and its one open line sits at line 5, the ruler's place. Five firm lines and one yielding
one, and the yielding one is where the power is. Fire has nothing of its own; it lives on what it
clings to, and here what it clings to is all the sky's vigour beneath it. **The strength is in the
carrying below; the having is at the top, and it belongs to the one line that grips nothing.** The
彖 says it in its own terms: 柔得尊位，大中而上下應之 (*róu dé zūn wèi, dà zhōng ér shàng xià yìng
zhī*, "the yielding gets the honoured place, great at the centre, and above and below answer it").

### In nature

火在天上 (*huǒ zài tiān shàng*, "fire above the sky", 大象). Go out at midday in high summer: the
sun at its height, shadows at their shortest, everything in sight lit from above and nothing in
shade. 說卦 ch 5 gives fire's trigram the south and this very quality — 萬物皆相見 (*wàn wù jiē xiāng
jiàn*, "the countless things all see one another"). A great having in that light is one that can be
seen, which is why its lines are about what is shown and what is not.

Then look at the cart. 大車以載 (*dà chē yǐ zài*, "a great cart, loaded", 14.2), and the 小象's
comment is a carter's knowledge: 積中不敗也 (*jī zhōng bù bài yě*, "loaded at the middle, it does not
break down"). Weight piled over the axle rides; weight piled at the tail lifts the shafts off the
ox; weight to one side goes over at the first rut. 王弼: 任重而不危 (*rèn zhòng ér bù wēi*, "it bears
heavy, and is not in peril").

And listen to line 4's 彭 (*péng*), which 說文 gives as 鼓聲也 (*gǔ shēng yě*, "the sound of a drum").
A drum is loud because it is a skin stretched over hollow, and struck.

### The movement of the lines

**The judgment.** 大有：元亨 — "great having: from the source, gets through." No condition, no 貞
(*zhēn*, "holding firm"), nothing to beware of. 王弼: 不大通何由得大有乎 (*bù dà tōng hé yóu dé dà yǒu
hū*, "without getting through greatly, how could there be great having?").

**Line 1.** 无交害，匪咎，艱則无咎 (*wú jiāo hài, fěi jiù, jiān zé wú jiù*, "no dealings with harm;
not blame; when it goes hard, no blame"). 說文's 艱 (*jiān*) is 土難治也 (*tǔ nán zhì yě*, "earth that
is hard to work"). The first line of plenty remembers stony ground.

**Line 2.** 大車以載，有攸往，无咎 (*dà chē yǐ zài, yǒu yōu wǎng, wú jiù*, "a great cart, loaded;
somewhere to go: no blame"). The firm centre of the lower figure: capacity, and a destination.

**Line 3.** 公用亨于天子，小人弗克 (*gōng yòng xiǎng yú tiānzǐ, xiǎo rén fú kè*, "the lord makes an
offering to the son of the sky; small people cannot"). The top of the carrying trigram passes the
having upward. Here 亨 is read as 享 (*xiǎng*, "offer, feast"), not the working *gets through* — but
王弼 keeps *gets through*: 通乎天子 (*tōng hū tiānzǐ*, "gets through to the son of the sky"). The 小象
names the other side: 小人害也 (*xiǎo rén hài yě*, "for small people, it is harm"). Plenty that can
only be kept turns into damage.

**Line 4.** 匪其彭，无咎 (*fěi qí péng, wú jiù*, "not its drumming: no blame"). At the seam, pressed
up under the ruler, the having does not sound itself. The 小象: 明辨晰也 (*míng biàn xī yě*,
"clarity that tells things apart, plainly"). 王弼 reads 彭 as 旁 (*páng*, "the side") — not siding
with the strong line below. Both readings keep the line quiet.

**Line 5.** 厥孚交如，威如；吉 (*jué fú jiāo rú, wēi rú; jí*, "its confidence goes back and forth;
it commands respect: auspicious"). 說文's 交 (*jiāo*) is 交脛也 (*jiāo jìng yě*, "shins crossed") —
a going both ways. The yielding ruler gives confidence and gets it back, and the respect needs no
guard: 威如之吉，易而无備也 (*wēi rú zhī jí, yì ér wú bèi yě*, "the good fortune of commanding
respect: it is easy, and nothing is guarded against", 小象). 王弼 glosses the line in the Laozi's
own words — 不言而教行 (*bù yán ér jiào xíng*, "without words, the teaching goes") — 王弼's
cross-reading at work, and not the core's.

**Line 6.** 自天佑之，吉无不利 (*zì tiān yòu zhī, jí wú bù lì*, "from the sky, help for it:
auspicious, nothing not advantageous"). The 繫辭上 explains it: 祐者，助也。天之所助者，順也。人之所助者，信也
(*yòu zhě, zhù yě. tiān zhī suǒ zhù zhě, shùn yě. rén zhī suǒ zhù zhě, xìn yě*, "help is aid. The
one the sky aids goes along; the one people aid is trusted"). The having ends as something
received, not something kept. No line in the hexagram is ominous.

### In a life now

A good year: a windfall, a book that sells, a business flush with money, a harvest that fills every
barn, a person at the height of their powers, a house full of children and guests. The hexagram does
not warn against any of it — the judgment is plain good fortune. What it shows is how plenty is
carried. Remember the hard ground (line 1). Load it centred and let it go somewhere (2). Pass it
upward and outward (3); for those who can only keep, plenty becomes harm. Do not beat the drum of it
(4). Let confidence run both ways, and respect will need no guards (5). What comes back at the end is
help (6).

The 大象 turns this toward practice: 君子以遏惡揚善，順天休命 (*jūnzǐ yǐ è è yáng shàn, shùn tiān
xiū mìng*, "the noble one checks the bad and lifts the good, going along with the sky's fine
allotted course"). In the full light of noon everything shows; the noble one's work is to choose
which of it to raise.

### An echo from the Laozi

**One graph shared, and the books pull hard against each other on it.** 有 (*yǒu*) is in the core,
in the name and in line 2. Shalom's translation renders it as a verb in chapters 2, 10 and 51 —
*"Giving birth without possessing"* (ch 51). The Laozi's sage does not 有; the I Ching calls a great
有 元亨 (*yuán hēng*, "from the source, gets through") and does not apologise. **That difference stays
open.** The cart is really loaded, and the book is glad of it; it is not an ascetic text, and
softening it toward the Laozi would lose that. Where the two touch is in how the having is held:
14.4 does not drum it, 14.5's holder is the one line that grips nothing, and 14.6 receives it as
help. And the shared English bears on the name: if this hexagram were *Great Possession*, the two
books in one project would use one English word to say opposite things.

### Words kept

- 大有 (*dà yǒu*) — name pending; 有 (*yǒu*) is read here as *having*, the ordinary "have, there is", not the Tao Te Ching's *presence*, and never *Being*; *Great Possession* (proposed, pending) strains, and *possessing* is already Shalom's English for the 有 the Laozi's sage refuses
- 大 (*dà*) — *great* (locked)
- 天 (*tiān*) — *sky*, never *Heaven* (locked); 天子 (*tiānzǐ*) — *the son of the sky*
- 命 (*mìng*) — *the allotted course*, never *mandate* (locked)
- 善 (*shàn*) — *good*, because the 大象 names the category against 惡 (*è*, "bad"); *masterful* where it is skill (locked)
- 信 (*xìn*) — *trust* (locked), in 繫辭上's 人之所助者，信也; so 孚 (*fú*) stays *confidence* (A6)
- 公 (*gōng*) — *lord*, the office (locked)
- 亨 (*hēng*) — *gets through* (A6), but read as 享 (*xiǎng*, "offer") at 14.3; 元 (*yuán*) — *from the source*; 无咎 (*wú jiù*) — *no blame* (A4)
