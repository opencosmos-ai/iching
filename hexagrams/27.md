## The signature — a reading

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-24. This reads the figure through the Wings — the declared lens
([`README.md`](../README.md#the-lens)) — and it is a reading, not a ruling. The name still comes from
the lines ([`read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums`](../principles/read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums.md)).*

**The signature.** Within: **thunder** (震 · yang · moving out). Without: **mountain** (艮 · yang ·
stopping). **Across** — thunder ↔ wind under mountain ↔ lake, both poles yang.

**What the tradition reads.** The 彖 (*tuàn*) says 養正則吉也 (*yǎng zhèng zé jí yě*, "nourishing what
is right is auspicious") and 觀頤，觀其所養也 (*guān yí, guān qí suǒ yǎng yě*, "look at the jaws: look
at what they nourish"). The 大象 (*dà xiàng*) sees 山下有雷 (*shān xià yǒu léi*, "below the mountain,
thunder"), and the noble one 慎言語，節飲食 (*shèn yán yǔ, jié yǐn shí*, "is careful in speech, and
measured in eating and drinking"). The tradition reads the jaws as nourishment rightly taken, and 雜卦
(*záguà*) agrees: 頤養正也 (*yí yǎng zhèng yě*, "頤 is nourishing rightly").

**What the polarity implies.** The figure draws itself. Its two odd lines sit as far apart as six
places allow — thunder's firm line at the very bottom, mountain's at the very top — with four open
lines between: a jaw, open. Thunder is the lower jaw, which moves; mountain is the upper, which holds
still; chewing is exactly that, motion worked against a stop. The same stop governs what goes in and
what comes out, which is why the 大象 puts speech and food in one sentence.

And the lines divide on the trigram line, cleanly. Every line within, on the moving side, is ominous:
觀我朵頤，凶 (*guān wǒ duǒ yí, xiōng*, "watching my working jaws — ominous", 27.1), 征凶 (*zhēng
xiōng*, "setting out — ominous", 27.2), 貞凶 (*zhēn xiōng*, "holding firm — ominous", 27.3). Every line
without, on the stopping side, is auspicious — even the tiger, 虎視眈眈，其欲逐逐 (*hǔ shì dān dān, qí
yù zhú zhú*, "the tiger stares and stares, its wanting chases on", 27.4) — and the top line, the
upper jaw itself, is 由頤 (*yóu yí*, "where the nourishing comes from"). The structure suggests that
here appetite alone is the danger and the stop is what feeds: the moving jaw is nourished only by the
one that holds.

**For the name.** **Jaws** — deepens: the signature makes the figure a jaw in fact, the lower moving
and the upper stopping, so the name, the figure and the lines say one thing. **Candidates the
signature suggests:** *Nourishing* — fails the lines: 觀我朵頤 (*guān wǒ duǒ yí*, "watching my
working jaws", 27.1) needs the body part, not the act; *The Mouth* — fails the lines: it collides with
自求口實 (*zì qiú kǒu shí*, "seek for yourself what fills the mouth") in the hexagram's own judgment.
The signature finds nothing better than the name it already has.

## The heart of it

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-25 — an interpretive reading, not a translation. Each claim is marked by
layer: the core text (the judgment and the six lines, Western Zhou), the Wings (Warring States to
Han), Wang Bi (the 3rd-century commentator), and echoes of the Tao Te Ching in this project's own
translation. The formula words — 吉 ("auspicious"), 凶 ("ominous"), 无咎 ("no blame"), 亨 ("gets
through"), 孚 ("confidence"), 貞 ("holding firm") — carry working glosses until WORKLIST A4 and A6
rule on them ([`hexagram-names.md`](../hexagram-names.md)).*

### Essence

**Look at the jaws: what fills the mouth has to be sought for oneself, and the jaw that moves is fed
only by the one that holds still.** 頤 ("jaws") is on the unmatched list here for the Shuowen, the c.
100 CE character dictionary, so this reads the graph as it stands: 頁 ("the head") beside an older
drawing of the jaw. The judgment's instruction is to look — 觀頤 ("look at the jaws") — and the whole
hexagram is about where a person's eye goes while they eat: to their own food, or to someone else's
mouth.

### The polarities in it

Thunder within, mountain without, and the figure is a picture of itself: a firm line at the bottom,
a firm line at the top, four open lines between — a jaw, open (see *The signature*, above). Thunder
is the lower jaw, which moves; the mountain is the upper, which stops. The core text divides on
exactly that seam. Every line of the moving trigram is ominous; every line of the still one is
auspicious. **Appetite alone goes wrong. What feeds is motion worked against a stop.**

### In nature

Put a finger on your own jaw and chew. Only the lower jaw moves; the upper is part of the skull and
does not. Every mammal eats this way. Watch a cow at the cud: it has no upper front teeth at all,
only a hard pad, and the lower jaw grinds sideways against it for hours. The Great Image, the Wing
on the whole figure, sees 山下有雷 ("below the mountain, thunder") — the rumble under the still mass. It
is anatomy before it is weather.

Line 1's creature is the tortoise, 龜 ("tortoise"). A tortoise eats slowly and can go months without
food through a cold season; it was kept in Zhou for divining, its shell read under heat. 靈
("numinous") is in the Shuowen as the word for one who 以玉事神 ("serves the numinous with jade"). Line
4's creature is the tiger, and the Shuowen describes its look exactly: 眈 ("stare") is 視近而志遠
("looking at what is near, with the aim far off"). A tiger stalks close under cover, holds still,
stares, and most of its hunts still fail; it waits and tries again. The line's 逐 ("chase") is in the
Shuowen 追也 ("to pursue"), drawn as feet after a young pig.

### The movement of the lines

**The judgment.** 頤：貞吉。觀頤，自求口實 ("Jaws. Holding firm: auspicious. Look at the jaws; seek for yourself
what fills the mouth."). 口實 ("what fills the mouth") is food. The counsel is to look, and then to
seek one's own.

**Line 1** is the lower jaw itself. 舍爾靈龜，觀我朵頤，凶 ("you set aside your numinous tortoise and watch my
working jaws — ominous"). Wang Bi: 朶頥者嚼也 ("working jaws means chewing"). His printing writes the
variant graphs 朶頥 ("working jaws") for 朵頤 ("working jaws"). The one who had the creature that needs
almost nothing now watches another eat.

**Line 2**, open, in the lower centre. 顛頤，拂經，于丘頤，征凶 ("the jaws upended, going against the warp; to
the hill for feeding — setting out is ominous"). 經 ("the warp") is the warp thread, the standing
line; Wang Bi reads 顛 ("upended") as feeding from below, and 丘 ("hill") — in the Shuowen 土之高也，非人所爲也
("earth's height, not made by people") — as the high place one goes up to for food.

**Line 3**, open, at the seam. 拂頤，貞凶，十年勿用，无攸利 ("going against the jaws: holding firm is ominous; ten
years, do not act; nothing is advantageous"). The worst line: feeding itself turned wrong, and held
to.

**Line 4**, open, the base of the mountain. 顛頤吉，虎視眈眈，其欲逐逐，无咎 ("the jaws upended: auspicious. The
tiger looks, stares and stares; its wanting chases on and on — no blame"). The same upending that
was ominous at line 2 is auspicious here, because now the high feeds the low. And the tiger's
wanting is not condemned: it is steady, and it waits.

**Line 5**, open, the upper centre. 拂經，居貞吉，不可涉大川 ("going against the warp: staying put and holding
firm is auspicious; the great river cannot be crossed"). Line 2 and line 5 share 拂經 ("going against
the warp"). Line 2 sets out and it is ominous; line 5 stays and it is auspicious. The deviation is
the same; the movement decides.

**Line 6** is the upper jaw. 由頤，厲吉，利涉大川 ("where the feeding comes from: peril, and auspicious; it is
advantageous to cross the great river"). The still jaw is the source, and to be the source is peril.

### In a life now

This is the figure for everything a person takes in — food first, then money, praise, news, other
people's attention — and for everything they give out, since the Great Image puts both in one mouth.
Line 1 is the scroll through other lives while one's own table goes unset; line 2 is looking to the
wrong quarter for support; line 3 is the habit that feeds nothing, kept up. Line 4 is a parent or a
teacher or an employer feeding those below with the tiger's patience, and line 6 is the burden of
being the one others eat from. The core text counsels looking before taking, seeking one's own, and
staying put when one's feeding is out of line.

The Great Image makes it daily practice: 君子以慎言語，節飲食 ("the noble one is careful in speech, and
measured in eating and drinking"). One jaw governs both.

### An echo from the Laozi

**One graph shared, and a disagreement about who fills the mouth.** The Laozi's chapter 3 has the
sage governing by *"emptying their hearts and filling their bellies."* *Filling* is 實 ("filling") —
the same graph as the judgment's 口實 ("what fills the mouth"), in the core text, and Shalom's English
answers to it. Chapter 12's *"the sage acts for the belly, not for the eye"* reads close to line 1's
eye on another's jaws, but that is thematic only — 目 ("eye") is not in this hexagram.

They part on the subject. In chapter 3 the sage fills other people's bellies. The core text of 頤
("jaws") says 自求 ("seek for yourself"). It is the Tuan, the Wing on the judgment, a Wing, that comes
round to the Laozi's side — 聖人養賢，以及萬民 ("the sage nourishes the worthy, and so reaches the countless
people"). **The core asks each mouth to seek its own; this reading keeps that, and marks where the
Wing moves toward the Laozi.**
