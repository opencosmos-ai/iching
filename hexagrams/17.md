## The signature — a reading

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-24. This reads the figure through the Wings — the declared lens
([`README.md`](../README.md#the-lens)) — and it is a reading, not a ruling. The name still comes from
the lines ([`read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums`](../principles/read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums.md)).*

**The signature.** Within: **thunder** (震 · yang · moving out). Without: **lake** (兌 · yin · releasing).
**Across** — thunder ↔ wind × mountain ↔ lake.

**What the tradition reads.** The 彖傳 builds following out of a descent: 剛來而下柔，動而說，隨
(*gāng lái ér xià róu, dòng ér yuè, suí*), "the firm comes and puts itself below the yielding; moving
out, and releasing: following." It widens the word to time — 天下隨時 (*tiānxià suí shí*), "all under
the sky follows the time." The 大象傳 then sends the figure to bed: 澤中有雷 (*zé zhōng yǒu léi*), "in
the middle of the lake there is thunder," and 君子以嚮晦入宴息 (*jūnzǐ yǐ xiàng huì rù yàn xī*), "the
noble one, as it turns toward dark, goes in to rest." The tradition says that whoever is followed
has first gone underneath, and that following the time includes knowing when to stop.

**What the polarity implies.** The figure is `100110`: its firm line is at the very bottom and its
open line at the very top, which gives force at the root and an opening at the lid. Nothing in it
resists motion. What moves out from within meets, without, a mouth rather than a wall. 說卦 ch 9
supplies the body: 震為足 (*zhèn wéi zú*, "thunder is the foot") and 兌為口 (*duì wéi kǒu*, "lake is
the mouth"). A foot below and a mouth above make a body walking toward what calls it, which is
following at its plainest. The yang pole is inside and the yin pole outside, so the strength does
not show; what shows is the opening. The structure suggests that the one followed is never the
loudest one. It is the one whose force has gone under (剛來而下柔). And the thunder of the image
lies in the lake, spent. The figure holds both pursuit and rest: 隨有獲 (*suí yǒu huò*, "following,
there is a catch") and going in at dusk. What the lines add, and the signature explains, is
choosing. 系小子，失丈夫 (*xì xiǎozǐ, shī zhàngfū*), "tied to the little boy, you lose the grown
man": a foot can walk toward only one voice.

**For the name.** **Following**. The signature supports it and makes it bodily, a foot answering a
mouth, and it keeps the leg sense the word needs at 31.3 and 52.2 (執其隨, *zhí qí suí*, "grasp what
follows it"). **Candidates the signature suggests:** *Keeping Pace* survives the lines (隨有獲,
"keeping pace, there is a catch"; 隨，有求得, *suí, yǒu qiú dé*, "keeping pace, what is sought is
got"), but it cannot be the leg at 31 and 52. *Answering* fails the lines: "answering, there is a
catch" loses the going-after, and 拘系之，乃從維之 (*jū xì zhī, nǎi cóng wéi zhī*, "seize and tie it,
then go after it and bind it") is pursuit, not reply.

## The heart of it

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-25 — an interpretive reading, not a translation. Each claim is marked by
layer: the core text (the judgment and the six lines, Western Zhou), the Wings (Warring States to
Han), Wang Bi (3rd century), and echoes of the Tao Te Ching in this project's own translation. The
formula words — 吉 ("auspicious"), 凶 ("ominous"), 无咎 ("no blame"), 亨 ("gets through"), 孚
("confidence"), 貞 ("holding firm") — carry working glosses until WORKLIST A4 and A6 rule on them
([`hexagram-names.md`](../hexagram-names.md)).*

### Essence

**To follow is to walk behind something you have chosen — and the choosing is most of it.** The
Shuowen, the c. 100 CE character dictionary, gives 隨 ("Following") as 从也 ("going after"), and sets
it beside 辵 ("walking"), the walking foot; the graph 从 ("going after") is two people, one behind the
other. The core text is not about obedience. Its lines ask what you walk behind and what that costs:
系小子，失丈夫 ("tied to the little boy, you lose the grown man"), and at the next line the same sentence
reversed. One pair of feet can go after one voice. Following **(proposed)** here is a choice with a
loss inside it, and the book watches the loss as closely as the gain.

### The polarities in it

Within is thunder, whose one firm line is at the very base: force at the root, moving out. Without
is the lake, whose one open line is at the very top: a mouth, not a wall. So what starts from below
meets, above, something open enough to go toward, and nothing in the figure resists the going. The
strength stays inside and does not show; what shows is the opening. The Tuan, the Wing on the
judgment, puts it as a descent — 剛來而下柔 ("the firm comes and puts itself below the yielding") — so
the one who is followed has first gone underneath. **But the figure has nothing to choose between,
and the lines are made of choosing.** The structure explains why following happens; only the text
says that it costs. Where they differ, the text leads.

### In nature

The image in the Great Image, the Wing on the whole figure, is 澤中有雷 ("in the middle of the lake
there is thunder"). Watch a summer storm cross open water in the late afternoon, when such storms
most often break: thunder carries a long way over a lake, rolls off the far shore, and then the
surface closes over it. By dusk the water is flat again. The Great Image draws exactly that
conclusion — 君子以嚮晦入宴息 ("the noble one, as it turns toward dark, goes in to rest").

Dusk is when following is easiest to see. On a winter evening, rooks and jackdaws stream across the
fields to a shared roost in long loose lines, each bird going after the ones ahead, none of them
leading for long; at the roost the noise rises and then stops. The fourth line has the other kind of
following, the hunter's: 獲 ("catch") is in the Shuowen 獵所獲也 ("what is taken in the hunt"). A track
in mud or new snow is freshest at first light, and following it means keeping your eyes on the
ground in front of you.

The sixth line's cord is a real object. 維 ("cord") is in the Shuowen 車蓋維也 ("the cord that ties down
a carriage canopy") — the lashing that keeps a cover from lifting in the wind.

### The movement of the lines

**The judgment.** 隨：元亨。利貞。无咎 ("Following: from the source, it gets through. Holding firm is
advantageous. No blame.") The same four words as the first hexagram, with a *no blame* added:
following is not a lesser state than leading.

**Line 1** leaves home. 官有渝，貞吉。出門交有功 ("the post changes; holding firm, auspicious. Going out of the
gate, dealings bring achievement"). The Shuowen has 渝 ("change") as 變汙也 ("a change, a sullying").
Following begins when the place you were given shifts and you go out of the door to meet others.

**Line 2** chooses the near. 系小子，失丈夫 ("tied to the little boy, you lose the grown man"). 丈 ("ten
feet") is the Shuowen's 十尺也 ("ten feet") — the grown man is the one at full height. The line has no
verdict; the loss is the verdict.

**Line 3**, at the seam, chooses the other way. 系丈夫，失小子。隨，有求得，利居貞 ("tied to the grown man, you lose
the little boy. Following, what is sought is got; it is advantageous to stay put and hold firm").
The Small Image, the Wing on each line, says 志舍下也 ("the will lets go of what is below"). It gets
what it seeks, and is told to stop there.

**Line 4** follows for the catch. 隨有獲，貞凶。有孚在道，以明，何咎 ("following, there is a catch; holding firm,
ominous. There is confidence on the road; with clarity, what blame?"). To hold on to what following
has caught is ominous; Wang Bi sees someone in a minister's place drawing people to themselves. What
rescues it is confidence out on the road (道 ("road"), in the core text a road) and seeing clearly (明
("see clearly")).

**Line 5**, firm and central, settles. 孚于嘉，吉 ("confidence in what is fine: auspicious"). The Shuowen
gives 嘉 ("fine") as 美也 ("fine"), built on 壴 ("drum"), a drum on its stand. The following has found
something worth its confidence.

**Line 6** binds. 拘系之，乃從維之。王用亨于西山 ("seized and tied, then followed and lashed fast; the ruler makes
offering at the western mountain"). 拘 ("seize") is the Shuowen's 止也 ("to stop"). At the top,
following has become being held. Most readers take this 亨 ("gets through") as an offering; Wang Bi
reads 通 ("get through") — 王用通于西山 ("the ruler gets through to the western mountain"), reaching the
one who would not follow.

The auspicious words fall at 1 and 5, both of them firm lines, both of them about holding firm or
confidence. The ominous word falls at 4, where holding firm means holding on to the catch.

### In a life now

This is the figure for choosing a teacher, a trade, a partner, a cause, a leader — and for noticing
that each choice closes a door. Lines 2 and 3 are the plainest counsel in it: what is near and easy
and what is grown and demanding cannot both be walked behind. Line 4 is the warning for anyone who
follows a movement or a boss for what it brings in: the catch is real, and holding on to it is where
the trouble starts. Line 6 is loyalty that has become a tether, the follower now tied fast.

What the text counsels is stance, not morals: go out of the gate when the post changes; choose; take
what is sought and then stay put; put confidence in what is fine. And the Great Image adds the
rhythm — 嚮晦入宴息 ("toward dark, go in to rest"). Following the time includes following it indoors.

### An echo from the Laozi

**The graph is shared; the weight falls differently.** Chapter 29: *"So among things, some go ahead,
some follow."* The word is 隨 ("follow"), as in this hexagram's name and in lines 3 and 4 of the core
text, and *follow* answers to it in both books. Both treat following as a real place to stand, not a
failure to lead.

But in the Laozi going ahead and following take turns among things, and the chapter's lesson is not
to seize the turn: *"Those who grasp it lose it."* The following of 隨 ("Following") is chosen, costs
something, and at line 6 ends in seizing and lashing (拘系 ("seized and tied")). The Laozi warns
against the place this hexagram's lines finish. That is a thematic contrast, not a shared line, and
it is left open here.
