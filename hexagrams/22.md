## The signature — a reading

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-24. This reads the figure through the Wings — the declared lens
([`README.md`](../README.md#the-lens)) — and it is a reading, not a ruling. The name still comes from
the lines ([`read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums`](../principles/read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums.md)).*

**The signature.** Within: **fire** (離 · yin · attaching). Without: **mountain** (艮 · yang · stopping).
**Across** — water ↔ fire × mountain ↔ lake.

**What the tradition reads.** The 彖傳: 柔來而文剛 (*róu lái ér wén gāng*), "the yielding comes and
patterns the firm," and 文明以止，人文也 (*wén míng yǐ zhǐ, rén wén yě*), "pattern and clarity,
held: that is the pattern of people." The 大象傳: 山下有火 (*shān xià yǒu huǒ*), "below the mountain
there is fire," and 君子以明庶政，无敢折獄 (*jūnzǐ yǐ míng shù zhèng, wú gǎn zhé yù*), "the noble one
makes the many matters of government clear, and does not venture to decide lawsuits." The tradition
reads adornment as pattern laid over what is firm: culture, with a limit set on what it may do.

**What the polarity implies.** Fire at the foot of a mountain lights the slope from below. It shows
the mountain and does not move it. Fire's hollow is at its middle, and the mountain's firm line is
at its top: an inner opening that attaches, under an outer closure that holds. Adornment is exactly
this, a surface catching light against something that stays. The 大象's limit follows from the
structure. Light is enough for ordinary business but not for lawsuits, because adornment can make
things clear but cannot decide them; deciding needs the thunder that 21 has and 22 lacks. 22 is 21
turned over. The jaw is upside down, the bite's thunder has gone, and the fire has sunk under a
mountain. Where 21 shackles the foot and cuts at the ear, 22 adorns the toes and the beard (賁其趾,
*bì qí zhǐ*; 賁其須, *bì qí xū*). The mountain, stopping at the top, finishes the figure's own
argument. The top line is 白賁 (*bái bì*, "white adornment"), and the 雜卦 has 賁无色也 (*bì wú sè
yě*, "賁 is without colour"). The structure suggests adornment held to its height ends uncoloured:
the fire goes out on the summit, and the pattern turns plain. 序卦 sends it straight to 23: once
ornament is carried to the end, it is used up (則盡矣, *zé jìn yǐ*, "then it is exhausted").

**For the name.** **Adornment**. The signature deepens it, because this is adornment held from
above and ending in white. The name has to survive 白賁, "white adornment," and it does, since the
phrase needs a noun that can be plain. **Candidates the signature suggests:** *Pattern* survives the
lines (賁其趾, "pattern his toes"; 白賁, "white pattern"), but it is the 彖傳's 文 (*wén*, "pattern")
and not 賁, which is commentary supplying the word. *Glow* fails the lines: 賁其趾 and 賁其須 need a
transitive verb, and "glow his toes" is not English.

## The heart of it

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-25 — an interpretive reading, not a translation. Each claim is marked by
layer: the core text (the judgment and the six lines, Western Zhou), the Wings (Warring States to
Han), 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*, 3rd century), and echoes of the Tao Te Ching in this project's own translation.
The formula words — 吉, 凶, 无咎, 亨, 孚, 貞 — carry working glosses until WORKLIST A4 and A6 rule on
them ([`hexagram-names.md`](../hexagram-names.md)).*

### Essence

**Adornment gets things through, but only a little way — and the further it goes, the plainer it
becomes.** 說文 (*shuōwén*) gives 賁 (*bì*) as 飾也 (*shì yě*, "to adorn"), and builds it on 貝 (*bèi*),
the cowrie shell — the first ornament and the first money. The core text adorns a body from the feet
up: the toes, the beard, a glistening, white hair, a hill orchard, and at the top 白賁 (*bái bì*,
"white adornment"). Read in order, the colour drains out of it. The 雜卦 (*záguà*) says it in four
characters: 賁无色也 (*bì wú sè yě*, "賁 is without colour").

### The polarities in it

Fire within, mountain without. The inner figure is hollow at its centre and attaches; the outer one
is closed at its top and stops. So the light is inside and below, and what it lights is a mass that
does not move for it. The 彖 (*tuàn*) puts the limit in one phrase — 文明以止 (*wén míng yǐ zhǐ*,
"pattern and clarity, and stopping"). Adornment shows a thing; it does not change what the thing is,
and the mountain sets the edge past which showing cannot go.

### In nature

The 大象 (*dà xiàng*) gives the image: 山下有火 (*shān xià yǒu huǒ*, "below the mountain there is
fire"). Look at a hillside lit from low down — by fires in a valley at night, or by the sun in its
last half hour. Low light rakes across a surface and throws every ridge, terrace wall and line of
trees into relief; the same slope at noon looks flat. That is what adornment does at its best: it
does not add a ridge, it shows the ones that are there. And the light only reaches so far. The
summit stays dark.

The 彖 looks up from the mountain: 觀乎天文，以察時變 (*guān hū tiān wén, yǐ chá shí biàn*, "watch the
pattern of the sky to see the changes of the seasons"). This is good observation. The stars rise
about four minutes earlier each night, so the constellations overhead at a fixed hour move round
through the year, and the pattern tells the season. The lines keep their own creatures: 皤 (*pó*),
which 說文 gives as 老人白也 (*lǎo rén bái yě*, "the white of the old") — hair going white, an
adornment no one chooses — and a white horse coming at a gallop.

### The movement of the lines

**The judgment.** 賁：亨。小利有攸往 (*bì: hēng. xiǎo lì yǒu yōu wǎng*) — "Adornment *(proposed)*: gets
through. A small advantage in having somewhere to go." The core gives adornment its due, and its
measure: *small*.

**Line 1** starts at the feet. 賁其趾，舍車而徒 (*bì qí zhǐ, shě jū ér tú*, "adorns the toes; leaves
the carriage and goes on foot"). The first act of adornment is to give something up — the carriage,
the display of rank — and walk.

**Line 2** is brief. 賁其須 (*bì qí xū*, "adorns the beard"). 須 (*xū*) is 說文 面毛也 (*miàn máo yě*,
"the hair of the face"). A beard moves when the jaw moves; the 小象 (*xiǎo xiàng*) has 與上興也 (*yǔ
shàng xīng yě*, "it rises with what is above"). Adornment that goes where the face goes, and has no
motion of its own.

**Line 3**, the top of the fire, is the height of it. 賁如濡如，永貞吉 (*bì rú rú rú, yǒng zhēn jí*,
"adorned, glistening; holding firm long — auspicious"). 濡 (*rú*) is wet — the gloss on a surface.
At its most lustrous the line is warned to hold firm, and for a long time.

**Line 4**, at the seam, turns white. 賁如皤如，白馬翰如，匪寇婚媾 (*bì rú pó rú, bái mǎ hàn rú, fěi
kòu hūn gòu*, "adorned, white-haired; a white horse as if winged — not raiders: a marriage party").
Riders coming fast look like an attack; they are a wedding. The 小象: 當位疑也 (*dāng wèi yí yě*,
"rightly placed, and in doubt"). Here, appearance misleads, and the line has to look again.

**Line 5**, the centre above, is adorned by the land. 賁於丘園，束帛戔戔，吝，終吉 (*bì yú qiū yuán,
shù bó jiān jiān, lìn, zhōng jí*, "adorned in the hill orchard; the bundle of silk is meagre —
stinting, in the end auspicious"). 園 (*yuán*) is 說文 所以樹果也 (*suǒ yǐ shù guǒ yě*, "where fruit
trees are planted"). The orchard is the ornament; the gift is thin. 王弼: 用莫過儉 (*yòng mò guò jiǎn*,
"in use, nothing exceeds frugality").

**Line 6** is white. 白賁，无咎 (*bái bì, wú jiù*, "white adornment — no blame"). 王弼: 飾終反素 (*shì
zhōng fǎn sù*, "adornment at its end turns back to the undyed"). The top of the figure, the
mountain's closed edge, is where the colour ends.

### In a life now

This is the figure for how things are presented: dress, a wedding, a room made ready, a book's
design, the way a proposal is written. The core does not despise any of it — the judgment says it
gets through — but it says *a little*, and its arc runs one way, toward plain. Begin by putting down
the carriage (line 1). Distrust the gloss when it is at its brightest (line 3). Do not judge riders by
their dust (line 4). A small gift in a true place is enough (line 5). The finest adornment is white.

The 大象 draws the limit as practice: 君子以明庶政，无敢折獄 (*jūnzǐ yǐ míng shù zhèng, wú gǎn zhé
yù*, "the noble one makes the many matters of government clear, and does not venture to decide
cases"). Presentation may make things clear. It may not decide them — the case that 21 bit through
is not settled by how it looks.

### An echo from the Laozi

**Thematic only, and it runs through the Wings and 王弼, not the core.** The Laozi's chapter 19 sets
aside three goods because *"these three, taken as cultural form, are not enough"*, and ends *"see the
undyed silk, embrace the uncarved wood."* The graphs are 文 (*wén*, "pattern, cultural form") and 素
(*sù*, "undyed silk"). Neither is in 賁's core text: 文 is the 彖's word, and 素 is 王弼's, in the
note on line 6 quoted above. The core has only 白 (*bái*, "white") and 帛 (*bó*, "silk") — close in
image, not in word. So the two-question test fails, and this stays a thematic echo.

**Where they part is the point.** Chapter 19 cuts cultural form off. 賁 keeps it: every line adorns,
the judgment says it gets through, and the 彖 ends 以化成天下 (*yǐ huà chéng tiān xià*, "to transform
and complete the world"). The Laozi says *not enough*; the Changes says *enough, and small, and
ending in white*. The I Ching limits adornment; it does not renounce it. This reading does not make
it renounce it.

### Words kept

- 賁 (*bì*) — *Adornment*, the proposed name (not yet approved); *pattern* is 文 (*wén*), the 彖's
  word, and the Tao Te Ching has *cultural form* for 文 in chapter 19 — two Englishes for one graph,
  for Shalom to reconcile
- 白 (*bái*) — *white*; 白賁 (*bái bì*) *white adornment*, never *plain* alone, which would drop the
  adornment

- 天下 (*tiān xià*) — *the world* (locked); 天文 (*tiān wén*) *the pattern of the sky*
