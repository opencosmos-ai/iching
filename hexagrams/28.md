## The signature — a reading

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-24. This reads the figure through the Wings — the declared lens
([`README.md`](../README.md#the-lens)) — and it is a reading, not a ruling. The name still comes from
the lines ([`read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums`](../principles/read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums.md)).*

**The signature.** Within: **wind** (巽 · yin · entering). Without: **lake** (兌 · yin · releasing).
**Across** — thunder ↔ wind under mountain ↔ lake, both poles yin.

**What the tradition reads.** The 彖 (*tuàn*) says 大者過也 (*dà zhě guò yě*, "the great exceeds"), 棟橈，本末弱也
(*dòng náo, běn mò ruò yě*, "the ridgepole sags: root and tip are yielding"), and 剛過而中，巽而說行
(*gāng guò ér zhōng, xùn ér yuè xíng*, "the firm exceeds, yet holds the centre; entering and
releasing, it goes"). The 大象 (*dà xiàng*) sees 澤滅木 (*zé miè mù*, "the lake drowns the wood"), and
the noble one 獨立不懼，遯世无悶 (*dú lì bù jù, dùn shì wú mèn*, "stands alone without fear,
withdraws from the world without gloom"). The tradition reads more weight in the middle than the ends
can carry — 雜卦 (*záguà*): 大過顛也 (*dà guò diān yě*, "great exceeding is toppling").

**What the polarity implies.** Two yin trigrams — and four of the six lines are firm. The poles are
yin because each trigram's odd line is open, and the signature puts those two open lines at the
extremes: wind is open at its base, lake at its top. So the only give in the figure is the bottom line
and the top line, and all the strength is packed between. That is the 彖's 本末 (*běn mò*, "root and
tip"), arrived at from the structure alone. It is 27's complement line for line: there, firm ends and
an empty middle, a mouth wanting filling; here, a full middle and yielding ends, a beam carrying more
than its supports.

The ends then act as their trigrams act. At the base, wind enters beneath: 藉用白茅，无咎 (*jiè yòng
bái máo, wú jiù*, "a mat of white rushes laid under — no fault", 28.1), softness put where the load
will land. At the top, the lake opens over: 過涉滅頂 (*guò shè miè dǐng*, "wading across, the water
closes over the crown", 28.6). The structure suggests the figure's counsel is not to lighten the
middle, which cannot be done, but to tend the ends — cushion the root, and know that at the tip the
water closes over. Between, the withered willow still puts out shoots — 枯楊生稊 (*kū yáng shēng tí*,
"the withered willow brings forth shoots", 28.2): too much, out of season, and alive.

**For the name.** **Great Exceeding** — deepens: the signature shows what exceeds what — the middle
exceeds its ends — and the name keeps 過 (*guò*, "exceed, go past") a verb, as 62 needs. **Candidates
the signature suggests:** *Great Overreach* — survives: 過涉滅頂 reads "overreaching the ford, the
water closes over the crown", and 過 stays a verb; *Great Overload* — fails the lines: it names the
sagging ridgepole of 28.3 and 28.4 well, but 過涉 (*guò shè*, "wading past") needs 過 as a verb, and 62
needs it four times.

## The heart of it

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-25 — an interpretive reading, not a translation. Each claim is marked by
layer: the core text (the judgment and the six lines, Western Zhou), the Wings (Warring States to
Han), 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*, 3rd century), and echoes of the Tao Te Ching in this project's own translation.
The formula words — 吉, 凶, 无咎, 亨, 孚, 貞 — carry working glosses until WORKLIST A4 and A6 rule on
them ([`hexagram-names.md`](../hexagram-names.md)).*

### Essence

**The ridgepole sags — more weight in the middle than the ends can carry — and the counsel is not to
wait beneath it but to go.** The judgment gives the house before anything else: 棟橈 (*dòng náo*, "the
ridgepole sags"). 說文 (*shuōwén*) has 棟 (*dòng*) as 極也 (*jí yě*, "the utmost", the highest beam)
and 橈 (*náo*) as 曲木 (*qū mù*, "bent wood"). And 過 (*guò*) is 度也 (*dù yě*, "to pass across"), walking
beyond a measure. Everything in the lines is past its measure — an old tree putting out shoots, an
old man with a young wife, a wader in over the head — and the judgment still says 利有攸往，亨 (*lì
yǒu yōu wǎng, hēng*, "it is advantageous to have somewhere to go; it gets through").

### The polarities in it

Wind within, lake without, both open at their odd line — wind at the very bottom, the lake at the
very top — so the only give in the figure is at its two ends, and four firm lines are packed between
(see *The signature*, above). The 彖 (*tuàn*) says it from the structure: 本末弱也 (*běn mò ruò yě*,
"root and tip are yielding"). It is 27 turned inside out: there, firm ends round an empty mouth;
here, a full middle on ends that give. **The middle cannot be made lighter. What can be done is at
the ends: something soft laid under the root, and the knowledge that at the tip the water closes
over.**

### In nature

The 大象 (*dà xiàng*) sees 澤滅木 (*zé miè mù*, "the lake drowns the wood"). Find a place where water
has risen and stayed — behind a beaver dam, at the edge of a new reservoir, on a river meadow after a
wet year. The trees stand grey in the water, dead from the root: roots need air, and standing water
takes it. The weight is not on the branches but at the root, below the surface.

Now look at the willows along the same water. Willow is among the few trees that endure flooding;
it puts out new roots from the drowned stem. And an old willow that seems finished — hollow,
split, pollarded to a stump — will send up a crown of straight shoots from the wood that is left,
and a broken branch pushed into wet mud will root. Lines 2 and 5 hold this exactly: 枯楊生稊 (*kū
yáng shēng tí*, "the withered willow brings forth shoots") and 枯楊生華 (*kū yáng shēng huā*, "the
withered willow brings forth flowers"). 王弼 says what the difference is: 能生華不能生稊 (*néng shēng
huā bù néng shēng tí*, "it can bring forth flowers, and cannot bring forth shoots"). Shoots renew
the tree. Catkins come in early spring, before the leaves, and are gone in weeks.

Line 1's 白茅 (*bái máo*, "white rushes") is a grass with silky white plumes and white running roots.
說文 gives 藉 (*jiè*) as 祭藉也 (*jì jiè yě*, "the mat under an offering").

### The movement of the lines

**The judgment.** 大過：棟橈，利有攸往，亨 (*dà guò: dòng náo, lì yǒu yōu wǎng, hēng*) — "Great
exceeding. The ridgepole sags. It is advantageous to have somewhere to go; it gets through." A house
under too much load is not a place to stay.

**Line 1**, open, the root. 藉用白茅，无咎 (*jiè yòng bái máo, wú jiù*, "for a mat, use white rushes — no
blame"). The 繫辭上 (*xìcí shàng*) gives this line a paragraph: 苟錯諸地而可矣 (*gǒu cuò zhū dì ér kě
yǐ*, "setting it on the ground would have done"), and yet the mat — 慎之至也 (*shèn zhī zhì yě*, "care
at its utmost").

**Line 2**, firm, the lower centre. 枯楊生稊，老夫得其女妻，无不利 (*kū yáng shēng tí, lǎo fū dé qí nǚ qī,
wú bù lì*, "the withered willow brings forth shoots; the old man gets a young wife — nothing is not
advantageous"). Excess at the root, and it renews.

**Line 3**, firm, at the seam. 棟橈，凶 (*dòng náo, xiōng*, "the ridgepole sags — ominous"). The beam
is firm in a firm place and bends anyway. The 小象 (*xiǎo xiàng*): 不可以有輔也 (*bù kě yǐ yǒu fǔ yě*,
"it cannot be propped").

**Line 4**, firm, across the seam. 棟隆，吉。有它吝 (*dòng lóng, jí. yǒu tā lìn*, "the ridgepole rises —
auspicious. If there is something else, stinting"). The same beam, arched up instead of bowed; the
小象 says 不橈乎下也 (*bù náo hū xià yě*, "it does not sag toward what is below"). 3 and 4 are the
ridgepole's two halves, and the fortune turns at the seam.

**Line 5**, firm, the upper centre. 枯楊生華，老婦得其士夫，无咎无譽 (*kū yáng shēng huā, lǎo fù dé qí shì
fū, wú jiù wú yù*, "the withered willow brings forth flowers; the old woman gets a young husband —
no blame, no praise"). Excess at the tip, and it flowers and spends. The core text gives the old
man's match *nothing not advantageous* and the old woman's *no blame, no praise*; the 小象 goes
further and calls hers 亦可丑也 (*yì kě chǒu yě*, "also shameful"). That verdict is the Wing's, not
the line's.

**Line 6**, open, the tip. 過涉滅頂，凶，无咎 (*guò shè miè dǐng, xiōng, wú jiù*, "wading across, the water
closes over the crown — ominous, no blame"). 說文 gives 頂 (*dǐng*) as 顚也 (*diān yě*, "the top of the
head"). Here the text separates outcome from fault in one line: it ends badly, and no one is to
blame. 王弼: 志在救時 (*zhì zài jiù shí*, "the aim was to save the time").

### In a life now

This is the figure for the load that is simply too much: the carer holding a family through an
illness, the small organisation doing a large one's work, the year in which everything falls due
at once, the land carrying more than it can. The text does not say lighten the middle, which
cannot be done. It says tend the ends — lay something soft under the root, with more care than
seems needed (line 1); expect renewal from the base, not from display at the top (lines 2 and 5);
and do not stay under the sagging beam (the judgment). Line 6 is the person who goes in over their
head because someone had to, and the text will not blame them for it.

The 大象 gives the stance: 君子以獨立不懼，遯世无悶 (*jūnzǐ yǐ dú lì bù jù, dùn shì wú mèn*, "the noble one
stands alone without fear, and withdraws from the world without gloom").

### An echo from the Laozi

**A shared graph, and a flat disagreement.** The Laozi's chapter 76: *"In life the countless things,
the grasses and trees, are soft and tender. / In death, withered and dry."* *Withered* is 枯 (*kū*) —
the graph of lines 2 and 5, in the core text, and Shalom's English answers to it. In both books the
withered tree stands for what is past its life.

They part completely on what happens next. In the Laozi the withered is death's side, and it stays
there. In 大過 (*dà guò*, "great exceeding") the withered willow brings forth shoots, and the text calls
it advantageous. And the 彖's 本末弱 (*běn mò ruò*, "root and tip are yielding") names the yielding
ends as the reason the beam sags — where chapter 76 has *"the soft and yielding dwell above."* **The
books disagree about the yielding here, and this reading leaves them disagreeing.** (The 大象's
獨立 (*dú lì*, "stand alone") is also in chapter 25's *"Solitary"*, said of the Tao; that is a
Wing's phrase, and the subject differs.)
