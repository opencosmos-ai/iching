## The signature — a reading

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-24. This reads the figure through the Wings — the declared lens
([`README.md`](../README.md#the-lens)) — and it is a reading, not a ruling. The name still comes from
the lines ([`read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums`](../principles/read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums.md)).*

**The signature.** Within: **water** (坎 · yang · sinking in). Without: **earth** (坤 · yin · going
along). **Across** — water ↔ fire meets sky ↔ earth, yang within and yin without, the odd line at the
middle within and none without.

**What the tradition reads.** The 彖 (*tuàn*): 師，眾也。貞，正也。能以眾正，可以王矣 (*shī, zhòng
yě. zhēn, zhèng yě. néng yǐ zhòng zhèng, kěyǐ wàng yǐ*), "師 (*shī*) is the many; 貞 (*zhēn*) is being
upright; one who can make the many upright can rule." And: 剛中而應，行險而順 (*gāng zhōng ér yìng,
xíng xiǎn ér shùn*), "firm at the centre and answered; going into hazard, and going along." The 大象
(*dà xiàng*, the great image): 地中有水，師；君子以容民畜衆 (*dì zhōng yǒu shuǐ, shī; jūnzǐ yǐ róng
mín xù zhòng*), "within the earth there is water: 師; the noble one, by it, makes room for the folk
and keeps the many." The tradition reads the many organised round one firm centre.

**What the polarity implies.** One firm line in six, and it is not in the ruler's seat: it is 九二
(*jiǔ èr*), the centre of the lower trigram. The yielding fifth answers it — the 彖's 剛中而應. So the
force of the figure belongs to the commander in the field, not the ruler, and the line says so:
在師中吉 (*zài shī zhōng jí*), "in the midst of the army: auspicious." The image is groundwater —
hazard stored inside the body of the ground, invisible from outside. An army is the dangerous thing
held within the settled many: farmers who are also soldiers, earth that goes along wrapped round a
sunk, hard core. Everything therefore depends on that one line holding. 師出以律 (*shī chū yǐ lǜ*),
"the army goes out by regulation"; replace the one who commands and the open lines have nothing to
go along with — 弟子輿尸 (*dìzǐ yú shī*), "the younger son carts corpses."

Turn the figure over and it is 8: the single firm line moves from second to fifth, from commander to
ruler, and the water comes up out of the earth to lie on it. In 7 the many are held together under a
line below them; in 8 they are drawn toward a line above.

**For the name.** **The Army** — supported and deepened: one firm line holding five yielding ones
from the lower centre is the shape of a command, and the signature adds that an army is a hazard
kept inside a peaceable body. **Candidates the signature suggests:** *The Levy* — the many raised
from the land; survives the lines: 師出以律 "the levy goes out by regulation", 師或輿尸 (*shī huò yú
shī*) "the levy perhaps carts corpses", 長子帥師 (*zhǎngzǐ shuài shī*) "the eldest son leads the
levy." *Discipline* — from 律 (*lǜ*, "regulation") and the lone firm line; fails the lines at 師或輿尸,
since discipline does not cart corpses.

## The heart of it

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-25 — an interpretive reading, not a translation. Each claim is marked by
layer: the core text (the judgment and the six lines, Western Zhou), the Wings (Warring States to
Han), 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*, 3rd century), and echoes of the Tao Te Ching in this project's own translation.
The formula words — 吉, 凶, 无咎, 亨, 孚, 貞 — carry working glosses until WORKLIST A4 and A6 rule on
them ([`hexagram-names.md`](../hexagram-names.md)).*

### Essence

**An army is a hazard carried inside a people, and everything turns on who holds it.** 說文
(*shuōwén*) gives 師 (*shī*) as a number — 二千五百人爲師 (*èr qiān wǔ bǎi rén wéi shī*, "two thousand
five hundred people make a 師") — and reads its parts as 眾意也 (*zhòng yì yě*, "the sense of the
many"). The core text is almost entirely about command: the army going out by rule, one leader in
its midst, the wrong leader carting corpses, the ruler choosing between an eldest son and a younger.
Its judgment names the only thing it trusts: 丈人 (*zhàngrén*), the elder — a grown person of
standing. The many are dangerous; the question the lines keep asking is who holds them.

### The polarities in it

Within is water: hazard, its one firm line at the centre. Without is earth, open all through, going
along. So the force of the figure sits low, at the second line, and the ruler's seat above is
yielding — it does not command in person; it answers and delegates. The 大象 (*dà xiàng*) sees the
shape exactly: 地中有水 (*dì zhōng yǒu shuǐ*, "within the earth there is water"). The army is the
dangerous thing kept inside a peaceable body, farmers who are also soldiers. Everything depends on
the one line that holds it — and when that line is wrongly filled, the open lines around it have
nothing to go along with.

### In nature

Under every field there is water. Dig a well in a dry plain and at some depth the sides begin to
darken and seep; that water was there all along, under the crops, unseen from above. It is what
keeps the field alive, and it is also what floods a cellar or undermines a bank when it rises.
That is the 大象's image, and a good one for a levy: the strength of the many, stored where no one
sees it until it is drawn up.

The fifth line's 田有禽 (*tián yǒu qín*, "there is game in the fields") is farm country too. 說文
gives 禽 (*qín*) as 走獸緫名 (*zǒu shòu zǒng míng*, "the general name for running beasts") — not
birds; deer and boar in the millet. Anyone who farms near woodland knows the night raids and the
flattened rows. The hunt that clears them used the same people, carts and drill as the campaign;
in this line they are one act.

And 說文 has an unsparing picture in the third line's 尸 (*shī*, "corpse"): 象臥之形 (*xiàng wò zhī
xíng*, "it pictures the shape of one lying down"). The graph does not look away.

### The movement of the lines

**The judgment.** 師：貞丈人吉，无咎 (*shī: zhēn zhàngrén jí, wú jiù*) — "The army. Holding firm, the
elder: auspicious, no blame." Here the older sense of 貞 (*zhēn*) matters: "an inquiry about the
elder — auspicious" reads as naturally, and on either reading the good fortune is tied to one
person. 王弼 says what kind: 嚴莊之稱也 (*yán zhuāng zhī chēng yě*, "a name for the stern and grave").

**Line 1** is the going out. 師出以律，否臧，凶 (*shī chū yǐ lǜ, pǐ zāng, xiōng*, "the army goes out
by regulation; if not well done, ominous"). 說文 gives 律 (*lǜ*) as 均布也 (*jūn bù yě*, "spread
evenly") — the rule that reaches every rank alike. 王弼 reads 否臧 (*pǐ zāng*) as "whether it
succeeds or not": 否臧皆凶 (*pǐ zāng jiē xiōng*, "success and failure both ominous") — an army out of
order is ominous even when it wins.

**Line 2** is the one firm line, the centre of the water. 在師中吉，无咎；王三錫命 (*zài shī zhōng
jí, wú jiù; wáng sān xī mìng*, "in the midst of the army: auspicious, no blame; the ruler three times
confers the allotted course"). The commander is in the midst of the troops, not behind them, and the
charge is spoken over them from above, again and again.

**Line 3** is open at the seam, sitting on the firm line. 師或輿尸，凶 (*shī huò yú shī, xiōng*, "the
army perhaps carts corpses: ominous"). The first ominous outcome falls where the wrong kind of line
takes the place of command. The 小象 (*xiǎo xiàng*) adds 大无功也 (*dà wú gōng yě*, "greatly without
achievement").

**Line 4** is the first line of earth: the army stops. 師左次，无咎 (*shī zuǒ cì, wú jiù*, "the army
halts on the left: no blame"). 說文 gives 次 (*cì*) as 不前 (*bù qián*, "not going forward").
Nothing is gained, and nothing is lost; the 小象 says 未失常也 (*wèi shī cháng yě*, "it has not lost
the abiding"). Halting is a move.

**Line 5** is the ruler's seat, open, and the line where the decision is made. 田有禽，利執言，无咎。
長子帥師，弟子輿尸，貞凶 (*tián yǒu qín, lì zhí yán, wú jiù. zhǎngzǐ shuài shī, dìzǐ yú shī, zhēn
xiōng*, "there is game in the fields: advantage in grasping it — no blame. The eldest son leads the
army; the younger son carts corpses: holding firm, ominous"). There is a real cause — the game is in
the fields — and the open ruler must hand the command to someone. 王弼: 柔非軍帥 (*róu fēi jūn
shuài*, "the soft is not a commander"). Choose rightly and there is no blame; choose wrongly, and
holding to that choice is ominous.

**Line 6** is the end of the campaign. 大君有命，開國承家，小人勿用 (*dà jūn yǒu mìng, kāi guó chéng
jiā, xiǎorén wù yòng*, "the great ruler speaks the allotted course: states are opened, houses carried
on; small people — do not use them"). The war ends in founding and rewarding, and the line's one
warning is about who receives what the war has won.

Both ominous outcomes carry the same cart of corpses (lines 3 and 5), and both come from the same
fault: the wrong one in command.

### In a life now

Not only war: anything where many people are raised at once to meet a hazard — a strike, a fire
crew, an emergency ward in a bad winter, a campaign, a family gathering round a parent who is
dying. The figure's counsel is organisational and blunt. **Go out by rule, evenly applied** (line 1).
**Put one experienced person in the midst of it, with real authority, and back them repeatedly**
(line 2). **If you are the one who delegates, the choice of who leads is the whole of your part** —
the wrong one fills the carts (line 5). **Know when to halt** (line 4). And when it is over, do not
hand what was won to small people (line 6). 雜卦 (*záguà*) sums up its mood in one word: 師憂
(*shī yōu*, "the army: care, sorrow").

The 大象 turns the matter back to the people the army comes from: 君子以容民畜衆 (*jūnzǐ yǐ róng mín
xù zhòng*, "the noble one makes room for the folk and keeps the crowd"). 畜 (*xù*) has a field, 田 (*tián*),
in it: the many are kept as a farm keeps what it rears. The strength of a levy is the life of the
land it is drawn from.

### An echo from the Laozi

**The two books share the graph, and see the army from opposite sides.** The Laozi's chapter 30:
*"Where armies camp, thorns and brambles grow. / After a big military campaign, a year of disaster
follows."* The word is 師 (*shī*), the same graph that names this hexagram and stands in five of its
lines in the core text.

The Laozi stands outside the campaign and counts what grows after it. 師 stands inside it and asks
how it is done rightly: its second line is auspicious, and its fifth finds an army sometimes
warranted — there is game in the fields. The two meet only at the carts of corpses, which the core
text does not soften any more than the Laozi does. **That the I Ching counsels how to command, where
the Laozi counsels not to dominate, is a real difference and stays one** (see
[`tao-te-ching-relation.md`](../tao-te-ching-relation.md) § 6, failure 2).

### Words kept

- 師 (*shī*) — *the army*, the proposed name (not yet approved); *the levy* is the strongest alternative
- 眾 (*zhòng*) — *the crowd / the many*, never *the multitude* or *the masses* (locked)
- 命 (*mìng*) — *the allotted course*, never *mandate*, never *decree* (locked); here it strains toward a commission spoken over the one sent
- 王 (*wáng*) — *ruler*, never *king* (locked); 大君 (*dà jūn*) — *the great ruler*, which collides with 王 and needs a ruling
- 常 (*cháng*) — *the abiding*, never *eternal* (locked)
- 貞 (*zhēn*) — *holding firm*, working gloss; the older reading, *the inquiry*, matters in the judgment
- 吉 · 凶 · 无咎 (*jí · xiōng · wú jiù*) — *auspicious · ominous · no blame*, working glosses (A4)
- 小人 · 丈人 (*xiǎorén · zhàngrén*) — *small people · the elder*
- 律 (*lǜ*) — *regulation*; 執 (*zhí*) — *grasp* (locked), here in its plain sense, the hand on the game; never *cling*
