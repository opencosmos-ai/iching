## The signature — a reading

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-24. This reads the figure through the Wings — the declared lens
([`README.md`](../README.md#the-lens)) — and it is a reading, not a ruling. The name still comes from
the lines ([`read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums`](../principles/read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums.md)).*

**The signature.** Within: **sky** (乾 · yang · vigour). Without: **sky** (乾 · yang · vigour).
**Doubled** — the yang pole of sky ↔ earth taken twice, with no odd line anywhere in the six.

**What the tradition reads.** The 大象 (*dà xiàng*, the great image) gives the plainest version:
天行健，君子以自強不息 (*tiān xíng jiàn, jūnzǐ yǐ zì qiáng bù xī*), "the sky moves with vigour; the
noble one, by it, makes the self strong and does not stop." The 彖 (*tuàn*) makes the figure the
start of everything: 大哉乾元，萬物資始，乃統天 (*dà zāi qián yuán, wànwù zī shǐ, nǎi tǒng tiān*),
"great is 乾's (*qián*'s) origin — the countless things draw their start from it, and so it runs
through the sky" — and orders that start in time: 時乘六龍以御天 (*shí chéng liù lóng yǐ yù tiān*),
"in its season it rides six dragons to drive the sky." The tradition reads pure initiating force,
paced by the six positions.

**What the polarity implies.** Every trigram but 乾 (*qián*) and 坤 (*kūn*) is defined by where its odd line stands;
here there is no odd line inside or out, so nothing in the figure marks a place. With no height to
read, the six lines can only be read as *time*: the dragon hidden (潛龍 *qián lóng*), seen in the
field, striving all day, perhaps leaping in the deep, flying in the sky, overreaching. The six
dragons of the 彖 arrive straight from the structure. Vigour within meets vigour without, so
nothing pushes back — and the structure suggests the figure's hazard is therefore not an obstacle
but the absence of one. A force with no counter-pole in its own figure has to supply its own check,
which is exactly what line 3 does: 夕惕若 (*xī tì ruò*), "wary at dusk." When it does not, the top
line has it: 亢龍有悔 (*kàng lóng yǒu huǐ*), "the overreaching dragon has regret."

Read with 2, the pair is not strong against weak but beginning against bearing: 乾 (*qián*) 資始
(*zī shǐ*), "draws the start", where 坤 (*kūn*) 資生 (*zī shēng*), "draws the birth". Each, doubled
to its top, runs into the other — 乾's dragon overreaches; 坤's top line is a dragon fighting in the
open country. And their two extra lines part ways: 乾's 用九 (*yòng jiǔ*, "using the nines") ends in
見羣龍无首 (*jiàn qún lóng wú shǒu*), "seeing a flock of dragons with no head" — pure vigour, led by
nobody, is auspicious; 坤's ends in duration.

**For the name.** **Striving** — the signature deepens it: 乾乾 (*qián qián*) in line 3 doubles the
graph as the figure doubles the trigram, and an unopposed force is one that has to keep itself
going and keep itself wary, which *striving* carries and *Vigour* (kept for 34, and the action-word
of this very trigram) would not. **Candidates the signature suggests:** *Unresting* — from 自強不息
(*zì qiáng bù xī*, "makes the self strong and does not stop"); fails the lines, since the first is
潛龍勿用 (*qián lóng wù yòng*), "hidden dragon: do not act." *The Dragon* — survives five of the six
lines and the 用九, but not line 3, whose 乾乾 has no dragon, and it names the lines' image rather
than the graph.

## The heart of it

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-25 — an interpretive reading, not a translation. Each claim is marked by
layer: the core text (the judgment and the six lines, Western Zhou), the Wings (Warring States to
Han), Wang Bi (the 3rd-century commentator), and echoes of the Tao Te Ching in this project's own
translation. The formula words — 吉 ("auspicious"), 凶 ("ominous"), 无咎 ("no blame"), 亨 ("gets
through"), 孚 ("confidence"), 貞 ("holding firm") — carry working glosses until WORKLIST A4 and A6
rule on them ([`hexagram-names.md`](../hexagram-names.md)).*

### Essence

**A force that begins everything and meets nothing, so it has to find its own pace and its own
limit.** The Shuowen, the c. 100 CE character dictionary, gives 乾 ("Striving") as 上出也 ("going up and
out"), with 乙 ("a curling sprout") at its side, glossed 物之達也 ("things getting through"). The core
text never says what 乾 ("Striving") does. It follows one creature, the dragon, through six stations
— under water, seen in the field, gone while a person strives, leaping, flying, overreaching — and
ends with a flock that has no head. The strength never changes from line to line. What changes is
the moment, and the same strength is right at one station and ruinous at another. **This is the
book's first lesson, and it is about timing.**

### The polarities in it

Sky within, sky without: the firm pole twice, and not one open line in the six. Every other trigram
is marked by the height of its odd line; here nothing marks a height, so the six places can only be
read as moments (see *The signature*, above). Nothing in the figure pushes back. That is its gift
and its hazard at once. **With nothing outside to check it, the check has to come from inside** —
line 3's wariness at dusk — or it comes too late, at the top, as regret.

### In nature

Two natural things are in this figure. The first is the sky, which the Great Image, the Wing on the
whole figure, names: 天行健 ("the sky moves with vigour"). Watch it through one clear evening. The
stars rise in the east, cross, and set in the west, the whole bowl wheeling round a still point in
the north, and it has never once stopped. That is the Great Image's picture — not a force that
strikes, but a going that does not halt.

The second is the dragon, and the Shuowen reads it by the sky's own calendar: 龍 ("dragon")
能幽，能明，能細，能巨 ("it can be dark, it can be clear, it can be small, it can be huge") … 春分而登天，秋分而潛淵 ("at
the spring equinox it climbs the sky; at the autumn equinox it sinks into the deep"). A modern
reading, not in this project's sources, takes the lines' dragon for the band of stars early Chinese
astronomy called the Dragon — whose second lodge is named 亢 ("overreaching"), the word of line 6,
which the Shuowen glosses 人頸也 ("a person's neck"). Look east an hour after sunset in April: the
bright star Spica, the Dragon's horn, lifts off the horizon — a dragon seen in the field. On
midsummer evenings the long body, with red Antares at its heart, lies low across the south; in
autumn it slides down into the west at dusk; in winter it is under the earth with the sun. Whether
or not the line-writers meant it, the sky does what the lines say, and anyone can watch it.

### The movement of the lines

**The judgment.** 乾：元亨。利貞 ("Striving *(proposed)*: from the source, it gets through; advantageous:
holding firm"). Four words, and no image and no condition. The Shuowen gives 貞 ("holding firm") as
卜問也 ("asking by the cracked bone"), so the older sense is "an advantageous inquiry". The Wenyan, the
Wing on hexagrams 1 and 2, turns the four words into four qualities of the noble one, and gives 貞
("holding firm") its later weight: 貞者，事之幹也 ("holding firm is the trunk of affairs"). The working
gloss *holding firm* comes from that Wing, and it is labelled as such.

**Line 1** is under water. 潛龍勿用 ("the dragon submerged: do not act"). The Shuowen adds to 潛
("submerged") 一曰藏也 ("also: to store away"). The strength is already whole; the time is not. The
Wenyan gives the line its stance: 確乎其不可拔 ("firm — it cannot be pulled up"), like a root, not a sulk.

**Line 2** is the lower centre. 見龍在田，利見大人 ("the dragon appears in the field; advantageous to see the
great person"). The Shuowen's 田 ("field") is ground planted with grain, its cross the paths between
plots: the dragon first shows itself where people work.

**Line 3** is the seam, and the dragon is gone. 君子終日乾乾，夕惕若；厲，无咎 ("the noble one strives and strives
all day, and at dusk is wary; peril — no blame"). The Shuowen gives 惕 ("wary") as 敬也 ("attentive
care"). Wang Bi says why the creature has left the line: 乾乾夕惕非龍德也 ("striving and dusk-wariness are
not a dragon's integrity"). At the top of the lower sky, with no centre to stand on, the force needs
a person — effort by day, a check at nightfall. This is the figure's own brake.

**Line 4** crosses into the upper sky and tests it. 或躍在淵，无咎 ("perhaps leaping in the deep — no
blame"). The Shuowen gives 淵 ("the deep") as 回水也 ("water that turns back on itself"), a pool where
the current circles. The Wenyan reads the first word: 或之者，疑之也 ("'perhaps' means in doubt"). A trial
leap, from the water and back into it — blameless because it is a test.

**Line 5** is the upper centre. 飛龍在天，利見大人 ("the dragon flies in the sky; advantageous to see the
great person"). Both centres get the same verdict — seen on the ground, seen in the sky. The Wenyan
answers with its list of kinds that find each other: 水流濕，火就燥，雲從龍，風從虎 ("water flows to the wet, fire
goes to the dry, clouds follow the dragon, wind follows the tiger").

**Line 6** is past the height. 亢龍，有悔 ("the overreaching dragon: there is regret"). The Small Image,
the Wing on each line: 盈不可久也 ("fullness cannot last"); the Wenyan: 知進而不知退 ("it knows advancing and
not withdrawing"). Not a punishment — only a force that has not noticed the station changed.

**Using the nines.** 見羣龍无首，吉 ("seeing a flock of dragons with no head: auspicious"). 羣 ("flock") is
written with 羊 ("sheep") — a flock's word, given to dragons. The Small Image: 天德不可為首也 ("the sky's
integrity cannot be made a head"). Pure strength is well only when no one part of it rules the rest.

### In a life now

This is the figure for a beginning with nothing yet against it: a first real talent, a venture with
money and goodwill behind it, a movement with no rival, a founder's early years — and also the long
strong middle of a working life. It does not tell anyone to be less strong. It tells them where they
are. **Stay under while the time is not come**; show yourself where the work is done, and find the
person who can see you; through the long middle, **work all day and take stock at dusk**; test a
leap before trusting it; and know that the top has regret — the founder who cannot hand on, the
campaign that has won and will not stop, the leader past their season. The extra line imagines the
alternative: a company of capable people in which no one needs to be the head.

The Great Image turns all of it toward practice: 天行健，君子以自強不息 ("the sky moves with vigour; the noble
one, by it, makes the self strong and does not stop"). Read beside line 3, *not stopping* is not
*never resting*. The sky does not halt, but it keeps its seasons; the day's striving ends in
wariness, not in collapse.

### An echo from the Laozi

**The Wings and the Laozi share two graphs at the top of this figure, and part over the rest of
it.** Chapter 9: *"Rather than keep filling what you hold, stop"* … *"The work done, the body
withdraws. This is the way of nature."* The graphs are 盈 ("full") and 退 ("withdraw") — the same two
the Wings use for the overreaching dragon: 盈不可久也 ("fullness cannot last", the Small Image) and
知進而不知退 ("it knows advancing and not withdrawing", the Wenyan). Both are Wings. The core text has
only 亢 ("overreaching") and 悔 ("regret").

Where they part: the Laozi makes withdrawal the rule, and 乾 ("Striving") makes it the last station.
Five of its six lines say go on — appear, strive, leap, fly — and its Great Image says do not stop.
The I Ching does not ask the dragon to be less a dragon; it asks it to know where it is. The one
core-text phrase the books seem to share, 君子終日 ("the noble one, all day"), in line 3 and chapter 26,
is not leaned on here: two of the Laozi's editions read 聖人 ("the sage") in that line
([`tao-te-ching-relation.md`](../tao-te-ching-relation.md) § 4).
