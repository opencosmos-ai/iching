## The signature — a reading

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-24. This reads the figure through the Wings — the declared lens
([`README.md`](../README.md#the-lens)) — and it is a reading, not a ruling. The name still comes from
the lines ([`read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums`](../principles/read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums.md)).*

**The signature.** Within: **mountain** (艮 *gèn* · yang · stopping). Without: **water** (坎 *kǎn* · yang · sinking in).
**Across** — the yang pole of mountain ↔ lake within, the yang pole of water ↔ fire without.

**What the tradition reads.** The 彖 reads the two trigrams as a danger and a response: 蹇，難也，險在前也。
見險而能止，知矣哉 (*jiǎn, nàn yě, xiǎn zài qián yě. jiàn xiǎn ér néng zhǐ, zhì yǐ zāi*, "hobbling is hardship:
hazard lies ahead. To see the hazard and be able to stop — that is knowing!"). The 大象: 山上有水，蹇；君子以反身修德
(*shān shàng yǒu shuǐ, jiǎn; jūnzǐ yǐ fǎn shēn xiū dé*, "water on the mountain: hobbling; the noble one turns
round on their own person and cultivates integrity"). For the tradition, the hardship is real and the
answer is to stop and turn inward, not to push through.

**What the polarity implies.** Both trigrams are yang, and both are actions that do not go forward:
the mountain within holds, the water without sinks in. Each has its one firm line — its hardness — at a
different height, and each is exactly where a foot would land: at the top of the ground one stands on
(the mountain's lid), and at the centre of what lies ahead (the water's hidden core — the danger is not
surface, it has a hard middle). The figure is a steep path with a flood across it, one leg on rock and
the other in the current. That is hobbling: a going checked at every step, not a wall.

The structure suggests the way out is the mountain's own nature — to stop, and to come back — and the
lines say it five times over: 往蹇，來譽 (*wǎng jiǎn, lái yù*, "going, hobbling; coming back, praise"),
往蹇來反 (*wǎng jiǎn lái fǎn*, "going, hobbling; coming, returning"). 反身 (*fǎn shēn*, "turning round on
oneself") is the mountain's answer to the water. And stopping is what lets help arrive: at the water's
firm centre, 大蹇朋來 (*dà jiǎn péng lái*, "great hobbling — friends come"). Turned over, the water goes
below and thunder rises over it: 40, the untying. 雜卦 pairs them: 解緩也，蹇難也 (*xiè huǎn yě, jiǎn nàn yě*,
"release is slackening; hobbling is hardship").

**For the name.** **Hobbling** — the signature supports it: two stoppages, one on each side of the step,
is a hobble rather than a blockage, and a hobble is a thing that can be untied, which sets up 40.
**Candidates the signature suggests:** *Halting*, *Impasse* — *Halting* survives the lines, and carries
both limping and stopping: 往蹇，來譽 (*wǎng jiǎn, lái yù*, "going, halting; coming back, praise"), 王臣蹇蹇
(*wáng chén jiǎn jiǎn*, "the ruler's servant, halting and halting"), 大蹇朋來 (*dà jiǎn péng lái*, "great
halting — friends come"); *Impasse*, the flood across the path, fails the lines — 王臣蹇蹇 (*wáng chén jiǎn
jiǎn*) cannot be "impasse, impasse", and the lines' 來 (*lái*, "coming back") needs a movement that is
checked, not one that is barred.

## The heart of it

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-25 — an interpretive reading, not a translation. Each claim is marked by
layer: the core text (the judgment and the six lines, Western Zhou), the Wings (Warring States to
Han), Wang Bi (the 3rd-century commentator), and echoes of the Tao Te Ching in this project's own
translation. The formula words — 吉 ("auspicious"), 凶 ("ominous"), 无咎 ("no blame"), 亨 ("gets
through"), 孚 ("confidence"), 貞 ("holding firm") — carry working glosses until WORKLIST A4 and A6
rule on them ([`hexagram-names.md`](../hexagram-names.md)).*

### Essence

**Hobbling is going that is checked at every step, and the text's answer, four times over, is to
come back.** The Shuowen, the c. 100 CE character dictionary, glosses 蹇 ("hobbling") as 跛也 ("lame"):
a foot, 足 ("foot"), with a shortened 寒 ("cold") for the sound. The core text is built on one formula
— 往蹇 ("going: hobbling"), then 來 ("coming back") and what is found there — and at lines 1, 3, 4 and
6, what waits on the way back is better than what lay ahead: praise, home, company, something great.
The two centres break the pattern. Line 2 cannot come back, because the hardship is not its own;
line 5 stands where the hardship is greatest, and there others come. So the figure has two answers,
and which one is yours depends on where you stand.

### The polarities in it

Mountain within, water without. The mountain stops; the water ahead sinks in. Each has its one firm
line — the mountain's at its top, the water's at its centre — and both are the yang pole, both
actions that do not go forward (see *The signature*, above). The Tuan, the Wing on the judgment,
reads it as a sight and a response: 險在前也 ("the hazard is ahead"), and 見險而能止，知矣哉 ("to see the hazard
and be able to stop — that is knowing!"). **Structure and text agree here more closely than almost
anywhere**: the stopping within is the mountain, the hazard ahead is the water, and the lines'
coming back is the mountain doing what mountains do. Where the text goes beyond the figure is at the
two centres, which do not stop.

### In nature

山上有水 ("water on the mountain") is a path in spate. Snowmelt and spring rain turn a mountain track
into a streambed; wet rock is where the feet go; a crossing that is one step in August is thigh-deep
and fast in May. Snow-fed streams run lowest in the early morning and highest in the late afternoon,
after a day of sun on the snow — the ford that was possible at dawn is not possible by teatime.
Mountain walkers learn the word the Small Image, the Wing on each line, gives the first line: 宜待也
("it is right to wait").

A hobble is a real thing, too: a strap or rope between a horse's forelegs, so that it can graze
through the night but cannot run off. A hobbled animal still moves — slowly, in short steps, never
far. That is the difference between this figure and a wall. And ch 5 of the Shuogua, the Wing on the
trigrams, gives the judgment's directions their ground: southwest is 坤 ("earth"), and northeast is 艮
("mountain"). Wang Bi says it without the system: 西南地也，東北山也 ("southwest is level ground; northeast
is mountain"). When the path is hobbling you, go toward the level, not toward more mountain.

### The movement of the lines

**The judgment.** 蹇：利西南，不利東北；利見大人，貞吉 ("Hobbling. Advantageous to the southwest, not to the
northeast; advantageous to see the great person; holding firm, auspicious"). Go toward level ground,
and toward someone who can help.

**Line 1.** 往蹇，來譽 ("going: hobbling; coming back: praise"). The Shuowen's 譽 ("praise") is 稱也 ("being
spoken well of"). The first line sees the hazard first, and stops.

**Line 2.** 王臣蹇蹇，匪躬之故 ("the ruler's servant, hobbling and hobbling — not for their own body's
sake"). The one line with no coming back. 躬 ("the body") is the trunk of the body, the person; the
servant goes on because the hardship is someone else's. The Small Image clears them: 終无尤也 ("in the
end, no fault").

**Line 3.** 往蹇來反 ("going: hobbling; coming: turning back"). The firm top of the mountain, the line
the lower trigram leans on, turns for home — and the Small Image says 內喜之也 ("those within are glad
of it").

**Line 4.** 往蹇來連 ("going: hobbling; coming back: joined"). At the seam, one foot in the water. The
Shuowen draws 連 ("joined") 从辵从車 ("from walking and a cart") — people hauling together. On the way
back one is no longer alone.

**Line 5.** 大蹇朋來 ("great hobbling — friends come"). The firm centre of the water: the worst place,
and the only place where the coming is done by others. The traveller holds, and help arrives.

**Line 6.** 往蹇來碩，吉；利見大人 ("going: hobbling; coming back: something great — auspicious; advantageous
to see the great person"). The Shuowen's 碩 ("great") is 頭大也 ("large-headed"): big, weighty. At the
top, past the hazard, the return brings the most.

### In a life now

This is the figure for the project that meets obstacle after obstacle, a treatment that stalls, a
negotiation where every step forward costs two, the injury that will not let you run. Its counsel is
not to push through. It is: see the hazard and stop; go back to where you have footing, and people;
wait for the water to drop. Coming back is not failure in this text — it is praise, home, company,
greatness. But the text honours two other places. Some cannot turn back, because they carry someone
else's hardship — the carer, the one who stays in the post for others' sake — and the Small Image
finds no fault in them (line 2). And at the heart of the worst of it, holding still and holding the
centre, friends come (line 5).

The Great Image, the Wing on the whole figure, turns it inward: 君子以反身修德 ("the noble one turns back
on their own body and cultivates integrity"). When the road ahead hobbles you, the work that can
still be done is on yourself.

### An echo from the Laozi

**A graph shared, and a scale that differs.** Chapter 40, in Shalom's translation, opens:
*"Returning is the Tao's movement; / Yielding is the Tao's use."* The graph is 反 ("returning"), and
it stands in 39's core text at line 3: 往蹇來反 ("going: hobbling; coming: turning back"). Shalom's
*returning* answers to it; the test passes. 反 ("returning") comes again in the Great Image's 反身
("turning back on one's own body").

But the scale is not the same. In the Laozi, returning is how the Tao itself moves — everything goes
out and comes back. In 39, 反 ("turning back") is one traveller's practical choice on one bad road,
and the book does not make it a law: line 2 goes on and is blameless; line 5 stays and is helped.
The Laozi says returning is the way things go. The I Ching says: on this road, in this weather, turn
back — unless it is not yours to turn.

### Words kept

- 蹇 ("Hobbling") — *hobbling* (proposed); the Shuowen: 跛也 ("lame"); not *impasse*, which the lines'
  來 ("coming back") refuses
- 往 ("going") · 來 ("coming back") — *going · coming back* · 反 ("turning back") — *turning back*,
  *returning*
- 身 ("body") — *body* (locked): 反身 ("turning back on one's own body") is *turning back on one's own
  body*, never *the self*
- 德 ("integrity") — *integrity*, never *virtue* (locked)
- 王 ("ruler") — *ruler*, never *king* (locked) · 大人 ("the great person") — *the great person*
- 險 ("hazard") — *hazard*, never *danger*; it is the Tuan's word here, not the core's
- 貞吉 ("holding firm, auspicious") — *holding firm, auspicious*, working glosses (A4, A6)
