## The signature — a reading

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-24. This reads the figure through the Wings — the declared lens
([`README.md`](../README.md#the-lens)) — and it is a reading, not a ruling. The name still comes from
the lines ([`read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums`](../principles/read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums.md)).*

**The signature.** Within: **mountain** (艮 *gèn* · yang · stopping). Without: **water** (坎 *kǎn* · yang · sinking in).
**Across** — the yang pole of mountain ↔ lake within, the yang pole of water ↔ fire without.

**What the tradition reads.** The 彖 reads the two trigrams as a danger and a response: 蹇，難也，險在前也。
見險而能止，知矣哉 (*jiǎn, nàn yě, xiǎn zài qián yě. jiàn xiǎn ér néng zhǐ, zhì yǐ zāi*, "hobbling is hardship:
hazard lies ahead. To see the hazard and be able to stop — that is knowing!"). The 大象: 山上有水，蹇；君子以反身修德
(*shān shàng yǒu shuǐ, jiǎn; jūnzǐ yǐ fǎn shēn xiū dé*, "water on the mountain: hobbling; the noble one turns
round on their own person and cultivates integrity"). For the tradition, the hardship is real and the
answer is to stop and turn inward, not to push through.

**What the polarity implies.** Both trigrams are yang, and both are actions that do not go forward:
the mountain within holds, the water without sinks in. Each has its one firm line — its hardness — at a
different height, and each is exactly where a foot would land: at the top of the ground one stands on
(the mountain's lid), and at the centre of what lies ahead (the water's hidden core — the danger is not
surface, it has a hard middle). The figure is a steep path with a flood across it, one leg on rock and
the other in the current. That is hobbling: a going checked at every step, not a wall.

The structure suggests the way out is the mountain's own nature — to stop, and to come back — and the
lines say it five times over: 往蹇，來譽 (*wǎng jiǎn, lái yù*, "going, hobbling; coming back, praise"),
往蹇來反 (*wǎng jiǎn lái fǎn*, "going, hobbling; coming, returning"). 反身 (*fǎn shēn*, "turning round on
oneself") is the mountain's answer to the water. And stopping is what lets help arrive: at the water's
firm centre, 大蹇朋來 (*dà jiǎn péng lái*, "great hobbling — friends come"). Turned over, the water goes
below and thunder rises over it: 40, the untying. 雜卦 pairs them: 解緩也，蹇難也 (*xiè huǎn yě, jiǎn nàn yě*,
"release is slackening; hobbling is hardship").

**For the name.** **Hobbling** — the signature supports it: two stoppages, one on each side of the step,
is a hobble rather than a blockage, and a hobble is a thing that can be untied, which sets up 40.
**Candidates the signature suggests:** *Halting*, *Impasse* — *Halting* survives the lines, and carries
both limping and stopping: 往蹇，來譽 (*wǎng jiǎn, lái yù*, "going, halting; coming back, praise"), 王臣蹇蹇
(*wáng chén jiǎn jiǎn*, "the ruler's servant, halting and halting"), 大蹇朋來 (*dà jiǎn péng lái*, "great
halting — friends come"); *Impasse*, the flood across the path, fails the lines — 王臣蹇蹇 (*wáng chén jiǎn
jiǎn*) cannot be "impasse, impasse", and the lines' 來 (*lái*, "coming back") needs a movement that is
checked, not one that is barred.

## The heart of it

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-25 — an interpretive reading, not a translation. Each claim is marked by
layer: the core text (the judgment and the six lines, Western Zhou), the Wings (Warring States to
Han), 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*, 3rd century), and echoes of the Tao Te Ching in this project's own translation.
The formula words — 吉, 凶, 无咎, 亨, 孚, 貞 — carry working glosses until WORKLIST A4 and A6 rule on
them ([`hexagram-names.md`](../hexagram-names.md)).*

### Essence

**Hobbling is going that is checked at every step, and the text's answer, four times over, is to
come back.** 說文 (*shuōwén*) glosses 蹇 (*jiǎn*) as 跛也 (*bǒ yě*, "lame"): a foot, 足 (*zú*), with a
shortened 寒 (*hán*, "cold") for the sound. The core text is built on one formula — 往蹇 (*wǎng jiǎn*,
"going: hobbling"), then 來 (*lái*, "coming back") and what is found there — and at lines 1, 3, 4
and 6, what waits on the way back is better than what lay ahead: praise, home, company, something
great. The two centres break the pattern. Line 2 cannot come back, because the hardship is not its
own; line 5 stands where the hardship is greatest, and there others come. So the figure has two
answers, and which one is yours depends on where you stand.

### The polarities in it

Mountain within, water without. The mountain stops; the water ahead sinks in. Each has its one
firm line — the mountain's at its top, the water's at its centre — and both are the yang pole,
both actions that do not go forward (see *The signature*, above). The 彖 reads it as a sight and a
response: 險在前也 (*xiǎn zài qián yě*, "the hazard is ahead"), and 見險而能止，知矣哉 (*jiàn xiǎn ér
néng zhǐ, zhì yǐ zāi*, "to see the hazard and be able to stop — that is knowing!"). **Structure and
text agree here more closely than almost anywhere**: the stopping within is the mountain, the
hazard ahead is the water, and the lines' coming back is the mountain doing what mountains do.
Where the text goes beyond the figure is at the two centres, which do not stop.

### In nature

山上有水 (*shān shàng yǒu shuǐ*, "water on the mountain") is a path in spate. Snowmelt and spring rain
turn a mountain track into a streambed; wet rock is where the feet go; a crossing that is one step
in August is thigh-deep and fast in May. Snow-fed streams run lowest in the early morning and
highest in the late afternoon, after a day of sun on the snow — the ford that was possible at dawn
is not possible by teatime. Mountain walkers learn the 小象's word for the first line: 宜待也 (*yí dài
yě*, "it is right to wait").

A hobble is a real thing, too: a strap or rope between a horse's forelegs, so that it can graze
through the night but cannot run off. A hobbled animal still moves — slowly, in short steps, never
far. That is the difference between this figure and a wall. And 說卦 ch 5 gives the judgment's
directions their ground: southwest is 坤 (*kūn*), earth, and northeast is 艮 (*gèn*), the mountain.
王弼 says it without the system: 西南地也，東北山也 (*xīnán dì yě, dōngběi shān yě*, "southwest is
level ground; northeast is mountain"). When the path is hobbling you, go toward the level, not
toward more mountain.

### The movement of the lines

**The judgment.** 蹇：利西南，不利東北；利見大人，貞吉 (*jiǎn: lì xīnán, bù lì dōngběi; lì jiàn dàrén,
zhēn jí*, "Hobbling. Advantageous to the southwest, not to the northeast; advantageous to see the
great person; holding firm, auspicious"). Go toward level ground, and toward someone who can help.

**Line 1.** 往蹇，來譽 (*wǎng jiǎn, lái yù*, "going: hobbling; coming back: praise"). 說文's 譽 (*yù*) is
稱也 (*chēng yě*, "being spoken well of"). The first line sees the hazard first, and stops.

**Line 2.** 王臣蹇蹇，匪躬之故 (*wáng chén jiǎn jiǎn, fěi gōng zhī gù*, "the ruler's servant, hobbling
and hobbling — not for their own body's sake"). The one line with no coming back. 躬 (*gōng*) is
the trunk of the body, the person; the servant goes on because the hardship is someone else's. The
小象 clears them: 終无尤也 (*zhōng wú yóu yě*, "in the end, no fault").

**Line 3.** 往蹇來反 (*wǎng jiǎn lái fǎn*, "going: hobbling; coming: turning back"). The firm top of the
mountain, the line the lower trigram leans on, turns for home — and the 小象 says 內喜之也 (*nèi xǐ zhī
yě*, "those within are glad of it").

**Line 4.** 往蹇來連 (*wǎng jiǎn lái lián*, "going: hobbling; coming back: joined"). At the seam, one
foot in the water. 說文 draws 連 (*lián*) 从辵从車 (*cóng chuò cóng chē*, "from walking and a cart") —
people hauling together. On the way back one is no longer alone.

**Line 5.** 大蹇朋來 (*dà jiǎn péng lái*, "great hobbling — friends come"). The firm centre of the
water: the worst place, and the only place where the coming is done by others. The traveller holds,
and help arrives.

**Line 6.** 往蹇來碩，吉；利見大人 (*wǎng jiǎn lái shuò, jí; lì jiàn dàrén*, "going: hobbling; coming
back: something great — auspicious; advantageous to see the great person"). 說文's 碩 (*shuò*) is
頭大也 (*tóu dà yě*, "large-headed"): big, weighty. At the top, past the hazard, the return brings the
most.

### In a life now

This is the figure for the project that meets obstacle after obstacle, a treatment that stalls,
a negotiation where every step forward costs two, the injury that will not let you run. Its
counsel is not to push through. It is: see the hazard and stop; go back to where you have footing,
and people; wait for the water to drop. Coming back is not failure in this text — it is praise,
home, company, greatness. But the text honours two other places. Some cannot turn back, because
they carry someone else's hardship — the carer, the one who stays in the post for others' sake —
and the 小象 finds no fault in them (line 2). And at the heart of the worst of it, holding still and
holding the centre, friends come (line 5).

The 大象 turns it inward: 君子以反身修德 (*jūnzǐ yǐ fǎn shēn xiū dé*, "the noble one turns back on
their own body and cultivates integrity"). When the road ahead hobbles you, the work that can still
be done is on yourself.

### An echo from the Laozi

**A graph shared, and a scale that differs.** Chapter 40, in Shalom's translation, opens:
*"Returning is the Tao's movement; / Yielding is the Tao's use."* The graph is 反 (*fǎn*,
"returning"), and it stands in 39's core text at line 3: 往蹇來反 (*wǎng jiǎn lái fǎn*, "going:
hobbling; coming: turning back"). Shalom's *returning* answers to it; the test passes. 反 comes again
in the 大象's 反身 (*fǎn shēn*, "turning back on one's own body").

But the scale is not the same. In the Laozi, returning is how the Tao itself moves — everything
goes out and comes back. In 39, 反 is one traveller's practical choice on one bad road, and the book
does not make it a law: line 2 goes on and is blameless; line 5 stays and is helped. The Laozi says
returning is the way things go. The I Ching says: on this road, in this weather, turn back —
unless it is not yours to turn.

### Words kept

- 蹇 (*jiǎn*) — *hobbling* (proposed); 說文 跛也 (*bǒ yě*, "lame"); not *impasse*, which the lines'
  來 (*lái*, "coming back") refuses
- 往 · 來 (*wǎng · lái*) — *going · coming back* · 反 (*fǎn*) — *turning back*, *returning*
- 身 (*shēn*) — *body* (locked): 反身 (*fǎn shēn*) is *turning back on one's own body*, never *the self*
- 德 (*dé*) — *integrity*, never *virtue* (locked)
- 王 (*wáng*) — *ruler*, never *king* (locked) · 大人 (*dàrén*) — *the great person*
- 險 (*xiǎn*) — *hazard*, never *danger*; it is the 彖's word here, not the core's
- 貞吉 (*zhēn jí*) — *holding firm, auspicious*, working glosses (A4, A6)
