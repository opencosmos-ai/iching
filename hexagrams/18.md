## The signature — a reading

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-24. This reads the figure through the Wings — the declared lens
([`README.md`](../README.md#the-lens)) — and it is a reading, not a ruling. The name still comes from
the lines ([`read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums`](../principles/read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums.md)).*

**The signature.** Within: **wind** (巽 · yin · entering). Without: **mountain** (艮 · yang · stopping).
**Across** — thunder ↔ wind × mountain ↔ lake.

**What the tradition reads.** The 彖傳: 剛上而柔下，巽而止，蠱 (*gāng shàng ér róu xià, xùn ér zhǐ,
gǔ*), "the firm goes up and the yielding goes down; entering, then held: rot." It then turns the rot
into a hinge: 終則有始，天行也 (*zhōng zé yǒu shǐ, tiān xíng yě*), "an end, and then there is a
beginning: that is the sky's going." The 大象傳: 山下有風 (*shān xià yǒu fēng*), "below the mountain
there is wind," and 君子以振民育德 (*jūnzǐ yǐ zhèn mín yù dé*), "the noble one shakes the people and
nurtures integrity." The tradition treats rot as work to be taken up (序卦: 蠱者事也, *gǔ zhě shì yě*,
"蠱 is affairs"), and taking it up closes one cycle and opens the next.

**What the polarity implies.** 18 is 17 turned over. It is also 17 with every line changed, which
is true of only four King Wen pairs (11/12, 17/18, 53/54, 63/64). The figure `011001` is open at the
base and closed at the lid, the exact reverse of 17's firm-foot-below, open-mouth-above. It is a
sealed jar with a seep in its floor: what gets in from below is shut in by what holds above.
說文's 皿蟲 (*mǐn chóng*, "worms in a vessel") is the figure drawn. Wind at the foot of a mountain has
nowhere to blow, and air that enters and is held goes stale. 說卦 ch 11 has 巽為臭 (*xùn wéi chòu*,
"wind is odour"). The firm has gone up and stayed up (剛上), so the top holds, the bottom seeps, and
nothing passes between them. That is how an inheritance rots: the father's order sealed over, the
son's life seeping in underneath. The structure suggests the cure uses the same action as the
fault. Entering is turned from seeping into reaching, and 振 (*zhèn*, "shaking") is wind's work done
on purpose. The lid is more than a seal, too. 說卦 ch 6: 終萬物、始萬物者，莫盛乎艮 (*zhōng wànwù,
shǐ wànwù zhě, mò shèng hū gèn*), "for ending the countless things and beginning them, nothing is
fuller than 艮." The mountain is where 終則有始 happens.

**For the name.** **Rot**. The signature deepens it. Rot is what happens to a closed thing that
something has got into, and the figure draws the jar and its seep. It also supports keeping a
physical word over an abstract one. **Candidates the signature suggests:** *Festering* survives the
lines (幹父之蠱, *gàn fù zhī gǔ*, "set right the father's festering"; 裕父之蠱, *yù fù zhī gǔ*,
"indulge the father's festering"), but it is a process where the lines want a thing one can set
right. *The Sealed Jar* fails the lines: "set right the father's sealed jar" loses the spoiling, and
the jar is the signature's image, not the text's.

## The heart of it

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-25 — an interpretive reading, not a translation. Each claim is marked by
layer: the core text (the judgment and the six lines, Western Zhou), the Wings (Warring States to
Han), 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*, 3rd century), and echoes of the Tao Te Ching in this project's own translation.
The formula words — 吉, 凶, 无咎, 亨, 孚, 貞 — carry working glosses until WORKLIST A4 and A6 rule on
them ([`hexagram-names.md`](../hexagram-names.md)).*

### Essence

**Something handed down has spoiled in the keeping, and it falls to the one who inherits it to set
it right.** 說文 (*shuōwén*) gives 蠱 (*gǔ*) as 腹中蟲也 (*fù zhōng chóng yě*, "worms in the belly"),
built from 蟲 (*chóng*, "worm, grub") and 皿 (*mǐn*, "vessel"), and quotes an older saying, 皿蟲爲蠱
(*mǐn chóng wéi gǔ*, "worms in the vessel make 蠱"). In the core text five of the six lines name a
parent's rot — 父之蠱 (*fù zhī gǔ*, "the father's rot") four times, 母之蠱 (*mǔ zhī gǔ*, "the mother's
rot") once. The rot is never the child's own. The work is. And the judgment, surprisingly, is
favourable: rot **(proposed)** is not the end of the story but the start of the next task.

### The polarities in it

Within is wind, open at its base: it enters, it gets in from below. Without is the mountain, closed
at its top: it stops, it holds the lid down. What seeps in underneath is shut in by what stops
above, and air that gets in and cannot get out goes stale — the jar and its worms, drawn in lines.
This figure is the previous one with every line changed: where 17 was force below and an opening
above, 18 is an opening below and a seal above. The 彖 reads the cure out of the same pair —
巽而止 (*xùn ér zhǐ*, "entering, and held") — and the 大象 turns the wind's entering to purpose.
**The figure draws the vessel; only the lines put a family in it.** Nothing in wind and mountain
says *father* or *mother*; the text does.

### In nature

The 大象's image is 山下有風 (*shān xià yǒu fēng*, "below the mountain there is wind"). On a still
night, cold air slides down a mountain's flanks and settles in the hollows at its foot; the valley
floor fills with damp, unmoving air, and that is where frost and mist gather first. Rot wants
exactly that: damp, still and closed. A grain store spoils where air does not move, which is why
granaries are raised and vented. The worms in the vessel are real too — weevils lay their eggs
inside single grains, and the grub eats from within, unseen, until the kernel is a shell.

Rot has a second half, and the 彖 names it: 終則有始 (*zhōng zé yǒu shǐ*, "an end, and then there is
a beginning"). In an old wood a fallen trunk is opened by fungi and beetle grubs, softens over years,
and seedlings root along its length. Turn a compost heap that has gone sour and airless, and within
days the smell changes: the same breakdown, with air let in, becomes soil. That is the 大象's verb.
振 (*zhèn*) is in 說文 舉救也 (*jǔ jiù yě*, "to lift up and rescue"), and 一曰奮也 (*yī yuē fèn yě*,
"also: to rouse").

### The movement of the lines

**The judgment.** 蠱：元亨。利涉大川。先甲三日，後甲三日 (*gǔ: yuán hēng. lì shè dà chuān. xiān jiǎ
sān rì, hòu jiǎ sān rì*) — "Rot: from the source, it gets through. It is advantageous to cross the
great river. Three days before 甲 (*jiǎ*), three days after." 甲 is the first day of the ten-day
count, and 說文 draws it as 木戴孚甲之象 (*mù dài fú jiǎ zhī xiàng*, "a tree still wearing its seed
husk"). 王弼 reads 甲 as the day a new order is issued, 甲者創制之令也 (*jiǎ zhě chuàng zhì zhī lìng yě*,
"甲 is the command that founds something new"), announced three days ahead and followed up three days
after. The repair is a crossing, and it is timed.

**Line 1** takes it up first. 幹父之蠱，有子考，无咎，厲終吉 (*gàn fù zhī gǔ, yǒu zǐ kǎo, wú jiù, lì
zhōng jí*, "setting right the father's rot: there is a son, and the late father is without blame;
peril, and in the end auspicious"). 考 (*kǎo*) is 說文's 老也 (*lǎo yě*, "old"); in use, the father
who has died. The child's work clears the parent's name. 幹 (*gàn*) is not in the vendored 說文; as
the graph stands it is a trunk, the main timber — to 幹 a thing is to take its weight and hold it
straight.

**Line 2**, firm and central, is told to bend. 幹母之蠱，不可貞 (*gàn mǔ zhī gǔ, bù kě zhēn*, "setting
right the mother's rot: you cannot hold firm"). 王弼 says the firm line here must bend its own
firmness. Some rot is not mended by being hard about it.

**Line 3**, firm in a firm place at the seam, is a little too hard. 幹父之蠱，小有悔，无大咎 (*gàn fù
zhī gǔ, xiǎo yǒu huǐ, wú dà jiù*, "setting right the father's rot: a little regret, no great blame").
Done roughly, but done.

**Line 4** is the one line that does not set it right. 裕父之蠱，往見吝 (*yù fù zhī gǔ, wǎng jiàn lìn*,
"letting the father's rot be; going on, you meet stinting"). 說文 gives 裕 (*yù*) as 衣物饒也 (*yī wù
ráo yě*, "clothing cut ample") — slack, roomy, left loose. Indulgence is the only real fault in the
figure.

**Line 5**, the soft centre in the place of honour, mends by honouring. 幹父之蠱，用譽 (*gàn fù zhī
gǔ, yòng yù*, "setting right the father's rot, with praise"). 譽 (*yù*) is 說文's 稱也 (*chēng yě*, "to
speak well of"). The 小象: 承以德也 (*chéng yǐ dé yě*, "it carries on with integrity"). The rot is
cleared without the father being shamed.

**Line 6** is the only line without rot in it. 不事王侯，高尚其事 (*bù shì wáng hóu, gāo shàng qí
shì*, "not serving rulers and princes, holding one's own affairs high"). Above the family, someone
steps out of service altogether; the 小象 says 志可則也 (*zhì kě zé yě*, "the will can be a pattern").

Nothing in this hexagram is ominous. Its weights are peril at 1, a little regret at 3, and stinting
at 4 — the line that left the rot alone.

### In a life now

This is the figure for clearing a dead parent's affairs; for the family firm that turns out to be in
debt; for the drinking no one named; for damp inside the walls of an old house; for an institution
you join and find a buried wrong in; for a country's past. The text does not ask whose fault it was.
It says the rot is real, the task is yours, and it can get through. Its counsel is in the manner:
take up the weight (line 1); do not meet a mother's rot with rigidity (line 2); accept some regret
for being rough (line 3); do not let it be (line 4); and where you can, mend it while speaking well
of the one who left it (line 5). Sometimes the answer is to walk out of the system altogether
(line 6). And prepare before, and look again after: three days, and three days.

The 大象 turns this toward the long work: 君子以振民育德 (*jūnzǐ yǐ zhèn mín yù dé*, "the noble one
lifts the people and rears integrity"). 育 (*yù*) is in 說文 養子使作善也 (*yǎng zǐ shǐ zuò shàn yě*,
"raising a child so that it does well"). Integrity is raised the way a child is — which is how the
next rot is prevented.

### An echo from the Laozi

**The two books share a verb and part over the timing.** The 彖 says of this hexagram 蠱，元亨，而天下治也
(*gǔ, yuán hēng, ér tiānxià zhì yě*, "rot gets through from the source, and the world is governed").
Chapter 64 has the same 治 (*zhì*): *"Order it before it falls into disorder."* The graph is shared,
but only in the Wings layer — the core text does not use 治.

And they part exactly here. The Laozi's counsel is to act before: *"Be as careful at the end as at the
beginning, and nothing will be ruined."* 蠱 begins after. The rot is already in the vessel, left by
someone else, and the 彖 does not ask for an end held as carefully as a beginning; it lets the end
become one — 終則有始 (*zhōng zé yǒu shǐ*, "an end, and then there is a beginning"). **One book
prevents; the other repairs.** This reading keeps both, and does not make them the same advice.

### Words kept

- 幹 (*gàn*) — *set right*, working gloss; not in the vendored 說文, argued from the graph as it stands (a trunk that takes the weight)
- 裕 (*yù*) — *let be, leave slack*; the one line of indulgence
- 事 (*shì*) — *serve* (verb) and *affairs* (noun), both in 18.6 (locked); 序卦 also has 蠱者事也 (*gǔ zhě shì yě*, "蠱 is affairs")
- 德 (*dé*) — *integrity*, never *virtue* (locked)
