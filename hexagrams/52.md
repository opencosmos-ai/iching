## The signature — a reading

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-24. This reads the figure through the Wings — the declared lens
([`README.md`](../README.md#the-lens)) — and it is a reading, not a ruling. The name still comes from
the lines ([`read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums`](../principles/read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums.md)).*

**The signature.** Within: **mountain** (艮 · yang · stopping). Without: **mountain** (艮 · yang · stopping).
**Doubled** — the yang pole of mountain ↔ lake, twice.

**What the tradition reads.** The 彖傳 glosses the figure in one word, 艮，止也 (*gèn, zhǐ yě*, "*gèn* is
stopping"), and then refuses to let stopping be the whole of it: 時止則止，時行則行 (*shí zhǐ zé zhǐ, shí xíng
zé xíng*, "when it is time to stop, stop; when it is time to go, go"). It reads the doubling as two equals
that do not join: 上下敵應，不相與也 (*shàng xià dí yìng, bù xiāng yǔ yě*, "above and below answer as
equals and do not join"). The 大象傳 has 兼山 (*jiān shān*, "mountain joined to mountain"), and from it
君子以思不出其位 (*jūnzǐ yǐ sī bù chū qí wèi*, "the noble one's thought does not go beyond its place").

**What the polarity implies.** This is 51 inverted in every sense: one firm line again, but at the top of
each trigram, a lid over two open lines rather than a drive beneath them. The lines climb a body — toes,
calves, waist, trunk, jaws — so the two lids fall at the waist and at the end. The lower lid is the one line
that hurts. 艮其限，列其夤 (*gèn qí xiàn, liè qí yín*, "stilling his waist, tearing the flesh along his
spine"), with 厲薰心 (*lì xūn xīn*, "peril: the heart smoulders"). The upper lid is the one line wholly
good: 敦艮，吉 (*dūn gèn, jí*, "stilling made solid: good fortune"). The structure suggests the difference.
A stop imposed in the middle of a body splits it at the joint; a stop that comes at the top is simply where
the body ends. Read with 51 it is exact: thunder's first strike is good and its second bogs down; the
mountain's first closure is dangerous and its second is good fortune. 雜卦 (*záguà*, "the Mixed Figures") pairs them in two phrases,
震起也，艮止也 (*zhèn qǐ yě, gèn zhǐ yě*, "*zhèn* is rising; *gèn* is stopping"). But the lines show each pole
comes right at its own end of the figure.

**For the name.** **Stilling** — the signature deepens it: the lid is laid on a moving part, toes and jaw,
not on a thing already still, and *stilling* keeps that act. It must stay clear of *stopping*, which is
this project's reading of 止 (*zhǐ*) and not available as a name. **Candidates the signature suggests:** *Keeping
Still* — survives: 艮其趾 (*gèn qí zhǐ*, "keeping his toes still"), 艮其輔，言有序 (*gèn qí fǔ, yán yǒu xù*,
"keeping his jaws still: his words come in order"); *Summit* — fails the lines: 艮其趾 puts the stilling at
the feet, and only the top line is at the top.

## The heart of it

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-25 — an interpretive reading, not a translation. Each claim is marked by
layer: the core text (the judgment and the six lines, Western Zhou), the Wings (Warring States to
Han), 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*, 3rd century), and echoes of the Tao Te Ching in this project's own translation.
The formula words — 吉, 凶, 无咎, 亨, 孚, 貞 — carry working glosses until WORKLIST A4 and A6 rule on
them ([`hexagram-names.md`](../hexagram-names.md)).*

### Essence

**Stillness laid on a moving body from the feet up: it tears where it is forced at the middle, and
holds when it comes to rest at the top.** The core text climbs a body — toes, calves, waist, trunk,
jaws — and at each place lays 艮 (*gèn*) on it. It is always something that moves being made still,
never a thing already at rest. 說文 (*shuōwén*) reads the graph as an eye set against another, 目相匕，
不相下也 (*mù xiāng bǐ, bù xiāng xià yě*, "eyes set against each other, neither giving way"), and
the judgment begins where no eye can see: 艮其背 (*gèn qí bèi*, "stilling the back").

### The polarities in it

Mountain within, mountain without: one pole, twice. Each trigram is a firm line laid over two open
ones — a lid, closed at the top edge (see *The signature*, above). Doubled, the lids fall at line 3
and line 6, and the text treats them as opposites. At 3, the lid comes down in the middle of the
body, and the body splits at the joint; it is the one line of peril. At 6, the lid is where the body
ends, and it is the one line called auspicious. **A stop in the middle of a thing divides it; a stop
at its end completes it.** The 彖 (*tuàn*) reads the doubling as two that do not join: 上下敵應，不相與也
(*shàng xià dí yìng, bù xiāng yǔ yě*, "above and below answer as equals, and do not join").

### In nature

The 大象 (*dà xiàng*) gives 兼山 (*jiān shān*, "mountain joined to mountain"): stand on a height and look
out over a range, ridge behind ridge, each paler than the one before, none going anywhere. A mountain
is where things stop — weather banks against its windward side and drops its rain there; travel
halts at the pass; one valley's people end where the next ridge begins. 說卦 (*shuōguà*) ch 5 sets
艮 (*gèn*) in the north-east, where one cycle ends and the next begins: 萬物之所成，終而所成始也 (*wàn wù zhī suǒ
chéng, zhōng ér suǒ chéng shǐ yě*, "where the countless things are completed: an end, and what
begins them again").

But the lines are a body, and the body is where to look. Watch a heron fishing: the stillness starts
at the feet, and the last thing to move is the head. Watch a deer that has heard something — it stops
mid-step, one foot raised, and everything goes still from the ground up. The first line has the graph
for it: 趾 (*zhǐ*, "toes") is built on 止 (*zhǐ*), which 說文 gives as 下基也 (*xià jī yě*, "the base
below"), a plant's footing, 故以止爲足 (*gù yǐ zhǐ wéi zú*, "so 止 serves for 'foot'"). The word this
project reads as *stopping* began as a foot.

And the third line is anatomy anyone can check. Hold yourself rigid at the waist — braced over a
bench, stooped in a field, sitting still for hours by a sickbed — and the pain arrives in the long
muscles either side of the spine. 列其夤 (*liè qí yín*, "tearing the flesh along the spine"). 說文 gives
限 (*xiàn*, "the waist") as 阻也 (*zǔ yě*, "a barrier"), and as a door-sill: the threshold, the point
where one part of the body meets the other.

### The movement of the lines

**The judgment.** 艮：艮其背，不獲其身，行其庭，不見其人，无咎 (*gèn: gèn qí bèi, bù huò qí shēn, xíng
qí tíng, bù jiàn qí rén, wú jiù*) — "Stilling *(proposed)*: stilling the back; the body is not
seized; walking in the courtyard, not seeing the person there — no blame." 王弼 says why the back:
背者无見之物也 (*bèi zhě wú jiàn zhī wù yě*, "the back is the part that does not see"). Stillness laid
where there is nothing to look at comes of itself; laid on the face, it has to be forced.

**Line 1** stops before the first step. 艮其趾，无咎，利永貞 (*gèn qí zhǐ, wú jiù, lì yǒng zhēn*, "stilling
the toes — no blame; advantage in holding firm for long").

**Line 2** is stilled while what it follows moves on. 艮其腓，不拯其隨，其心不快 (*gèn qí féi, bù zhěng qí
suí, qí xīn bù kuài*, "stilling the calves; not lifting what they follow; the heart is not glad").
說文 gives 快 (*kuài*) as 喜也 (*xǐ yě*, "glad"). A part held still against the rest of the body does
not settle; it is only unhappy.

**Line 3**, the lid at the seam, is the one line of peril. 艮其限，列其夤，厲薰心 (*gèn qí xiàn, liè qí yín,
lì xūn xīn*, "stilling the waist, tearing the flesh along the spine — peril: smoke at the heart").
王弼 says it plainly: 止加其身中體而分 (*zhǐ jiā qí shēn zhōng tǐ ér fēn*, "stopping laid on the
middle of the body, and the body divides").

**Line 4** stills the body as a whole, and that is safe. 艮其身，无咎 (*gèn qí shēn, wú jiù*, "stilling
the body — no blame"). 王弼 takes 身 (*shēn*, "body") here as the trunk, above the waist; 小象 (*xiǎo
xiàng*) says 止諸躬也 (*zhǐ zhū gōng yě*, "stopping it in one's own body").

**Line 5**, the centre above, stills the mouth. 艮其輔，言有序，悔亡 (*gèn qí fǔ, yán yǒu xù, huǐ wáng*,
"stilling the jaws; words come in order — regret goes"). 說文 gives 輔 (*fǔ*) as 人頰車也 (*rén jiá
chē yě*, "the jaw"). Still jaws do not mean silence; they mean speech in its order.

**Line 6** is the top, and the stillness is whole. 敦艮，吉 (*dūn gèn, jí*, "stilling made solid —
auspicious"). 小象: 以厚終也 (*yǐ hòu zhōng yě*, "it ends thick"). The peril at 3 and the good fortune
at 6 are the same act at two heights.

### In a life now

This is the figure for every time a person has to stop: to stop drinking, to stop working when the
body says so, to stop an argument mid-sentence, to stop a project that is going wrong, to sit with
someone who is dying and do nothing else. It counsels where to lay the stillness. **Early is easy**
— stop at the toes, before the first step (line 1). **Partial is miserable** — a calf held still while
the rest of the body goes on only aches (line 2). **Forced at the middle is dangerous** — clamp down on
the joint that joins two halves of a life, and it tears (line 3). **Whole is safe** (line 4). And
**the mouth is where stillness shows most**: a person who has come to rest speaks in order (line 5).

The 大象 turns it to practice: 君子以思不出其位 (*jūnzǐ yǐ sī bù chū qí wèi*, "the noble one's thought
does not go beyond its place"). Stopping here is not being empty. It is being wholly where you are.

### An echo from the Laozi

**A shared word in the Wings, not the core; and the books part at the waist.** The 彖 glosses the
hexagram 艮，止也 (*gèn, zhǐ yě*, "*gèn* is stopping"), and 止 (*zhǐ*, "stopping") is the Laozi's word
in chapters 32 and 44: *"Know when to stop, and you come to no danger."* The two books share the
insistence that knowing where to stop is what keeps a life out of harm. But the core text's verb is
艮, not 止, so this echo belongs to the Wings' layer. And they part twice. The Laozi's stopping is
about enough — names, goods, reputation; 52's is bodily, and its third line says that stopping in the
wrong place is itself the peril, which the Laozi never says. And the 彖 will not take stillness's
side: 時止則止，時行則行 (*shí zhǐ zé zhǐ, shí xíng zé xíng*, "when it is time to stop, stop; when it is
time to go, go"). Where the Laozi's chapter 26 makes stillness *"the ruler of the agitated"*, this
book gives neither the rule over the other; it gives both to time.

### Words kept

- 艮 (*gèn*) — *Stilling*, the proposed name; *mountain* is the trigram's image, not this hexagram's word
- 止 (*zhǐ*) — *stopping* (this project's settled word), in the Wings only; kept off the name
- 身 (*shēn*) — *body*, never *self* or *soul* (locked); 躬 (*gōng*) *one's own body*
- 心 (*xīn*) — *heart*, never *mind* (locked)
- 靜 (*jìng*) — *stillness*, in the 彖's 動靜不失其時 (*dòng jìng bù shī qí shí*, "moving and stillness each keep their time")
- 道 (*dào*) — *the Tao* in the 彖's 其道光明 (*qí dào guāngmíng*, "its Tao is bright and clear"); the core text has no 道 here
- 萬物 (*wàn wù*) — *the countless things* (locked)
- 吉 · 无咎 · 悔亡 · 厲 · 貞 (*jí · wú jiù · huǐ wáng · lì · zhēn*) — *auspicious · no blame · regret goes · peril · holding firm*, working glosses (A4, A6)
