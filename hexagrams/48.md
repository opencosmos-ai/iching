## The signature — a reading

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-24. This reads the figure through the Wings — the declared lens
([`README.md`](../README.md#the-lens)) — and it is a reading, not a ruling. The name still comes from
the lines ([`read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums`](../principles/read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums.md)).*

**The signature.** Within: **wind** (巽 · yin · entering). Without: **water** (坎 · yang · sinking in).
**Across** — thunder ↔ wind × water ↔ fire: open at the base within, firm at the centre without.

**What the tradition reads.** The 彖傳: 巽乎水而上水，井；井養而不窮也 (*xùn hū shuǐ ér shàng shuǐ,
jǐng; jǐng yǎng ér bù qióng yě*, "entering into the water and bringing the water up: the well; the
well nourishes and is not used up"); 改邑不改井，乃以剛中也 (*gǎi yì bù gǎi jǐng, nǎi yǐ gāng zhōng
yě*, "the town changes, the well does not — because the firm is at the centre"). The 大象傳: 木上有水，
井；君子以勞民勸相 (*mù shàng yǒu shuǐ, jǐng; jūnzǐ yǐ láo mín quàn xiàng*, "above the wood there is
water: the well; the noble one labours for the people and urges them to help one another"). The
tradition reads the well as the one fixed thing a settlement stands on, which feeds everyone and
belongs to no one.

**What the polarity implies.** Water left to itself sinks in; that is its action. A well is the
arrangement that makes sinking water come up, and the figure draws the moment it happens: wind, open
at its base, has gone into the water — 巽乎水 (*xùn hū shuǐ*, "entering into the water") — and the
water now rides on the wood, the full bucket on its way up. The odd lines sit at the base within and
at the centre without: the entering starts at the bottom of the self, and what it reaches is the firm
core in the middle of the world — the fifth line, the spring. That is 乃以剛中也 (*nǎi yǐ gāng zhōng
yě*, "because the firm is at the centre") read off the figure: the town can move because what it
draws on cannot.

Yin within, yang without: the self is the empty shaft, and the substance lies outside it. The core
text climbs that shaft line by line — 井泥 (*jǐng ní*, "the well is mud") at the bottom, then 井谷
(*jǐng gǔ*, "the well's hollow"), 井渫 (*jǐng xiè*, "the well is cleaned"), 井甃 (*jǐng zhòu*, "the well
is lined"), and at the firm fifth line, where water has its core, 井冽，寒泉食 (*jǐng liè, hán quán
shí*, "the well is clear; the cold spring is drunk"). 47 is this figure upside down: there the water
had sunk beneath an empty lake and no one went down for it; here something does.

**For the name.** *The Well* — the signature supports it without strain: it describes a device, a
shaft that makes sinking water rise, and the lines name the well eleven times. **Candidates the
signature suggests:** *The Shaft*, *The Spring* — *The Shaft* survives, just: 改邑不改井 (*gǎi yì bù
gǎi jǐng*, "change the town, not the shaft") and 井甃 (*jǐng zhòu*, "the shaft is lined") read, but 井冽
(*jǐng liè*, "the shaft is clear") loses the water; *The Spring* — the firm core — fails the lines:
寒泉 (*hán quán*, "the cold spring") in the fifth line is inside the well, so the spring cannot name
the whole.

## The heart of it

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-25 — an interpretive reading, not a translation. Each claim is marked by
layer: the core text (the judgment and the six lines, Western Zhou), the Wings (Warring States to
Han), Wang Bi (the 3rd-century commentator), and echoes of the Tao Te Ching in this project's own
translation. The formula words — 吉 ("auspicious"), 凶 ("ominous"), 无咎 ("no blame"), 亨 ("gets
through"), 孚 ("confidence"), 貞 ("holding firm") — carry working glosses until WORKLIST A4 and A6
rule on them ([`hexagram-names.md`](../hexagram-names.md)).*

### Essence

**The town can move and the well cannot — and a well is worth only what is drawn from it.** The
Shuowen, the c. 100 CE character dictionary, gives 井 ("well") as 八家一井 ("eight households, one
well"), and says the graph 象構韓形 ("pictures the built frame of the curb") — the square of timbers
laid round a well's mouth, seen from above. Shared, fixed, and made. The core text goes down the
shaft and back up it: mud at the bottom, a leaking hollow, a well cleaned and not used, a lining,
clear cold water, and at the top a mouth left open. Good fortune rises with it. And the judgment's
one ominous moment is not about the water at all. It is the jar, broken just short of the top.

### The polarities in it

Wind within, water without: entering at the base below, a firm core above (see *The signature*,
above). Water left to itself goes down; that is its action. A well is the made thing that brings it
up, and the Tuan, the Wing on the judgment, reads the figure as that moment: 巽乎水而上水 ("entering into
the water and bringing the water up"). The firm line at the centre of the water is the spring, and
it is why the well stays when all else moves — 改邑不改井，乃以剛中也 ("the town changes, the well does not —
because the firm is at the centre"). The lines climb the shaft just as the figure is stacked. **But
the structure only shows that drawing is possible; the text insists it may not happen.** The broken
jar, the leaking pot, the clean well no one uses — the failures are all on the human side, in the
rope and the vessel and the will to draw. No trigram says that. The text does.

### In nature

The Great Image, the Wing on the whole figure: 木上有水 ("above the wood there is water"). The Shuogua,
the Wing on the trigrams, gives the trigram within as wood as well as wind: 巽為木 ("the wind trigram
is wood"). The plainest picture is the wooden bucket coming up full. Lean over a working well on a
hot day and feel the air: groundwater stays close to the year's average warmth, so a well is cold in
summer and mild in winter. 寒泉 ("the cold spring") is the summer truth of every deep well. Drawing
pulls fresh water in through the ground from all round; a well left undrawn goes the other way —
leaves fall in, silt settles, the bottom turns to mud.

The judgment's 汔 ("nearly") is in the Shuowen 水涸也 ("water running dry") — a low well, the rope paid
out far. The lining of line 4, 甃 ("lining"), is 井壁也 ("the wall of a well"): brick or fired tile set
round the shaft so the earth does not fall in. Everything named here is something a village had to
make and keep.

### The movement of the lines

**The judgment.** 井：改邑不改井，无喪无得，往來井井。汔至亦未繘井。羸其瓶，凶 ("The well: the town changes, the well does not.
Nothing lost, nothing gained; coming and going, drawing and drawing. Nearly there, and not yet roped
up out of the well: the jar is broken — ominous."). 井井 ("well upon well") is the name doubled:
everyone who comes, drawing in turn. 羸 ("worn, broken") is in the Shuowen 瘦也 ("lean") — a jar worn
thin that fails at the last.

**Line 1** is the bottom. 井泥不食，舊井无禽 ("the well is mud and not drunk from; an old well, no creatures
come"). 禽 ("creatures") is in the Shuowen 走獸緫名 ("a general name for animals"), wider than birds:
nothing living comes to it.

**Line 2** leaks. 井谷射鮒，瓮敝漏 ("the well's hollow shoots at the minnows; the jar is worn out and
leaks"). 鮒 ("minnows") is a small carp, at home in still water. Wang Bi reads the line as water
going the wrong way: 水不上出而反下注 ("the water does not come up and out, but pours back down"). A firm
centre within, and nothing above answers it; what it has serves the small things at the bottom.

**Line 3** is ready and unused. 井渫不食，為我心惻，可用汲，王明，并受其福 ("the well is cleaned and not drunk from: it
makes my heart ache. It could be drawn from. If the ruler sees clearly, all together receive its
blessing"). 惻 ("ache") is in the Shuowen 痛也 ("pain"), and 汲 ("draw") 引水於井也 ("to draw water from a
well"). The only line that says 我 ("I, my"). At the top of the lower trigram, the seam: clean water,
and no one reaching down.

**Line 4** is the repair. 井甃，无咎 ("the well is lined: no blame"). Work that gives no water itself —
and is blameless. The Small Image, the Wing on each line: 修井也 ("mending the well").

**Line 5** is the spring. 井冽，寒泉食 ("the well is clear; the cold spring is drunk from"). The firm
centre of the water, and at last someone drinks.

**Line 6** is the open mouth. 井收勿幕，有孚元吉 ("the well is drawn up; do not cover it. There is
confidence: from the source, auspicious"). 幕 ("cover") is in the Shuowen 覆食案亦曰幕 ("a cloth over a
food table is also called a cover"): do not cover the well as one covers a meal kept back. The
highest fortune in the figure falls on leaving it open. Wang Bi: 不擅其有，不私其利 ("not claiming what it
has, not keeping its advantage to itself").

### In a life now

The village well has its descendants: the water supply, a public library, a hospital, a good
teacher, a parent's steady house, a skill kept up for years — the shared source a community stands
on while the town changes round it. The counsel is about keeping and drawing. Clean it (line 3);
line it (line 4); do not let it silt up (line 1); do not cover it for yourself (line 6). Its grief
is line 3: water clean and ready and no one drawing — talent unused, a service no one funds — and
its remedy is 王明 ("the ruler sees clearly"), the ones who decide seeing what is there. Its warning
is the judgment's: most of the labour of bringing water up can be lost at the lip, when the jar
breaks — care at the end. The Great Image makes the well a practice: 君子以勞民勸相 ("the noble one labours
for the people and urges them to help one another"). A well is dug and kept by many hands.

### An echo from the Laozi

**The two books share a graph for what does not run out, and part over whether a source needs
keeping.** The Laozi's chapter 45: *"Great fullness seems empty, yet when used, it never runs dry."*
Its *never runs dry* is 不窮 ("not used up"), the words of the Tuan's 井養而不窮也 ("the well nourishes and
is not used up"). The echo passes the two-question test at the Wing layer; the core text's nearest
words, 无喪无得 ("nothing lost, nothing gained"), meet it only thematically. (Chapter 64's *"People
often ruin their affairs just short of completing them"* is the broken jar in spirit — thematic
only; the graphs differ.)

Both books know a source that gives and is not lessened. **But the Laozi's fullness needs nothing;
the well needs everything.** Rope, jar, lining, cleaning, someone to draw, a ruler who sees — the
core text spends most of its lines on the keeping. The Laozi's paradox is that fullness looks empty;
the well's plain fact is that water in the ground does no one any good until it is brought up.
