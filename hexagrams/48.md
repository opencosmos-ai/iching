## The signature — a reading

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-24. This reads the figure through the Wings — the declared lens
([`README.md`](../README.md#the-lens)) — and it is a reading, not a ruling. The name still comes from
the lines ([`read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums`](../principles/read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums.md)).*

**The signature.** Within: **wind** (巽 · yin · entering). Without: **water** (坎 · yang · sinking in).
**Across** — thunder ↔ wind × water ↔ fire: open at the base within, firm at the centre without.

**What the tradition reads.** The 彖傳: 巽乎水而上水，井；井養而不窮也 (*xùn hū shuǐ ér shàng shuǐ,
jǐng; jǐng yǎng ér bù qióng yě*, "entering into the water and bringing the water up: the well; the
well nourishes and is not used up"); 改邑不改井，乃以剛中也 (*gǎi yì bù gǎi jǐng, nǎi yǐ gāng zhōng
yě*, "the town changes, the well does not — because the firm is at the centre"). The 大象傳: 木上有水，
井；君子以勞民勸相 (*mù shàng yǒu shuǐ, jǐng; jūnzǐ yǐ láo mín quàn xiàng*, "above the wood there is
water: the well; the noble one labours for the people and urges them to help one another"). The
tradition reads the well as the one fixed thing a settlement stands on, which feeds everyone and
belongs to no one.

**What the polarity implies.** Water left to itself sinks in; that is its action. A well is the
arrangement that makes sinking water come up, and the figure draws the moment it happens: wind, open
at its base, has gone into the water — 巽乎水 (*xùn hū shuǐ*, "entering into the water") — and the
water now rides on the wood, the full bucket on its way up. The odd lines sit at the base within and
at the centre without: the entering starts at the bottom of the self, and what it reaches is the firm
core in the middle of the world — the fifth line, the spring. That is 乃以剛中也 (*nǎi yǐ gāng zhōng
yě*, "because the firm is at the centre") read off the figure: the town can move because what it
draws on cannot.

Yin within, yang without: the self is the empty shaft, and the substance lies outside it. The core
text climbs that shaft line by line — 井泥 (*jǐng ní*, "the well is mud") at the bottom, then 井谷
(*jǐng gǔ*, "the well's hollow"), 井渫 (*jǐng xiè*, "the well is cleaned"), 井甃 (*jǐng zhòu*, "the well
is lined"), and at the firm fifth line, where water has its core, 井冽，寒泉食 (*jǐng liè, hán quán
shí*, "the well is clear; the cold spring is drunk"). 47 is this figure upside down: there the water
had sunk beneath an empty lake and no one went down for it; here something does.

**For the name.** *The Well* — the signature supports it without strain: it describes a device, a
shaft that makes sinking water rise, and the lines name the well eleven times. **Candidates the
signature suggests:** *The Shaft*, *The Spring* — *The Shaft* survives, just: 改邑不改井 (*gǎi yì bù
gǎi jǐng*, "change the town, not the shaft") and 井甃 (*jǐng zhòu*, "the shaft is lined") read, but 井冽
(*jǐng liè*, "the shaft is clear") loses the water; *The Spring* — the firm core — fails the lines:
寒泉 (*hán quán*, "the cold spring") in the fifth line is inside the well, so the spring cannot name
the whole.

## The heart of it

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-25 — an interpretive reading, not a translation. Each claim is marked by
layer: the core text (the judgment and the six lines, Western Zhou), the Wings (Warring States to
Han), 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*, 3rd century), and echoes of the Tao Te Ching in this project's own translation.
The formula words — 吉, 凶, 无咎, 亨, 孚, 貞 — carry working glosses until WORKLIST A4 and A6 rule on
them ([`hexagram-names.md`](../hexagram-names.md)).*

### Essence

**The town can move and the well cannot — and a well is worth only what is drawn from it.** 說文
(*shuōwén*) gives 井 (*jǐng*, "well") as 八家一井 (*bā jiā yī jǐng*, "eight households, one well"), and
says the graph 象構韓形 (*xiàng gòu hán xíng*, "pictures the built frame of the curb") — the square of
timbers laid round a well's mouth, seen from above. Shared, fixed, and made. The core text goes down
the shaft and back up it: mud at the bottom, a leaking hollow, a well cleaned and not used, a
lining, clear cold water, and at the top a mouth left open. Good fortune rises with it. And the
judgment's one ominous moment is not about the water at all. It is the jar, broken just short of the
top.

### The polarities in it

Wind within, water without: entering at the base below, a firm core above (see *The signature*,
above). Water left to itself goes down; that is its action. A well is the made thing that brings it
up, and the 彖 (*tuàn*, the judgment commentary) reads the figure as that moment: 巽乎水而上水 (*xùn hū
shuǐ ér shàng shuǐ*, "entering into the water and bringing the water up"). The firm line at the
centre of the water is the spring, and it is why the well stays when all else moves — 改邑不改井，乃以剛中也
(*gǎi yì bù gǎi jǐng, nǎi yǐ gāng zhōng yě*, "the town changes, the well does not — because the firm
is at the centre"). The lines climb the shaft just as the figure is stacked. **But the structure
only shows that drawing is possible; the text insists it may not happen.** The broken jar, the
leaking pot, the clean well no one uses — the failures are all on the human side, in the rope and
the vessel and the will to draw. No trigram says that. The text does.

### In nature

The 大象: 木上有水 (*mù shàng yǒu shuǐ*, "above the wood there is water"). The 說卦 (*shuōguà*) gives the
trigram within as wood as well as wind: 巽為木 (*xùn wéi mù*, "the wind trigram is wood"). The plainest
picture is the wooden bucket coming up full. Lean over a working well on a hot day and feel the air:
groundwater stays close to the year's average warmth, so a well is cold in summer and mild in
winter. 寒泉 (*hán quán*, "the cold spring") is the summer truth of every deep well. Drawing pulls
fresh water in through the ground from all round; a well left undrawn goes the other way — leaves
fall in, silt settles, the bottom turns to mud.

The judgment's 汔 (*qì*, "nearly") is in 說文 水涸也 (*shuǐ hé yě*, "water running dry") — a low well, the
rope paid out far. The lining of line 4, 甃 (*zhòu*), is 井壁也 (*jǐng bì yě*, "the wall of a well"):
brick or fired tile set round the shaft so the earth does not fall in. Everything named here is
something a village had to make and keep.

### The movement of the lines

**The judgment.** 井：改邑不改井，无喪无得，往來井井。汔至亦未繘井。羸其瓶，凶 (*jǐng: gǎi yì bù gǎi jǐng, wú sàng wú dé, wǎng lái
jǐng jǐng. qì zhì yì wèi yù jǐng. léi qí píng, xiōng*) — "The well: the town changes, the well does
not. Nothing lost, nothing gained; coming and going, drawing and drawing. Nearly there, and not yet
roped up out of the well: the jar is broken — ominous." 井井 (*jǐng jǐng*, "well upon well") is the
name doubled: everyone who comes, drawing in turn. 羸 (*léi*, "worn, broken") is in 說文 瘦也 (*shòu yě*,
"lean") — a jar worn thin that fails at the last.

**Line 1** is the bottom. 井泥不食，舊井无禽 (*jǐng ní bù shí, jiù jǐng wú qín*, "the well is mud and not
drunk from; an old well, no creatures come"). 禽 (*qín*) is in 說文 走獸緫名 (*zǒu shòu zǒng míng*, "a
general name for animals"), wider than birds: nothing living comes to it.

**Line 2** leaks. 井谷射鮒，瓮敝漏 (*jǐng gǔ shè fù, wèng bì lòu*, "the well's hollow shoots at the minnows;
the jar is worn out and leaks"). 鮒 (*fù*) is a small carp, at home in still water. 王弼 reads the line
as water going the wrong way: 水不上出而反下注 (*shuǐ bù shàng chū ér fǎn xià zhù*, "the water does not come
up and out, but pours back down"). A firm centre within, and nothing above answers it; what it has
serves the small things at the bottom.

**Line 3** is ready and unused. 井渫不食，為我心惻，可用汲，王明，并受其福 (*jǐng xiè bù shí, wèi wǒ xīn cè, kě yòng jí,
wáng míng, bìng shòu qí fú*, "the well is cleaned and not drunk from: it makes my heart ache. It
could be drawn from. If the ruler sees clearly, all together receive its blessing"). 惻 (*cè*) is in
說文 痛也 (*tòng yě*, "pain"), and 汲 (*jí*) 引水於井也 (*yǐn shuǐ yú jǐng yě*, "to draw water from a well").
The only line that says 我 (*wǒ*, "I, my"). At the top of the lower trigram, the seam: clean water,
and no one reaching down.

**Line 4** is the repair. 井甃，无咎 (*jǐng zhòu, wú jiù*, "the well is lined: no blame"). Work that
gives no water itself — and is blameless. The 小象 (*xiǎo xiàng*, the line commentary): 修井也 (*xiū jǐng
yě*, "mending the well").

**Line 5** is the spring. 井冽，寒泉食 (*jǐng liè, hán quán shí*, "the well is clear; the cold spring is
drunk from"). The firm centre of the water, and at last someone drinks.

**Line 6** is the open mouth. 井收勿幕，有孚元吉 (*jǐng shōu wù mù, yǒu fú yuán jí*, "the well is drawn up;
do not cover it. There is confidence: from the source, auspicious"). 幕 (*mù*) is in 說文 覆食案亦曰幕 (*fù
shí àn yì yuē mù*, "a cloth over a food table is also called 幕"): do not cover the well as one
covers a meal kept back. The highest fortune in the figure falls on leaving it open. 王弼: 不擅其有，不私其利
(*bù shàn qí yǒu, bù sī qí lì*, "not claiming what it has, not keeping its advantage to itself").

### In a life now

The village well has its descendants: the water supply, a public library, a hospital, a good
teacher, a parent's steady house, a skill kept up for years — the shared source a community stands
on while the town changes round it. The counsel is about keeping and drawing. Clean it (line 3);
line it (line 4); do not let it silt up (line 1); do not cover it for yourself (line 6). Its grief
is line 3: water clean and ready and no one drawing — talent unused, a service no one funds — and
its remedy is 王明 (*wáng míng*, "the ruler sees clearly"), the ones who decide seeing what is there.
Its warning is the judgment's: most of the labour of bringing water up can be lost at the lip, when
the jar breaks — care at the end. The 大象 makes the well a practice: 君子以勞民勸相 (*jūnzǐ yǐ láo mín quàn
xiàng*, "the noble one labours for the people and urges them to help one another"). A well is dug
and kept by many hands.

### An echo from the Laozi

**The two books share a graph for what does not run out, and part over whether a source needs
keeping.** The Laozi's chapter 45: *"Great fullness seems empty, yet when used, it never runs dry."*
Its *never runs dry* is 不窮 (*bù qióng*), the words of the 彖's 井養而不窮也 (*jǐng yǎng ér bù qióng yě*,
"the well nourishes and is not used up"). The echo passes the two-question test at the Wing layer;
the core text's nearest words, 无喪无得 (*wú sàng wú dé*, "nothing lost, nothing gained"), meet it only
thematically. (Chapter 64's *"People often ruin their affairs just short of completing them"* is the
broken jar in spirit — thematic only; the graphs differ.)

Both books know a source that gives and is not lessened. **But the Laozi's fullness needs nothing;
the well needs everything.** Rope, jar, lining, cleaning, someone to draw, a ruler who sees — the
core text spends most of its lines on the keeping. The Laozi's paradox is that fullness looks empty;
the well's plain fact is that water in the ground does no one any good until it is brought up.

### Words kept

- 井 (*jǐng*) — *The Well*, the proposed name (not yet approved); *Wellspring* collides with 泉 (*quán*), *the spring*, which is inside the well
- 木 (*mù*) — *wood*, the 大象's image; the trigram 巽's own image is *wind*
- 心 (*xīn*) — *heart*, never *mind* (locked)
- 明 (*míng*) — *see clearly*, never *enlightened* (locked)
- 王 (*wáng*) — *ruler*, never *king* (locked)
- 窮 (*qióng*) — *used up*; in the Laozi's ch 45, *runs dry*
- 孚 (*fú*) — *confidence*, working gloss (A6); 元吉 (*yuán jí*) *from the source, auspicious*
- 凶 · 无咎 (*xiōng · wú jiù*) — *ominous · no blame*, working glosses (A4)
- 養 (*yǎng*) — *nourish*
