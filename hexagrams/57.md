## The signature — a reading

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-24. This reads the figure through the Wings — the declared lens
([`README.md`](../README.md#the-lens)) — and it is a reading, not a ruling. The name still comes from
the lines ([`read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums`](../principles/read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums.md)).*

**The signature.** Within: **Wind** (巽 · yin · entering). Without: **Wind** (巽 · yin · entering).
**Doubled** — thunder ↔ wind, the yielding pole twice.

**What the tradition reads.** The 彖 (*tuàn*) opens on the doubling: 重巽以申命 (*chóng xùn yǐ shēn mìng*), "巽 doubled, so as to send the word out again" — 命 (*mìng*) being what is spoken over a thing and sent with it — and 柔皆順乎剛 (*róu jiē shùn hū gāng*), "the yielding lines all go along with the firm." The 大象 (*dà xiàng*) makes it succession: 隨風，巽；君子以申命行事 (*suí fēng, xùn; jūnzǐ yǐ shēn mìng xíng shì*), "wind following wind: 巽; the noble one sends the word out again and carries out affairs." The tradition takes the figure as influence that works by repeating and by getting beneath, never by force.

**What the polarity implies.** Each trigram is two firm lines resting on one open line: the yielding at the bottom, taking the weight. Doubled, the figure shows that posture twice — getting under, and then under again. Thunder meets resistance and breaks it; wind entering wind meets nothing that is not also giving way, so its hazard is not collision but having no floor. The lines follow that exactly. It starts undecided, 進退 (*jìn tuì*), "advancing and retreating", which 說卦 (*shuōguà*) lists among wind's own marks: 為進退、為不果 (*wéi jìn tuì, wéi bù guǒ*), "it is advancing and retreating, it is not carrying through." In the middle it becomes 頻巽 (*pín xùn*), "kneeling again and again", and 吝 (*lìn*), "stinting." The same words, 巽在牀下 (*xùn zài chuáng xià*), "kneeling beneath the bed", are 吉 (*jí*), "auspicious", at the second line and 凶 (*xiōng*), "ominous", at the top, where the kneeler has 喪其資斧 (*sàng qí zī fǔ*), "lost the travelling axe." The structure suggests that getting beneath is right while the word it carries still has somewhere to arrive: at the fifth line it is sent 先庚三日，后庚三日 (*xiān gēng sān rì, hòu gēng sān rì*), "three days before 庚 (*gēng*, a day of the cycle), three days after." Taken all the way up, it is getting beneath with nothing left to carry.

**For the name.** *Kneeling* — the signature deepens it: 說文 (*shuōwén*)'s graph carries two kneeling figures, and the figure is two open bases under weight, so graph and figure double the same posture. **Candidates the signature suggests:** *Getting Under* — survives: 巽在牀下 "getting under the bed" and 頻巽 "getting under again and again" both read, though it drops the courtesy *kneeling* keeps; *Pervading* — fails the lines: "pervading beneath the bed" (巽在牀下) says nothing, and it is wind's image, which the trigram already owns.

## The heart of it

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-25 — an interpretive reading, not a translation. Each claim is marked by
layer: the core text (the judgment and the six lines, Western Zhou), the Wings (Warring States to
Han), 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*, 3rd century), and echoes of the Tao Te Ching in this project's own translation.
The formula words — 吉, 凶, 无咎, 亨, 孚, 貞 — carry working glosses until WORKLIST A4 and A6 rule on
them ([`hexagram-names.md`](../hexagram-names.md)).*

### Essence

**Getting low is how one gets in — and the same kneeling, beneath the same bed, is auspicious at
the second line and ominous at the top.** The core text says 巽在牀下 (*xùn zài chuáng xià*,
"kneeling beneath the bed") twice, word for word, and gives it opposite outcomes; everything in
this figure turns on what separates them. The judgment promises only 小亨 (*xiǎo hēng*, "a small
getting-through"): this is not the power that breaks things open. 說文 (*shuōwén*) glosses the
graph 具也 (*jù yě*, "set out, made ready") and builds it on a stand, 丌 (*jī*), under a pair of
figures that modern palaeography reads as kneeling (說文 calls that pair only the sound). Something
offered, low, and ready. The Wings name the act: 巽者入也 (*xùn zhě rù yě*, "巽 is entering",
序卦 *xùguà*), and 巽伏 (*xùn fú*, "巽 lies hidden", 雜卦 *záguà*).

### The polarities in it

Wind within, wind without: one pole twice, each trigram an open line carrying two firm ones. So the
figure has no firm base anywhere — nothing in it can push off and strike, as thunder does from its
firm bottom line. What it has is firmness at both centres, and the 彖 (*tuàn*) puts the weight there:
剛巽乎中正而志行 (*gāng xùn hū zhōng zhèng ér zhì xíng*, "the firm kneels, centred and upright, and
its intent is carried out"). The lines agree. The kneeling that works is done by strength from the
middle (lines 2 and 5, both auspicious); the open line at the bottom only wavers, and the firm line
at the top, kneeling past every centre, loses the thing that made it firm.

### In nature

Watch what wind does to a building in a gale. It rarely pushes a wall over. It gets under the edge
of a slate, the lip of a tile, the eaves of a barn roof, and lifts from beneath; the damage after a
storm is at the edges, where the wind found a way in. It does this by coming again: the 大象 (*dà
xiàng*) image is 隨風 (*suí fēng*, "wind following wind"), and a strong wind is exactly that — gust
after gust in trains, each arriving before the last has settled, working at the same gap.

Look too at trees on an exposed coast or a hill ridge. A steady wind from one quarter, year after
year, never breaks them; it shapes them, so that they lean and branch away from it, their crowns
drawn out like flags. That is 巽's kind of influence — not one blow, but the same light pressure,
repeated, getting into the growth itself. 說卦 (*shuōguà*) gives 巽 its season and quarter: 齊乎巽
(*qí hū xùn*, "things are made even in 巽"), 巽，東南也 (*xùn, dōng nán yě*, "巽 is the south-east")
— late spring into summer, when growth is thick and the wind moves through all of it.

### The movement of the lines

**The judgment.** 巽：小亨。利有攸往。利見大人 (*xùn: xiǎo hēng. lì yǒu yōu wǎng. lì jiàn dà rén*)
— "Kneeling *(proposed)*: a small getting-through. Advantage in having somewhere to go; advantage
in seeing the great person." Kneeling is always toward someone, and always on the way somewhere.

**Line 1** cannot decide. 進退，利武人之貞 (*jìn tuì, lì wǔ rén zhī zhēn*, "advancing, retreating;
advantage in a soldier's holding firm"). 說卦 lists 為進退、為不果 (*wéi jìn tuì, wéi bù guǒ*,
"advancing and retreating, not carrying through") among wind's own marks. What the open base
lacks is a soldier's resolve.

**Line 2** is the kneeling that works. 巽在牀下，用史巫紛若，吉无咎 (*xùn zài chuáng xià, yòng shǐ wū
fēn ruò, jí wú jiù*, "kneeling beneath the bed; using scribes and spirit-mediums in great number —
auspicious, no blame"). Under the bed is where the unseen trouble is — sickness, fear, whatever
cannot be faced standing. 說文 makes the 巫 (*wū*) one who 以舞降神 (*yǐ wǔ jiàng shén*, "brings the
numinous down by dancing"). 王弼 reads the line as the lowest humility turned toward the spirits and
not toward power.

**Line 3** kneels with a frown. 頻巽，吝 (*pín xùn, lìn*, "kneeling again and again — stinting").
王弼 reads 頻 (*pín*) as knitted brows, 頻蹙不樂 (*pín cù bù lè*, "frowning, unhappy"): kneeling
done because there is no choice. The 小象 (*xiǎo xiàng*): 志窮也 (*zhì qióng yě*, "the intent has run
out").

**Line 4** comes home with something. 悔亡，田獲三品 (*huǐ wáng, tián huò sān pǐn*, "regret goes;
hunting, taking three kinds of game"). 王弼: one for the offerings, one for guests, one for the
ruler's kitchen. The yielding line, rightly placed under the firm fifth, is the one that brings
back plenty.

**Line 5** is where the word goes out. 貞吉悔亡，无不利。无初有終，先庚三日，后庚三日，吉 (*zhēn jí
huǐ wáng, wú bù lì. wú chū yǒu zhōng, xiān gēng sān rì, hòu gēng sān rì, jí*, "holding firm,
auspicious; regret goes; nothing without advantage. No beginning, but an end: three days before
庚 (*gēng*, a day in the ten-day cycle), three days after — auspicious"). 王弼 makes 庚 the moment
of announcement: 申命令謂之庚 (*shēn mìng lìng wèi zhī gēng*, "sending out the word again is called
庚"). A change pressed on people is not welcomed at first; given three days' notice and three
days' grace, it ends well. 18 蠱 (*gǔ*) has the same span around 甲 (*jiǎ*).

**Line 6** repeats line 2 exactly, and loses. 巽在牀下，喪其資斧，貞凶 (*xùn zài chuáng xià, sàng qí zī
fǔ, zhēn xiōng*, "kneeling beneath the bed, losing the travelling axe; holding firm — ominous").
56 旅 (*lǚ*), the figure just before, has the traveller 得其資斧 (*dé qí zī fǔ*, "get the
travelling axe"); here it is gone. 王弼: 斧所以斷者也 (*fǔ suǒ yǐ duàn zhě yě*, "the axe is what one
cuts with") — what decides. Kneeling taken all the way up has nothing left to decide with.

### In a life now

This is the figure for influence without rank: the new person changing an old institution, the
carer at a bedside, the envoy, the teacher of a reluctant class, anyone who must get a thing
accepted by people who cannot be made to accept it. It counsels getting low, getting in, and
coming again — and it counsels timing: the word sent out with notice before and time after
(line 5). It is honest about the cost. Kneeling from a frown (line 3) stints. Kneeling as a habit,
past the point where there is anything to deliver, becomes the person who agrees with everyone and
can no longer decide anything (line 6). The test is line 2's and line 6's difference: does the
one who kneels still carry the axe?

The 大象 turns this into practice: 君子以申命行事 (*jūnzǐ yǐ shēn mìng xíng shì*, "the noble one
sends the word out again, and carries out affairs") — 命 (*mìng*) here what is spoken over a thing
and sent with it, not a decree. Influence that works is said more than once.

### An echo from the Laozi

**Partly shared, and they part at the floor.** The Laozi's chapter 66: *"Rivers and seas can rule
the hundred valleys / because they are masterful at staying below them."* The character is 下
(*xià*, "below"), and it is in 57's core text, 牀下 (*chuáng xià*, "beneath the bed"); the English
answers to it. But in 57 下 is first a place, and it becomes a stance only through 王弼's reading,
so the kinship is more thematic than textual. And the books part plainly: the Laozi sets no floor
under lowness — *"the large one ought to be underneath"* (ch 61). 57 sets one. At the top line,
the same getting-under is ominous. The I Ching's lowness has a limit, and this reading keeps it.
