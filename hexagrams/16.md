## The signature — a reading

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-24. This reads the figure through the Wings — the declared lens
([`README.md`](../README.md#the-lens)) — and it is a reading, not a ruling. The name still comes from
the lines ([`read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums`](../principles/read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums.md)).*

**The signature.** Within: **earth** (坤 · yin · going along). Without: **thunder** (震 · yang · moving out).
**Across** — sky ↔ earth under thunder ↔ wind.

**What the tradition reads.** The 彖 (*tuàn*) reads the two actions as one sentence:
順以動，豫 (*shùn yǐ dòng, yù*, "going along, and so moving: 豫"), and widens it to the whole order —
天地以順動，故日月不過，而四時不忒 (*tiān dì yǐ shùn dòng, gù rì yuè bù guò, ér sì shí bù tè*, "sky
and earth move by going along, so sun and moon do not overshoot and the four seasons do not slip").
The 大象 (*dà xiàng*) sees 雷出地奮 (*léi chū dì fèn*, "thunder comes out of the earth, rousing"),
and the former rulers 以作樂崇德 (*yǐ zuò yuè chóng dé*, "made music and honoured integrity").

**What the polarity implies.** 15 turned over: the one firm line has come up from line 3 to line 4,
from the closed top of a mountain under the ground to the firm base of thunder just above it. The
ground below is wholly yielding, and nothing resists the move, so the move costs nothing. That is
the ease: motion on a ground that goes along. 說文 (*shuōwén*)'s large elephant that harms nothing is
the same picture — great weight, no friction. But the structure splits the ease two ways. For the
one firm line it is 由豫，大有得 (*yóu yù, dà yǒu dé*, "ease that comes from it: great gain", 16.4),
and the five yielding lines gather to it: 朋盍簪 (*péng hé zān*, "friends gather as hair to a pin").
For the yielding lines that merely ride it, ease is ruin: 鳴豫，凶 (*míng yù, xiōng*, "crowing ease:
ominous", 16.1), 盱豫 (*xū yù*, "gaping ease", 16.3), 冥豫 (*míng yù*, "benighted ease", 16.6). Only
16.2 stays hard — 介于石，不終日 (*jiè yú shí, bù zhōng rì*, "firm as stone, not the whole day") — and
is auspicious. Set it against 15: 鳴謙，貞吉 (*míng qiān, zhēn jí*, "modesty that calls out: holding
firm is auspicious", 15.2) and 鳴豫，凶 (*míng yù, xiōng*, "crowing ease: ominous"). Sounded modesty is well; sounded ease is not.

**For the name.** **Ease** — supports: the signature is motion over a ground that does not push
back, and *ease* takes every modifier the lines put on it. **Candidates the signature suggests:**
*Readiness* — survives: it answers the judgment's 利建侯行師 (*lì jiàn hóu xíng shī*, "favourable to
set up lords and march armies"), and "crowing readiness", "benighted readiness" still read;
*Rousing* — fails the lines: 盱豫 (*xū yù*, "gaping ease") and 冥豫 (*míng yù*, "benighted ease") are slack, eyes up and in the dark, not roused, and the
rousing (奮, *fèn*) belongs to the image of the 大象 (*dà xiàng*, "the great image"), not to any line.

## The heart of it

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-25 — an interpretive reading, not a translation. Each claim is marked by
layer: the core text (the judgment and the six lines, Western Zhou), the Wings (Warring States to
Han), Wang Bi (the 3rd-century commentator), and echoes of the Tao Te Ching in this project's own
translation. The formula words — 吉 ("auspicious"), 凶 ("ominous"), 无咎 ("no blame"), 亨 ("gets
through"), 孚 ("confidence"), 貞 ("holding firm") — carry working glosses until WORKLIST A4 and A6
rule on them ([`hexagram-names.md`](../hexagram-names.md)).*

### Essence

**Ease is great weight moving over ground that gives — a gain for the one it comes through, and a
danger for everyone who only rests in it.** The Shuowen, the c. 100 CE character dictionary, gives 豫
("Ease") as 象之大者 ("an elephant of the large kind"), and adds 賈侍中說：不害於物 ("Jia Kui says: it does not
harm things"). Four of the six lines put a word in front of 豫 ("Ease"), and the word decides
everything: crowing (line 1), gaping (3), coming-through (4), benighted (6). Only line 4 gains. And
the judgment points ease at work, not rest: 豫：利建侯行師 ("ease: advantageous to set up lords and march
armies"). Ease, here, is what makes it possible to move.

### The polarities in it

Earth within, thunder without — 15 turned over, its one firm line lifted from 3 to 4. Earth goes
along; thunder moves out from its firm base. Nothing beneath resists, so the firm line moves at no
cost, and the five yielding lines gather to it. **The ground's giving is the ease, and the thunder's
base is what uses it; a line that only goes along, with no firm base of its own, is carried — and
goes slack.** The Tuan, the Wing on the judgment, puts the two actions in one phrase: 順以動 ("going
along, and so moving").

### In nature

雷出地奮 ("thunder comes out of the earth, rousing" — the Great Image, the Wing on the whole figure).
The Shuowen's 奮 ("rouse") is a bird with spread wings over a field — 奞在田上 ("wings spread, above the
field") — and it cites the Odes' 不能奮飛 ("cannot rise and fly"). Walk the edge of a stubble field and
a pheasant goes up from almost under your feet with a clatter of wings: sudden, loud, out of ground
that looked empty. Spring thunder has the same shape — the first storm after winter, out of a still
sky. The Shuogua, the Wing on the trigrams, gives thunder the east and the opening of the year:
萬物出乎震 ("the countless things come out in thunder").

Then the elephant. An elephant weighs as much as a small house and walks almost without a sound; its
foot is a thick cushion that spreads under the load. Great weight that harms nothing is good natural
history, and it is the ease of line 4: heavy, and without friction.

And the moon. Line 6's 冥 ("dark") is, in the Shuowen, the moon's word: 十六日而月始虧幽也 ("on the sixteenth
day the moon begins to wane into dark"). The night after full, the moon rises later, and the early
evening goes black. Benighted ease is that evening: still bright late, already turning.

### The movement of the lines

**The judgment.** 豫：利建侯行師 ("ease: advantageous to set up lords and march armies"). The Wings split
over the word exactly where the lines do. Part 2 of the Xici, the Great Commentary, reads readiness:
重門擊柝以待暴客，蓋取諸豫 ("double gates and the watchman's clapper, to await raiders — taken from Ease"). The
Zagua, the Wing that pairs the hexagrams, reads slackness: 豫怠也 ("ease is slackness").

**Line 1.** 鳴豫，凶 ("crowing ease: ominous"). The bottom line, yielding, with a strong friend in line
4 — and it sings about it. The Small Image, the Wing on each line: 志窮凶也 ("the aim runs out:
ominous"). Set it against 15.2's 鳴謙 ("modesty that calls out"), which is auspicious: the same call,
a different thing called.

**Line 2.** 介于石，不終日，貞吉 ("firm as stone; not the whole day; holding firm: auspicious"). The Shuowen's
介 ("boundary") is 畫也 ("a drawn line"), and 人各有介 ("each person has their boundary"). The centre of
the earth trigram, and the one yielding line that stays hard: it sees how ease goes and leaves
before the day is out. Wang Bi: 明禍福之所生 ("seeing clearly where misfortune and fortune are born").

**Line 3.** 盱豫，悔。遲有悔 ("gaping ease: regret. Slow: there is regret"). The Shuowen's 盱 ("gaping") is
張目也 ("eyes stretched wide"). At the seam, right under line 4, looking up at the source of ease — and
too slow either to follow it or to leave.

**Line 4.** 由豫，大有得。勿疑。朋盍簪 ("ease comes through it: great gain. Do not doubt. Friends gather, as hair
to the pin"). The one firm line, the thunder's base. 14's name stands inside it: 大有 ("great
having"), gained. 簪 ("hairpin") is ordinarily a hairpin; Wang Bi glosses it otherwise — 盍合也簪疾也
("*gather* is joining; *pin* is quick") — friends gather fast. Either way the many close on one
fastening point, on one condition: 勿疑 ("do not doubt").

**Line 5.** 貞疾，恆不死 ("holding firm: sickness; it lasts, and does not die"). Here the older sense of 貞
("holding firm") as "the inquiry" reads more naturally: "an inquiry into sickness — it lingers, and
does not die". The ruler's place, yielding, riding the firm line below it (the Small Image: 乘剛也 ("it
rides the firm")): a centre that has lost its strength to the line beneath and goes on anyway — 中未亡也
("the centre is not yet lost").

**Line 6.** 冥豫，成有渝，无咎 ("benighted ease; when it is complete, there is a change: no blame"). The
Shuowen's 渝 ("change") is 變汙也 ("to change, to stain"). The top of thunder, the end of ease. Even
ease gone dark ends without blame, if it changes — the only way out the line offers.

### In a life now

A good season: a holiday that has gone on a little long, retirement, the months after a victory, a
company growing without effort, a love that has settled, a rich country at peace. Ease is not
condemned — line 4 is great gain, and the judgment uses ease to organise and to move. But the lines
sort the people in it. The one through whom ease comes, the person who did the work, gains, and
friends gather fast, so long as there is no doubt (4). Those who crow about it (1), those who live
by gazing up at someone else's (3), those who drift on after dark (6) go wrong, or must change. Line
2 is the counsel most needed: know your line, see how it goes, leave before the day is out. Line 5
is the chronic illness, lived with and not died of.

The Great Image gives ease a form: 先王以作樂崇德 ("the former rulers made music and honoured integrity"),
and 殷薦之上帝，以配祖考 ("offered it richly to the god above, and set their ancestors beside it"). The
Shuowen's 樂 ("music") is 象鼓鞞 ("a picture of drums"); read another way, the same graph is joy.
Thunder out of the ground, made into drums, and turned toward integrity.

### An echo from the Laozi

**Two contacts, and each faces the other way.** The first is the graph itself. 豫 ("Ease") is in the
Laozi once, chapter 15, and [the Open Tao Te Ching](https://github.com/opencosmos-ai/taoteching) has *"Careful, like wading across a river in winter."*
There 豫 ("careful") is caution — feeling for the ice at each step; here, in the core, it is ease.
One character, two senses, and the two Englishes rightly differ. The Xici's watchman at the double
gate (part 2) stands nearer the Laozi's word than any line of 16 does.

The second is the three graphs 不終日 ("not the whole day"), recorded in
[`tao-te-ching-relation.md`](../tao-te-ching-relation.md) § 4 as a contact point: 16.2, and chapter
23's *"A downpour does not last out the day."* It passes the two-question test — the graphs are in
the core, and the Open Tao Te Ching's English answers to them. **But the subjects part.** In the Laozi it is
nature's excess that cannot last the day. In 16.2 it is a person who will not stay the whole day —
who sees ease for what it is and goes first. One is a fact about storms; the other is a choice. This
reading keeps them apart.
