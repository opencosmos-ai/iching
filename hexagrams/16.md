## The signature — a reading

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-24. This reads the figure through the Wings — the declared lens
([`README.md`](../README.md#the-lens)) — and it is a reading, not a ruling. The name still comes from
the lines ([`read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums`](../principles/read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums.md)).*

**The signature.** Within: **earth** (坤 · yin · going along). Without: **thunder** (震 · yang · moving out).
**Across** — sky ↔ earth under thunder ↔ wind.

**What the tradition reads.** The 彖 (*tuàn*) reads the two actions as one sentence:
順以動，豫 (*shùn yǐ dòng, yù*, "going along, and so moving: 豫"), and widens it to the whole order —
天地以順動，故日月不過，而四時不忒 (*tiān dì yǐ shùn dòng, gù rì yuè bù guò, ér sì shí bù tè*, "sky
and earth move by going along, so sun and moon do not overshoot and the four seasons do not slip").
The 大象 (*dà xiàng*) sees 雷出地奮 (*léi chū dì fèn*, "thunder comes out of the earth, rousing"),
and the former rulers 以作樂崇德 (*yǐ zuò yuè chóng dé*, "made music and honoured integrity").

**What the polarity implies.** 15 turned over: the one firm line has come up from line 3 to line 4,
from the closed top of a mountain under the ground to the firm base of thunder just above it. The
ground below is wholly yielding, and nothing resists the move, so the move costs nothing. That is
the ease: motion on a ground that goes along. 說文 (*shuōwén*)'s large elephant that harms nothing is
the same picture — great weight, no friction. But the structure splits the ease two ways. For the
one firm line it is 由豫，大有得 (*yóu yù, dà yǒu dé*, "ease that comes from it: great gain", 16.4),
and the five yielding lines gather to it: 朋盍簪 (*péng hé zān*, "friends gather as hair to a pin").
For the yielding lines that merely ride it, ease is ruin: 鳴豫，凶 (*míng yù, xiōng*, "crowing ease:
ominous", 16.1), 盱豫 (*xū yù*, "gaping ease", 16.3), 冥豫 (*míng yù*, "benighted ease", 16.6). Only
16.2 stays hard — 介于石，不終日 (*jiè yú shí, bù zhōng rì*, "firm as stone, not the whole day") — and
is auspicious. Set it against 15: 鳴謙，貞吉 (*míng qiān, zhēn jí*, "modesty that calls out: holding
firm is auspicious", 15.2) and 鳴豫，凶 (*míng yù, xiōng*, "crowing ease: ominous"). Sounded modesty is well; sounded ease is not.

**For the name.** **Ease** — supports: the signature is motion over a ground that does not push
back, and *ease* takes every modifier the lines put on it. **Candidates the signature suggests:**
*Readiness* — survives: it answers the judgment's 利建侯行師 (*lì jiàn hóu xíng shī*, "favourable to
set up lords and march armies"), and "crowing readiness", "benighted readiness" still read;
*Rousing* — fails the lines: 盱豫 (*xū yù*, "gaping ease") and 冥豫 (*míng yù*, "benighted ease") are slack, eyes up and in the dark, not roused, and the
rousing (奮, *fèn*) belongs to the image of the 大象 (*dà xiàng*, "the great image"), not to any line.

## The heart of it

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-25 — an interpretive reading, not a translation. Each claim is marked by
layer: the core text (the judgment and the six lines, Western Zhou), the Wings (Warring States to
Han), 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*, 3rd century), and echoes of the Tao Te Ching in this project's own translation.
The formula words — 吉, 凶, 无咎, 亨, 孚, 貞 — carry working glosses until WORKLIST A4 and A6 rule on
them ([`hexagram-names.md`](../hexagram-names.md)).*

### Essence

**Ease is great weight moving over ground that gives — a gain for the one it comes through, and a
danger for everyone who only rests in it.** 說文 (*shuōwén*) gives 豫 (*yù*) as 象之大者 (*xiàng zhī
dà zhě*, "an elephant of the large kind"), and adds 賈侍中說：不害於物 (*Jiǎ shìzhōng shuō: bù hài yú
wù*, "Jia Kui says: it does not harm things"). Four of the six lines put a word in front of 豫, and
the word decides everything: crowing (line 1), gaping (3), coming-through (4), benighted (6). Only
line 4 gains. And the judgment points ease at work, not rest: 豫：利建侯行師 (*yù: lì jiàn hóu xíng
shī*, "ease: advantageous to set up lords and march armies"). Ease, here, is what makes it possible
to move.

### The polarities in it

Earth within, thunder without — 15 turned over, its one firm line lifted from 3 to 4. Earth goes
along; thunder moves out from its firm base. Nothing beneath resists, so the firm line moves at no
cost, and the five yielding lines gather to it. **The ground's giving is the ease, and the thunder's
base is what uses it; a line that only goes along, with no firm base of its own, is carried — and
goes slack.** The 彖 puts the two actions in one phrase: 順以動 (*shùn yǐ dòng*, "going along, and
so moving").

### In nature

雷出地奮 (*léi chū dì fèn*, "thunder comes out of the earth, rousing", 大象). 說文's 奮 (*fèn*) is
a bird with spread wings over a field — 奞在田上 (*suī zài tián shàng*, "wings spread, above the
field") — and it cites the Odes' 不能奮飛 (*bù néng fèn fēi*, "cannot rise and fly"). Walk the edge
of a stubble field and a pheasant goes up from almost under your feet with a clatter of wings:
sudden, loud, out of ground that looked empty. Spring thunder has the same shape — the first storm
after winter, out of a still sky. 說卦 gives thunder the east and the opening of the year: 萬物出乎震
(*wàn wù chū hū zhèn*, "the countless things come out in thunder").

Then the elephant. An elephant weighs as much as a small house and walks almost without a sound;
its foot is a thick cushion that spreads under the load. Great weight that harms nothing is good
natural history, and it is the ease of line 4: heavy, and without friction.

And the moon. Line 6's 冥 (*míng*, "dark") is, in 說文, the moon's word: 十六日而月始虧幽也 (*shí liù
rì ér yuè shǐ kuī yōu yě*, "on the sixteenth day the moon begins to wane into dark"). The night after
full, the moon rises later, and the early evening goes black. Benighted ease is that evening: still
bright late, already turning.

### The movement of the lines

**The judgment.** 豫：利建侯行師 — "ease: advantageous to set up lords and march armies." The Wings
split over the word exactly where the lines do. 繫辭下 reads readiness: 重門擊柝以待暴客，蓋取諸豫
(*chóng mén jī tuò yǐ dài bào kè, gài qǔ zhū yù*, "double gates and the watchman's clapper, to await
raiders — taken from 豫"). The 雜卦 reads slackness: 豫怠也 (*yù dài yě*, "ease is slackness").

**Line 1.** 鳴豫，凶 (*míng yù, xiōng*, "crowing ease: ominous"). The bottom line, yielding, with a
strong friend in line 4 — and it sings about it. The 小象: 志窮凶也 (*zhì qióng xiōng yě*, "the aim
runs out: ominous"). Set it against 15.2's 鳴謙 (*míng qiān*, "modesty that calls out"), which is
auspicious: the same call, a different thing called.

**Line 2.** 介于石，不終日，貞吉 (*jiè yú shí, bù zhōng rì, zhēn jí*, "firm as stone; not the whole
day; holding firm: auspicious"). 說文's 介 (*jiè*) is 畫也 (*huà yě*, "a drawn line"), and 人各有介
(*rén gè yǒu jiè*, "each person has their boundary"). The centre of the earth trigram, and the one
yielding line that stays hard: it sees how ease goes and leaves before the day is out. 王弼: 明禍福之所生
(*míng huò fú zhī suǒ shēng*, "seeing clearly where misfortune and fortune are born").

**Line 3.** 盱豫，悔。遲有悔 (*xū yù, huǐ. chí yǒu huǐ*, "gaping ease: regret. Slow: there is
regret"). 說文's 盱 (*xū*) is 張目也 (*zhāng mù yě*, "eyes stretched wide"). At the seam, right under
line 4, looking up at the source of ease — and too slow either to follow it or to leave.

**Line 4.** 由豫，大有得。勿疑。朋盍簪 (*yóu yù, dà yǒu dé. wù yí. péng hé zān*, "ease comes
through it: great gain. Do not doubt. Friends gather, as hair to the pin"). The one firm line, the
thunder's base. 14's name stands inside it: 大有 (*dà yǒu*, "great having"), gained. 簪 (*zān*) is
ordinarily a hairpin; 王弼 glosses it otherwise — 盍合也簪疾也 (*hé hé yě zān jí yě*, "盍 is joining;
簪 is quick") — friends gather fast. Either way the many close on one fastening point, on one
condition: 勿疑 (*wù yí*, "do not doubt").

**Line 5.** 貞疾，恆不死 (*zhēn jí, héng bù sǐ*, "holding firm: sickness; it lasts, and does not
die"). Here the older sense of 貞 (*zhēn*) as "the inquiry" reads more naturally: "an inquiry into
sickness — it lingers, and does not die". The ruler's place, yielding, riding the firm line below it
(小象: 乘剛也, *chéng gāng yě*, "it rides the firm"): a centre that has lost its strength to the line
beneath and goes on anyway — 中未亡也 (*zhōng wèi wáng yě*, "the centre is not yet lost").

**Line 6.** 冥豫，成有渝，无咎 (*míng yù, chéng yǒu yú, wú jiù*, "benighted ease; when it is
complete, there is a change: no blame"). 說文's 渝 (*yú*) is 變汙也 (*biàn wū yě*, "to change, to
stain"). The top of thunder, the end of ease. Even ease gone dark ends without blame, if it changes —
the only way out the line offers.

### In a life now

A good season: a holiday that has gone on a little long, retirement, the months after a victory, a
company growing without effort, a love that has settled, a rich country at peace. Ease is not
condemned — line 4 is great gain, and the judgment uses ease to organise and to move. But the lines
sort the people in it. The one through whom ease comes, the person who did the work, gains, and
friends gather fast, so long as there is no doubt (4). Those who crow about it (1), those who live by
gazing up at someone else's (3), those who drift on after dark (6) go wrong, or must change. Line 2
is the counsel most needed: know your line, see how it goes, leave before the day is out. Line 5 is
the chronic illness, lived with and not died of.

The 大象 gives ease a form: 先王以作樂崇德 (*xiān wáng yǐ zuò yuè chóng dé*, "the former rulers made
music and honoured integrity"), and 殷薦之上帝，以配祖考 (*yīn jiàn zhī shàng dì, yǐ pèi zǔ kǎo*,
"offered it richly to the god above, and set their ancestors beside it"). 說文's 樂 (*yuè*,
"music") is 象鼓鞞 (*xiàng gǔ pí*, "a picture of drums"); read *lè*, the same graph is joy. Thunder
out of the ground, made into drums, and turned toward integrity.

### An echo from the Laozi

**Two contacts, and each faces the other way.** The first is the graph itself. 豫 (*yù*) is in the
Laozi once, chapter 15, and Shalom's English is *"Careful, like wading across a river in winter."*
There 豫 is caution — feeling for the ice at each step; here, in the core, it is ease. One
character, two senses, and the two Englishes rightly differ. The 繫辭下's watchman at the double
gate stands nearer the Laozi's word than any line of 16 does.

The second is the three graphs 不終日 (*bù zhōng rì*, "not the whole day"), recorded in
[`tao-te-ching-relation.md`](../tao-te-ching-relation.md) § 4 as a contact point: 16.2, and chapter
23's *"A downpour does not last out the day."* It passes the two-question test — the graphs are in
the core, and Shalom's English answers to them. **But the subjects part.** In the Laozi it is
nature's excess that cannot last the day. In 16.2 it is a person who will not stay the whole day —
who sees ease for what it is and goes first. One is a fact about storms; the other is a choice. This
reading keeps them apart.

- 德 (*dé*) — *integrity*, never *virtue* (locked)
