## The signature — a reading

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-24. This reads the figure through the Wings — the declared lens
([`README.md`](../README.md#the-lens)) — and it is a reading, not a ruling. The name still comes from
the lines ([`read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums`](../principles/read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums.md)).*

**The signature.** Within: **thunder** (震 · yang · moving out). Without: **thunder** (震 · yang · moving out).
**Doubled** — the yang pole of thunder ↔ wind, twice.

**What the tradition reads.** The 大象傳 reads the doubling as repetition: 洊雷，震 (*jiàn léi, zhèn*,
"thunder upon thunder: *zhèn*"), and from it 君子以恐懼修省 (*jūnzǐ yǐ kǒngjù xiū xǐng*, "the noble one, in
fear and dread, puts himself in order and looks within"). The 彖傳 makes the fear productive: 震來虩虩，恐致福也
(*zhèn lái xìxì, kǒng zhì fú yě*, "the clap comes, and people start in alarm: fear brings good fortune"),
and 驚遠而懼邇也 (*jīng yuǎn ér jù ěr yě*, "it startles the far and frightens the near"). The one who keeps
the rites is the one who 不喪匕鬯 (*bù sàng bǐ chàng*, "does not drop the ladle or the sacrificial wine").

**What the polarity implies.** Each trigram is one firm line under two open ones: a single force at the
base, driving up through what gives way. Doubled, the force comes twice, at the first line and the fourth,
and the lines show the two landing very differently. The first comes clean and ends in laughter: the first line is
good fortune. The second has no ground to strike from, because it rises off the two soft lines beneath it:
震遂泥 (*zhèn suì ní*, "the clap sinks into the mud"), and the 小象傳 (*xiǎo xiàng zhuàn*, the line commentary) says 未光也 (*wèi guāng yě*, "it has not
yet shone"). The structure suggests that thunder doubled is not thunder twice as strong. The first clap does
the work and the second is spent. Set it beside 52. 說卦 (*shuōguà*) calls 震 (*zhèn*) the first draw and 艮 (*gèn*) the third (一索, *yī
suǒ*; 三索, *sān suǒ*): force at the bottom here, closure at the top there. Thunder is best at its start;
the mountain, as 52 shows, is best at its end. And every line here is someone the clap happens to. The
actor is never inside the figure.

**For the name.** **Thunderclap** — the signature strains it a little: 說文 (*shuōwén*)'s single strike fits the first line, but the
doubling and the lines are repetition — 震蘇蘇 (*zhèn sūsū*, "the claps roll on, and he is dazed"), 震往來厲
(*zhèn wǎng lái lì*, "claps going and coming: peril"). **Candidates the signature suggests:** *Shock* —
survives, from 震來虩虩 ("shock comes, and people start") to 震遂泥 ("the shock bogs in the mud");
*Peals* — survives the rolling lines, but bends at the top line's 震不于其躬，于其鄰 (*zhèn bù yú qí gōng, yú qí lín*,
"it strikes not his own body but his neighbour's"), since a peal does not strike.

## The heart of it

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-25 — an interpretive reading, not a translation. Each claim is marked by
layer: the core text (the judgment and the six lines, Western Zhou), the Wings (Warring States to
Han), 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*, 3rd century), and echoes of the Tao Te Ching in this project's own translation.
The formula words — 吉, 凶, 无咎, 亨, 孚, 貞 — carry working glosses until WORKLIST A4 and A6 rule on
them ([`hexagram-names.md`](../hexagram-names.md)).*

### Essence

**The clap comes from outside; what matters is what is still in your hands when it has passed.** 說文
(*shuōwén*) gives 震 (*zhèn*) as 劈歴，振物者 (*pī lì, zhèn wù zhě*, "the thunderclap, the thing that
shakes things"), written with 雨 (*yǔ*, "rain") over its sound. In the core text nobody makes the
thunder. Every line is someone it happens to — startled, robbed, dazed, bogged, trembling. And the
judgment does not ask them not to be afraid. It asks them not to drop what they are carrying: 不喪匕鬯
(*bù sàng bǐ chàng*, "the ladle and the fragrant wine are not dropped").

### The polarities in it

Thunder within, thunder without: one pole, twice. Each trigram is a single firm line under two open
ones — force at the base, driving up through what gives way (see *The signature*, above). Doubled,
the force comes at line 1 and again at line 4, and the two land very differently. The first has
ground under it and ends in laughter. The second rises off the soft lines beneath it and sinks. **The
first clap does the work; the second is spent.** Thunder is strongest at its beginning, and the figure
says so.

### In nature

Watch a storm come in. Count from the flash to the clap: about three seconds for each kilometre. A
near strike is one hard crack; a far one rolls and grumbles, because the sound reaches you from the
whole length of the bolt at different moments. That is the difference between the first line's single
clap and the fifth line's 震往來 (*zhèn wǎng lái*, "claps going and coming"). Thunder is seldom heard
beyond twenty-odd kilometres, and 百里 (*bǎi lǐ*, "a hundred *lǐ*") is roughly twice that: the
judgment's 震驚百里 (*zhèn jīng bǎi lǐ*, "the clap shocks for a hundred *lǐ*") is the reach of the
alarm, not of the sound.

Notice what the living do. At a close clap, birds go silent and cattle bunch; a few minutes later the
birds start again. That is the first line exactly: 震來虩虩，后笑言啞啞 (*zhèn lái xìxì, hòu xiào yán
yāyā*, "the clap comes — a start of fear; afterwards, laughing talk"). 說文 gives 虩 (*xì*) as 恐懼
(*kǒngjù*, "fear"), with 虎 (*hǔ*, "tiger") in the graph, and adds a second sense: 蠅虎 (*yíng hǔ*,
"the fly-tiger"), the jumping spider, which freezes and then springs. 啞 (*yā*) is simply 笑也 (*xiào
yě*, "laughter").

And watch where the storm goes. Thunder comes with rain — the 雨 (*yǔ*, "rain") is in the graph — and
the rain turns the ground to mud. The fourth line's 震遂泥 (*zhèn suì ní*, "the clap sinks into the
mud") is a storm that has rained itself out: the claps grow low and dull, and go nowhere. 說卦
(*shuōguà*) ch 5 sets thunder in the east, where the year's growth begins: 萬物出乎震 (*wàn wù chū hū
zhèn*, "the countless things come out in thunder").

### The movement of the lines

**The judgment.** 震：亨。震來虩虩，笑言啞啞。震驚百里，不喪匕鬯 (*zhèn: hēng. zhèn lái xìxì, xiào yán
yāyā. zhèn jīng bǎi lǐ, bù sàng bǐ chàng*) — "Thunderclap *(proposed)*: it gets through. The clap
comes — a start of fear; then laughing talk. The clap shocks for a hundred *lǐ*; the ladle and the
fragrant wine are not dropped." 說文 gives 匕 (*bǐ*) as the spoon 所以用比取飯 (*suǒ yǐ yòng bǐ qǔ fàn*,
"for taking up food") and 鬯 (*chàng*) as a millet wine brewed with fragrant herbs for the offerings.
In the middle of the shock, someone at the altar keeps hold.

**Line 1** is the clap landing well. 震來虩虩，后笑言啞啞，吉 (*zhèn lái xìxì, hòu xiào yán yāyā, jí*, "the
clap comes — a start of fear; afterwards, laughing talk — auspicious"). Fear first, then relief: the
one good-fortune line.

**Line 2** loses something. 震來厲，億喪貝，躋于九陵，勿逐，七日得 (*zhèn lái lì, yì sàng bèi, jī yú jiǔ
líng, wù zhú, qī rì dé*, "the clap comes: peril; the cowries are lost; climb the nine hills; do not
chase them — in seven days you get them"). The counsel is exact: get to high ground and let the loss
go. 小象 (*xiǎo xiàng*) says why it is shaken: 乘剛也 (*chéng gāng yě*, "it rides on the firm"),
sitting right on top of the first clap.

**Line 3** is dazed and told to move. 震蘇蘇，震行无眚 (*zhèn sūsū, zhèn xíng wú shěng*, "the claps roll
on, and one is dazed; moving with the clap, no harm"). 說文 gives 眚 (*shěng*) as a film growing over
the eye — harm that blinds. Moving keeps the eyes clear.

**Line 4** bogs. 震遂泥 (*zhèn suì ní*, "the clap sinks into the mud"). No verdict; 小象 says only
未光也 (*wèi guāng yě*, "it has not yet shone").

**Line 5**, the centre above, stays at work. 震往來厲，億无喪，有事 (*zhèn wǎng lái lì, yì wú sàng, yǒu
shì*, "claps going and coming: peril; nothing is lost; there are affairs to attend to"). The storm is
still overhead, and the tasks are still there.

**Line 6** is the end of the storm, and the most frightened line. 震索索，視矍矍，征凶。震不于其躬，于其鄰，
无咎。婚媾有言 (*zhèn suǒsuǒ, shì juéjué, zhēng xiōng. zhèn bù yú qí gōng, yú qí lín, wú jiù. hūngòu yǒu
yán*, "claps, and trembling; eyes darting — setting out is ominous. It strikes not one's own body but
the neighbour's — no blame. In a marriage, there is talk"). 說文 pictures 矍 (*jué*) as a bird held in
the hand, trying to get away. Do not set out while shaking; take the neighbour's clap as the warning.

### In a life now

This is the figure for the thing that arrives without asking: the diagnosis, the call in the night,
the redundancy notice, the earthquake, the news from far away that is suddenly near. It does not say
be calm. It says the fear is how it begins (line 1), and that it passes into laughter more often than
the frightened believe. Then it counsels: **keep hold of what you were carrying** — the ladle, the
wine, the work in your hands (the judgment, line 5); **let the loss go and get to high ground; do not
chase it** (line 2); **keep moving rather than freeze** (line 3); **do not make big moves while you
are still shaking** (line 6); and **when it strikes the house next door, take it as your warning**,
not your catastrophe.

The 大象 turns it to practice: 君子以恐懼修省 (*jūnzǐ yǐ kǒngjù xiū xǐng*, "the noble one, in fear and
dread, puts themselves in order and looks within"). The fear is not argued away. It is used.

### An echo from the Laozi

**One shared graph, and the two books part over what it is for.** The judgment's 驚 (*jīng*, "shock")
is in the core text, and it is the Laozi's word in chapter 13: *"Favor and disgrace are both a shock"*. The Laozi goes on to locate the trouble in the body — *"The reason I
have great trouble is that I have a body"* — and this hexagram's last line spares exactly that:
the clap strikes 不于其躬 (*bù yú qí gōng*, "not one's own body"), and 說文 defines 身 (*shēn*, "body")
as 躳 (*gōng*, "the trunk") — another way of writing the line's 躬 (*gōng*, "one's own body"). But the two books want different things from the shock. The Laozi
would have us stop being shaken by favour and disgrace, because they are not worth it. The 彖
(*tuàn*) says the shaking is worth a great deal: 恐致福也 (*kǒng zhì fú yě*, "fear brings good
fortune"). The thunder is real, and the fear is right. **The Laozi dissolves the shock; this hexagram
keeps it and keeps hold of the ladle.**
