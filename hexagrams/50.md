## The signature — a reading

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-24. This reads the figure through the Wings — the declared lens
([`README.md`](../README.md#the-lens)) — and it is a reading, not a ruling. The name still comes from
the lines ([`read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums`](../principles/read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums.md)).*

**The signature.** Within: **wind** (巽 · yin · entering). Without: **fire** (離 · yin · attaching).
**Across** — thunder ↔ wind and water ↔ fire, both at their yin poles.

**What the tradition reads.** The 彖傳 opens by calling the figure a picture: 鼎，象也 (*dǐng, xiàng yě*,
"the cauldron is an image"). Then it reads the trigrams as fuel and flame: 以木巽火，亨飪也 (*yǐ mù xùn
huǒ, pēng rèn yě*, "wood entering fire: that is cooking"). The 大象傳 has 木上有火 (*mù shàng yǒu huǒ*,
"fire above wood"), and from it 君子以正位凝命 (*jūnzǐ yǐ zhèng wèi níng mìng*, "the noble one sets the
positions right and lets the allotted course set firm"). What is cooked is made to set.

**What the polarity implies.** This is 49 turned over, and the fire changes places. In 49 the fire was
within and the thing itself changed. Here the fire is without, and the vessel stands between the heat and
what it cooks. Wind within, open at the base, is wood fed in from below a little at a time. Fire without,
hollow at the centre, clings to what it is given. So the heat is steady and fed, never a blaze, and the
change goes on inside a container that does not change. That is the difference 雜卦 (*záguà*, "the Mixed Figures") names: 革去故也，鼎取新也
(*gé qù gù yě, dǐng qǔ xīn yě*, "*gé* removes the old; *dǐng* takes up the new"). The open base is also the weak
point, and the lines know it. The first line tips the vessel to empty it, 鼎顛趾，利出否 (*dǐng diān zhǐ, lì
chū pǐ*, "the cauldron upended on its feet: good for tipping out the spoiled"). The fourth breaks it:
鼎折足，覆公餗 (*dǐng zhé zú, fù gōng sù*, "the cauldron's leg breaks and the lord's stew spills"). The
pair's own seam is in the third line: 鼎耳革 (*dǐng ěr gé*, "the cauldron's ears are changed"). When 革 (*gé*) gets into the
vessel, 其行塞 (*qí xíng sè*, "its going is blocked").

**For the name.** **The Cauldron** — the signature supports it: the figure is the one object in which
entering and attaching are held apart and made to work, and the 彖傳 reads it as a picture of that object.
**Candidates the signature suggests:** *Cooking* — fails the lines: no line cooks; they name the vessel's
feet, belly, ears and carrying-bar (鼎黃耳金鉉, *dǐng huáng ěr jīn xuàn*, "the cauldron's yellow ears and
bronze bar"); *The Vessel* — survives every line (鼎有實, *dǐng yǒu shí*, "the vessel has its fill"), but
器 (*qì*) is locked to *vessel*, so it collides.

## The heart of it

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-25 — an interpretive reading, not a translation. Each claim is marked by
layer: the core text (the judgment and the six lines, Western Zhou), the Wings (Warring States to
Han), Wang Bi, the 3rd-century commentator, and echoes of the Tao Te Ching in this project's own
translation. The formula words — 吉 ("auspicious"), 凶 ("ominous"), 无咎 ("no blame"), 亨 ("gets
through"), 孚 ("confidence"), 貞 ("holding firm") — carry working glosses until WORKLIST A4 and A6
rule on them ([`hexagram-names.md`](../hexagram-names.md)).*

### Essence

**A vessel that stands steady over the fire, so that what is inside it can change.** Every line
names a part of the cauldron — its feet, its fill, its ears, its legs, its carrying-bar — and the
good and ill fortune of each line is the good and ill fortune of that part. The Shuowen, the c. 100
CE character dictionary, gives 鼎 ("cauldron") as 三足兩耳，和五味之寶器也 ("three feet, two ears: the treasured
vessel for blending the five flavours"). The judgment is among the shortest in the book and among
the best: 元吉，亨 ("auspicious from the source; it gets through"). Where 49 changed the thing itself,
this figure keeps the change in something that does not change.

### The polarities in it

Wind within, open at its base; fire without, hollow at its centre (see *The signature*, above). The
lower trigram enters, the upper clings, and the vessel stands between them. So the heat is fed from
below a little at a time and held above, never a blaze, and what cooks is contained. The figure is
49 turned over, and the Zagua, the Wing that pairs the hexagrams, names the pair in two phrases:
革去故也，鼎取新也 ("Shedding removes the old; the Cauldron takes up the new"). **The shedding clears; the
cauldron holds what comes next.** And the text is honest about where the structure is weak: the open
base is where the vessel is upended (line 1), and the seam above it is where the leg breaks (line
4).

### In nature

The Great Image, the Wing on the whole figure, gives 木上有火 ("fire above wood"), and the Shuowen reads
the whole figure as a cooking fire: 巽木於下者爲鼎，象析木以炊也 ("wind-and-wood below makes the cauldron: it
pictures split wood for cooking"). Watch a wood fire under a pot. Split wood catches where round
logs smoulder; the fire draws its air in at the base and its heat goes up; it has to be fed, stick
by stick, or it dies or flares. The Tuan, the Wing on the judgment, puts it exactly — 以木巽火 ("wood
entering fire").

Look at the vessel itself. Three legs stand firm on any ground, because three points always meet a
plane: a tripod never rocks, where a four-legged table does. The same fact is its weakness. There is
no spare leg, and when one breaks the whole thing goes over — line 4, 鼎折足 ("the cauldron's leg
breaks"). The ears are loops on the rim; a bar passed through them is how a hot, full bronze is
lifted from the fire. The Shuowen defines the bar, 鉉 ("carrying-bar"), by what it does: 舉鼎也
("lifting the cauldron").

The Great Image's second half has its own kitchen image: 君子以正位凝命 ("the noble one sets the positions
right and lets the allotted course set firm"). 凝 ("set firm") is what cooked food does as it cools —
fat whitening on a stock, the stock itself setting to jelly overnight. What the fire changed, the
cooling fixes.

### The movement of the lines

**The judgment.** 鼎：元吉，亨 ("The Cauldron *(proposed)*. Auspicious from the source; it gets
through."). No condition, no warning.

**Line 1** empties the vessel first. 鼎顛趾，利出否，得妾以其子，无咎 ("the cauldron upended on its feet; advantage
in tipping out the spoiled; taking a concubine for the sake of her son — no blame"). Before anything
new goes in, what has gone bad comes out. The second image says the same in household terms: someone
of low standing taken in because of what they bear.

**Line 2** is full. 鼎有實，我仇有疾，不我能即，吉 ("the cauldron has its fill; my rival is ailing and cannot come
near me — auspicious"). Wang Bi reads the fullness as a limit: 益之則溢 ("add to it and it spills
over").

**Line 3**, at the top of the lower trigram, is the pair's own seam. 鼎耳革，其行塞，雉膏不食，方雨虧悔，終吉 ("the
cauldron's ears are changed; its going is blocked; the pheasant fat is not eaten; as the rain comes,
regret wanes — in the end, auspicious"). When 革 ("change") gets into the vessel, it cannot be
carried; the good food sits uneaten. But it ends well.

**Line 4** is the one ominous line. 鼎折足，覆公餗，其形渥，凶 ("the cauldron's leg breaks; the lord's stew
spills; its form is drenched — ominous"). The Shuowen gives 渥 ("drenched") as 霑也 ("soaked"). Wang Bi
names the cause: 知小謀大 ("small knowledge planning great things") — a load past what the leg could
bear.

**Line 5**, the soft centre above, receives. 鼎黃耳金鉉，利貞 ("the cauldron's yellow ears and bronze bar;
advantage in holding firm"). The open line is the ear, open to the bar that lifts it.

**Line 6** is the vessel complete. 鼎玉鉉，大吉，无不利 ("the cauldron's jade bar; greatly auspicious; nothing
without advantage"). Jade is hard but brittle, not a material for hauling a full bronze; this
reading takes the jade bar as the sign of a vessel that has come to rest in its place. The good
fortune rises through the figure — 2, 3 in the end, 5, and the greatest at 6 — broken once, at 4.

### In a life now

This is the figure for whatever holds a change long enough for it to become nourishment: a kitchen
that feeds a family through hard years, an institution that takes a reform and makes it ordinary, a
craft practice, a studio, a daily discipline. It counsels three plain things. **Empty before you
fill** (line 1). **Know when the vessel is full** (line 2, and Wang Bi's spill). **Do not load it
past its legs** (line 4) — the one ruinous line is the one where the vessel was asked to carry more
than it could. And it holds the uncomfortable line 3: sometimes the vessel is changed so that it
cannot be lifted, and the best food goes uneaten for a while. The text does not say how to wait; it
says only that rain comes, and in the end, auspicious.

The Great Image turns it to practice: set the positions right, and let what has been cooked set. A
change is not finished when it is made; it is finished when it has cooled into something people can
live on.

### An echo from the Laozi

**Only in the Wings and in Wang Bi, and there the books part.** The core text never calls the
cauldron a 器 ("vessel"); the Xugua, the Wing on the sequence, does, by implication, in the next step
— 主器者莫若長子 ("none is fitter to govern the vessel than the eldest son") — and Wang Bi calls it 調和之器
("the vessel for blending"). Chapter 11 of the Laozi says of that same 器 ("vessel"): *"Knead clay to
make a vessel. / In what is not there, / the vessel has its use."* Wang Bi brings the thought into
line 3: 鼎之爲義虚中以待物者也 ("the meaning of the cauldron is to be empty in the middle, waiting for
things"). But the core text praises the opposite at line 2: 鼎有實 ("the cauldron has its fill"),
auspicious. The Laozi's vessel is useful for being empty; this one is useful for what is in it.
**The difference stands**, and this reading does not settle it.

- 木 ("wood") — in the Great Image's 木上有火 ("fire above wood") and the Tuan's 以木巽火 ("wood entering
  fire")
