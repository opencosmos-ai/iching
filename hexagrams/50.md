## The signature — a reading

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-24. This reads the figure through the Wings — the declared lens
([`README.md`](../README.md#the-lens)) — and it is a reading, not a ruling. The name still comes from
the lines ([`read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums`](../principles/read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums.md)).*

**The signature.** Within: **wind** (巽 · yin · entering). Without: **fire** (離 · yin · attaching).
**Across** — thunder ↔ wind and water ↔ fire, both at their yin poles.

**What the tradition reads.** The 彖傳 opens by calling the figure a picture: 鼎，象也 (*dǐng, xiàng yě*,
"the cauldron is an image"). Then it reads the trigrams as fuel and flame: 以木巽火，亨飪也 (*yǐ mù xùn
huǒ, pēng rèn yě*, "wood entering fire: that is cooking"). The 大象傳 has 木上有火 (*mù shàng yǒu huǒ*,
"fire above wood"), and from it 君子以正位凝命 (*jūnzǐ yǐ zhèng wèi níng mìng*, "the noble one sets the
positions right and lets the allotted course set firm"). What is cooked is made to set.

**What the polarity implies.** This is 49 turned over, and the fire changes places. In 49 the fire was
within and the thing itself changed. Here the fire is without, and the vessel stands between the heat and
what it cooks. Wind within, open at the base, is wood fed in from below a little at a time. Fire without,
hollow at the centre, clings to what it is given. So the heat is steady and fed, never a blaze, and the
change goes on inside a container that does not change. That is the difference 雜卦 (*záguà*, "the Mixed Figures") names: 革去故也，鼎取新也
(*gé qù gù yě, dǐng qǔ xīn yě*, "*gé* removes the old; *dǐng* takes up the new"). The open base is also the weak
point, and the lines know it. The first line tips the vessel to empty it, 鼎顛趾，利出否 (*dǐng diān zhǐ, lì
chū pǐ*, "the cauldron upended on its feet: good for tipping out the spoiled"). The fourth breaks it:
鼎折足，覆公餗 (*dǐng zhé zú, fù gōng sù*, "the cauldron's leg breaks and the lord's stew spills"). The
pair's own seam is in the third line: 鼎耳革 (*dǐng ěr gé*, "the cauldron's ears are changed"). When 革 (*gé*) gets into the
vessel, 其行塞 (*qí xíng sè*, "its going is blocked").

**For the name.** **The Cauldron** — the signature supports it: the figure is the one object in which
entering and attaching are held apart and made to work, and the 彖傳 reads it as a picture of that object.
**Candidates the signature suggests:** *Cooking* — fails the lines: no line cooks; they name the vessel's
feet, belly, ears and carrying-bar (鼎黃耳金鉉, *dǐng huáng ěr jīn xuàn*, "the cauldron's yellow ears and
bronze bar"); *The Vessel* — survives every line (鼎有實, *dǐng yǒu shí*, "the vessel has its fill"), but
器 (*qì*) is locked to *vessel*, so it collides.

## The heart of it

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-25 — an interpretive reading, not a translation. Each claim is marked by
layer: the core text (the judgment and the six lines, Western Zhou), the Wings (Warring States to
Han), 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*, 3rd century), and echoes of the Tao Te Ching in this project's own translation.
The formula words — 吉, 凶, 无咎, 亨, 孚, 貞 — carry working glosses until WORKLIST A4 and A6 rule on
them ([`hexagram-names.md`](../hexagram-names.md)).*

### Essence

**A vessel that stands steady over the fire, so that what is inside it can change.** Every line names
a part of the cauldron — its feet, its fill, its ears, its legs, its carrying-bar — and the good and
ill fortune of each line is the good and ill fortune of that part. 說文 (*shuōwén*) gives 鼎 (*dǐng*)
as 三足兩耳，和五味之寶器也 (*sān zú liǎng ěr, hé wǔ wèi zhī bǎo qì yě*, "three feet, two ears: the
treasured vessel for blending the five flavours"). The judgment is among the shortest in the book and
among the best: 元吉，亨 (*yuán jí, hēng*, "auspicious from the source; it gets through"). Where 49
changed the thing itself, this figure keeps the change in something that does not change.

### The polarities in it

Wind within, open at its base; fire without, hollow at its centre (see *The signature*, above). The
lower trigram enters, the upper clings, and the vessel stands between them. So the heat is fed from
below a little at a time and held above, never a blaze, and what cooks is contained. The figure is 49
turned over, and 雜卦 (*záguà*) names the pair in two phrases: 革去故也，鼎取新也 (*gé qù gù yě, dǐng qǔ
xīn yě*, "*gé* removes the old; *dǐng* takes up the new"). **The shedding clears; the cauldron holds
what comes next.** And the text is honest about where the structure is weak: the open base is where
the vessel is upended (line 1), and the seam above it is where the leg breaks (line 4).

### In nature

The 大象 (*dà xiàng*) gives 木上有火 (*mù shàng yǒu huǒ*, "fire above wood"), and 說文 reads the whole
figure as a cooking fire: 巽木於下者爲鼎，象析木以炊也 (*xùn mù yú xià zhě wéi dǐng, xiàng xī mù yǐ
chuī yě*, "wind-and-wood below makes the cauldron: it pictures split wood for cooking"). Watch a wood
fire under a pot. Split wood catches where round logs smoulder; the fire draws its air in at the base
and its heat goes up; it has to be fed, stick by stick, or it dies or flares. The 彖 (*tuàn*) puts it
exactly — 以木巽火 (*yǐ mù xùn huǒ*, "wood entering fire").

Look at the vessel itself. Three legs stand firm on any ground, because three points always meet a
plane: a tripod never rocks, where a four-legged table does. The same fact is its weakness. There is
no spare leg, and when one breaks the whole thing goes over — line 4, 鼎折足 (*dǐng zhé zú*, "the
cauldron's leg breaks"). The ears are loops on the rim; a bar passed through them is how a hot, full
bronze is lifted from the fire. 說文 defines the bar, 鉉 (*xuàn*), by what it does: 舉鼎也 (*jǔ dǐng
yě*, "lifting the cauldron").

The 大象's second half has its own kitchen image: 君子以正位凝命 (*jūnzǐ yǐ zhèng wèi níng mìng*,
"the noble one sets the positions right and lets the allotted course set firm"). 凝 (*níng*) is what
cooked food does as it cools — fat whitening on a stock, the stock itself setting to jelly overnight.
What the fire changed, the cooling fixes.

### The movement of the lines

**The judgment.** 鼎：元吉，亨 (*dǐng: yuán jí, hēng*) — "The Cauldron *(proposed)*. Auspicious from the
source; it gets through." No condition, no warning.

**Line 1** empties the vessel first. 鼎顛趾，利出否，得妾以其子，无咎 (*dǐng diān zhǐ, lì chū pǐ, dé qiè yǐ
qí zǐ, wú jiù*, "the cauldron upended on its feet; advantage in tipping out the spoiled; taking a
concubine for the sake of her son — no blame"). Before anything new goes in, what has gone bad comes
out. The second image says the same in household terms: someone of low standing taken in because of
what they bear.

**Line 2** is full. 鼎有實，我仇有疾，不我能即，吉 (*dǐng yǒu shí, wǒ chóu yǒu jí, bù wǒ néng jí, jí*,
"the cauldron has its fill; my rival is ailing and cannot come near me — auspicious"). 王弼 reads the
fullness as a limit: 益之則溢 (*yì zhī zé yì*, "add to it and it spills over").

**Line 3**, at the top of the lower trigram, is the pair's own seam. 鼎耳革，其行塞，雉膏不食，方雨虧悔，
終吉 (*dǐng ěr gé, qí xíng sè, zhì gāo bù shí, fāng yǔ kuī huǐ, zhōng jí*, "the cauldron's ears are
changed; its going is blocked; the pheasant fat is not eaten; as the rain comes, regret wanes — in the
end, auspicious"). When 革 (*gé*, "change") gets into the vessel, it cannot be carried; the good food
sits uneaten. But it ends well.

**Line 4** is the one ominous line. 鼎折足，覆公餗，其形渥，凶 (*dǐng zhé zú, fù gōng sù, qí xíng wò,
xiōng*, "the cauldron's leg breaks; the lord's stew spills; its form is drenched — ominous"). 說文 gives
渥 (*wò*) as 霑也 (*zhān yě*, "soaked"). 王弼 names the cause: 知小謀大 (*zhī xiǎo móu dà*, "small
knowledge planning great things") — a load past what the leg could bear.

**Line 5**, the soft centre above, receives. 鼎黃耳金鉉，利貞 (*dǐng huáng ěr jīn xuàn, lì zhēn*, "the
cauldron's yellow ears and bronze bar; advantage in holding firm"). The open line is the ear, open to
the bar that lifts it.

**Line 6** is the vessel complete. 鼎玉鉉，大吉，无不利 (*dǐng yù xuàn, dà jí, wú bù lì*, "the cauldron's
jade bar; greatly auspicious; nothing without advantage"). Jade is hard but brittle, not
a material for hauling a full bronze; this reading takes the jade bar as the sign of a vessel that has come
to rest in its place. The good fortune rises through the figure — 2, 3
in the end, 5, and the greatest at 6 — broken once, at 4.

### In a life now

This is the figure for whatever holds a change long enough for it to become nourishment: a kitchen
that feeds a family through hard years, an institution that takes a reform and makes it ordinary, a
craft practice, a studio, a daily discipline. It counsels three plain things. **Empty before you
fill** (line 1). **Know when the vessel is full** (line 2, and 王弼's spill). **Do not load it past its
legs** (line 4) — the one ruinous line is the one where the vessel was asked to carry more than it
could. And it holds the uncomfortable line 3: sometimes the vessel is changed so that it cannot be
lifted, and the best food goes uneaten for a while. The text does not say how to wait; it says only that
rain comes, and in the end, auspicious.

The 大象 turns it to practice: set the positions right, and let what has been cooked set. A change
is not finished when it is made; it is finished when it has cooled into something people can live on.

### An echo from the Laozi

**Only in the Wings and in 王弼, and there the books part.** The core text never calls the cauldron a
器 (*qì*, "vessel"); the 序卦 (*xùguà*) does, by implication, in the next step — 主器者莫若長子 (*zhǔ qì
zhě mò ruò zhǎng zǐ*, "none is fitter to govern the vessel than the eldest son") — and 王弼 calls it
調和之器 (*tiáo hé zhī qì*, "the vessel for blending"). Chapter 11 of the Laozi says of that same
器: *"Knead clay to make a vessel. / In what is not there, / the vessel has its use."* 王弼 brings the
thought into line 3: 鼎之爲義虚中以待物者也 (*dǐng zhī wéi yì xū zhōng yǐ dài wù zhě yě*, "the meaning
of the cauldron is to be empty in the middle, waiting for things"). But the core text praises the
opposite at line 2: 鼎有實 (*dǐng yǒu shí*, "the cauldron has its fill"), auspicious. The Laozi's
vessel is useful for being empty; this one is useful for what is in it. **The difference stands**,
and this reading does not settle it.

- 木 (*mù*) — *wood*, in the 大象's 木上有火 and the 彖's 以木巽火
- 命 (*mìng*) — *the allotted course*, never *mandate* or *destiny* (locked); 凝命 *let the allotted course set firm*
