## The signature — a reading

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-24. This reads the figure through the Wings — the declared lens
([`README.md`](../README.md#the-lens)) — and it is a reading, not a ruling. The name still comes from
the lines ([`read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums`](../principles/read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums.md)).*

**The signature.** Within: **Fire** (離 · yin · attaching). Without: **Water** (坎 · yang · sinking in).
**Crossed** — water ↔ fire, the rising pole below the sinking one, so they move into each other.

**What the tradition reads.** The 大象 (*dà xiàng*): 水在火上，既濟；君子以思患而豫防之 (*shuǐ zài huǒ shàng, jì jì; jūnzǐ yǐ sī huàn ér yù fáng zhī*), "water above fire: 既濟; the noble one thinks of trouble and guards against it beforehand." The 彖 (*tuàn*): 剛柔正而位當也 (*gāng róu zhèng ér wèi dāng yě*), "firm and yielding are upright and the positions fit" — `101010` is the one figure in the book with every line in its own place — and 終止則亂 (*zhōng zhǐ zé luàn*), "stopping at the end brings disorder." 雜卦 (*záguà*): 既濟定也 (*jì jì dìng yě*), "既濟 is settled." The tradition reads arrival as the moment vigilance begins.

**What the polarity implies.** Fire rises and water sinks; with fire below and water above, each moves toward the other and they meet. This is the pot on the hearth — the one arrangement in which water and fire are of use to each other. But the meeting is the dangerous part. 水火不相射 (*shuǐ huǒ bù xiāng shè*), "water and fire do not quench each other", holds only while something stands between them; left alone, the fire boils the water away, or the water boils over and puts the fire out. And since every line already fits its place, no change can improve the figure — any line that moves breaks the fit. That is the structural reason for the judgment's 初吉終亂 (*chū jí zhōng luàn*), "auspicious at the start, disorder at the end": the start is the only perfect state there is. The lines tell it as a fox at a river — 濡其尾 (*rú qí wěi*), "its tail wet", at the base, and at the top 濡其首，厲 (*rú qí shǒu, lì*), "its head wet: peril" — across, and still going under. The structure suggests 既濟 is not rest but a balance kept by attention, which is why the 大象 has the noble one 思患 (*sī huàn*), "think of trouble", before any has come.

**For the name.** *Already Across* — the signature deepens it: *already* carries the 彖's warning, arrival as a state that can only decline. **Candidates the signature suggests:** *Settled* — fails the lines: it is 雜卦's word, and it drops the river the pair needs (64.3 未濟，征凶，利涉大川, *wèi jì, zhēng xiōng, lì shè dà chuān*, "not yet across: setting out is ominous; favourable to cross the great river"); *Arrived* — survives 63's own lines (初吉終亂 is what arriving is), but strains 64, where *Not Yet Arrived* would lose that same river.

## The heart of it

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-25 — an interpretive reading, not a translation. Each claim is marked by
layer: the core text (the judgment and the six lines, Western Zhou), the Wings (Warring States to
Han), 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*, 3rd century), and echoes of the Tao Te Ching in this project's own translation.
The formula words — 吉, 凶, 无咎, 亨, 孚, 貞 — carry working glosses until WORKLIST A4 and A6 rule on
them ([`hexagram-names.md`](../hexagram-names.md)).*

### Essence

**Across, and done — and from done the only way left is undone.** The judgment is four words of
warning after two of grace: 初吉終亂 (*chū jí zhōng luàn*, "auspicious at the start, disorder at the
end"). 說文 (*shuōwén*) has the first graph, 既 (*jì*), as 小食也 (*xiǎo shí yě*, "a small meal"): a
food vessel (皀) beside a figure with its head turned away (旡) — the meal eaten, the diner turning
from the bowl. 濟 (*jì*) is, in 說文, the name of a river; the text uses it as crossing one. *Already
Across* (proposed): the river behind, the bowl empty.

### The polarities in it

Fire within rises; water without sinks. With fire below and water above they move into each
other, and in `101010` every line stands in its own place — the one figure in the book where
nothing is out of order. The signature has drawn the consequence: a perfect state can only change
for the worse. **The lines bear it out, and more gently than the structure would.** They do not
fall from 吉 (*jí*, "auspicious") straight to 凶 (*xiōng*, "ominous"). There is no 凶 in 63 at all.
They go from a wheel held back to a head under water — from caution kept to caution lost.

### In nature

The pot on the fire is the 大象 (*dà xiàng*)'s image — 水在火上 (*shuǐ zài huǒ shàng*, "water above
fire") — and it is a thing to watch rather than to imagine. At the moment water comes to the boil
the arrangement is at its most useful and least stable: turn away, and it boils over onto the
flame or boils dry and cracks the pot. Every cook knows the boil is the moment you do not leave.

The fox is at the river in both of the last two figures, and 說文 describes it with care. It gives
狐 (*hú*) three marks, and one is 小前大後 (*xiǎo qián dà hòu*, "small in front, large behind"). That
is accurate: a fox's head is narrow and its brush is thick, often a third of its length. Dry, the
brush is balance and warmth; soaked, it is weight dragging behind a swimmer. Foxes swim when they
must, and they avoid it. Another of 說文's marks is 死則丘首 (*sǐ zé qiū shǒu*, "dying, it turns its
head toward its hill") — a creature that keeps its bearing. 63 puts the fox's tail in the water
at the first line and its head at the last.

The fourth line is a boatman's line. 王弼 reads 繻 (*rú*) as 濡 (*rú*, "wet") and 衣袽 (*yī rú*,
"rags") as 所以塞舟漏也 (*suǒ yǐ sāi zhōu lòu yě*, "what is used to stop a boat's leak"). Anyone who
has rowed an old wooden boat knows the seams weep, and you keep a rag to hand.

### The movement of the lines

**The judgment.** 既濟：亨小。利貞。初吉終亂 (*jì jì: hēng xiǎo. lì zhēn. chū jí zhōng luàn*) —
"Already across: it gets through, in small things. Advantageous to hold firm. Auspicious at the
start, disorder at the end." The 彖 (*tuàn*) reads 亨小 as 小者亨也 (*xiǎo zhě hēng yě*, "it is the
small that get through"); 王弼 adds 小者不遺乃爲皆濟 (*xiǎo zhě bù yí nǎi wéi jiē jì*, "only when the
small are not left behind is everyone across"). The 彖 also names the cause of the end: 終止則亂
(*zhōng zhǐ zé luàn*, "stopping at the end brings disorder") — disorder comes from stopping, not
from going on.

**Line 1.** 曳其輪，濡其尾，无咎 (*yè qí lún, rú qí wěi, wú jiù*, "dragging its wheel, wetting its
tail — no blame"). The cart held back, the fox's tail touching water: the start of a crossing, done
slowly. No blame.

**Line 2.** 婦喪其茀，勿逐，七日得 (*fù sàng qí fú, wù zhú, qī rì dé*, "the wife loses her 茀 (*fú*);
do not chase it — in seven days it is found"). 王弼 has 茀 as 首飾 (*shǒu shì*, "a head ornament");
others read a carriage screen. The soft centre loses something and is told to wait: it comes back.

**Line 3.** 高宗伐鬼方，三年克之，小人勿用 (*gāozōng fá guǐ fāng, sān nián kè zhī, xiǎo rén wù yòng*,
"Gāozōng, the Shang ruler, attacks the 鬼方, *Guǐfāng*, a people beyond the border; three years to subdue it; small
people are not to be used"). The 小象 (*xiǎo xiàng*) names the cost in one word: 憊也 (*bèi yě*,
"exhaustion"). Even a won war, at the top of the lower trigram, is a crossing that drains.

**Line 4.** 繻有衣袽，終日戒 (*rú yǒu yī rú, zhōng rì jiè*, "a leak — have rags; on guard all day").
說文 reads 繻 differently, as coloured silk, which gives "fine silk turns to rags". Either way the
line is watchfulness at the seam, where the water begins.

**Line 5.** 東鄰殺牛，不如西鄰之禴祭，實受其福 (*dōng lín shā niú, bù rú xī lín zhī yuè jì, shí shòu qí
fú*, "the eastern neighbour slaughters an ox; it is not as good as the western neighbour's spring
offering, which truly receives the blessing"). 禴 (*yuè*) is the thin offering of the lean season. The
小象: 不如西鄰之時也 (*bù rú xī lín zhī shí yě*, "it is the western neighbour's timing"). 王弼: 在於合時不在於豐也
(*zài yú hé shí bù zài yú fēng yě*, "it lies in fitting the time, not in abundance"). At the height
of success the text praises the smaller gift.

**Line 6.** 濡其首，厲 (*rú qí shǒu, lì*, "its head wet: peril"). The fox that went on past the far
bank is under. The 小象: 何可久也 (*hé kě jiǔ yě*, "how could it last?").

### In a life now

This is the figure for the week after the wedding, the first year of a business that worked, the
remission, the book finished, the house paid off, the reform passed. It does not spoil any of them.
It says two things, in its own terms: the good was at the start, and what follows depends on not
stopping. Keep the wheel dragged a little. Keep a rag by the seam. Make the small offering on time
rather than the great one late. Lose a thing, and let it come back.

The 大象 turns it to practice: 君子以思患而豫防之 (*jūnzǐ yǐ sī huàn ér yù fáng zhī*, "the noble one
thinks of trouble and guards against it beforehand"). 王弼's gloss is one line, and
sufficient: 旣濟不忘未濟也 (*jì jì bù wàng wèi jì yě*, "already across, do not forget not yet
across").

### An echo from the Laozi

**The core text shares a graph with the Laozi, and they stand on opposite sides of it.** Chapter 64
in Shalom's translation: *"Order it before it falls into disorder."* The graph is 亂 (*luàn*,
"disorder"), locked, and it is the last word of 63's judgment. The Laozi glossary notes that 亂
names a tangle somebody's hands are in — addressable, if reached early. **Here the books part.**
The Laozi's counsel is all before: handle it before it exists. 63 is already after; the crossing is
made, the ordering done, and its counsel is to keep it — the rag, the dragged wheel, the thought of
trouble. The Laozi would have you not need the crossing; the Changes has crossed, and says what that
costs to hold.
