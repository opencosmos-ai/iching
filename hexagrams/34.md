## The signature — a reading

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-24. This reads the figure through the Wings — the declared lens
([`README.md`](../README.md#the-lens)) — and it is a reading, not a ruling. The name still comes from
the lines ([`read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums`](../principles/read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums.md)).*

**The signature.** Within: **sky** (乾 *qián* · yang · vigour). Without: **thunder** (震 *zhèn* · yang · moving out).
**Across** — the yang pole of sky ↔ earth within, the yang pole of thunder ↔ wind without.

**What the tradition reads.** The 彖 reads the name as a sentence and the figure as its cause:
大壯，大者壯也。剛以動，故壯 (*dà zhuàng, dà zhě zhuàng yě. gāng yǐ dòng, gù zhuàng*, "great vigour: it is
the great that is vigorous. Firm, and so moving — therefore vigorous"). It then turns the strength
toward uprightness: 大者正也 (*dà zhě zhèng yě*, "the great is upright"). The 大象 draws the same line:
雷在天上，大壯；君子以非禮弗履 (*léi zài tiān shàng, dà zhuàng; jūnzǐ yǐ fēi lǐ fú lǚ*, "thunder above the
sky: great vigour; the noble one does not tread where the rites do not lead"). Strength at its height,
for the tradition, is safe only when it is upright.

**What the polarity implies.** Read from the bottom, the figure is four firm lines in a row and then
two open ones: the force climbs unbroken out of the sky within and into thunder, whose one firm line
sits at its base — at the exact seam where it meets the sky. Nothing in the figure stands between the
vigour and its outlet. Where 33 set the same sky *above* a mountain, out of reach, here the sky is
*within* and has already become motion. And above the thunder there is nothing firm at all — only the
two open lines, empty air.

The structure suggests the danger is not weakness but the lack of anything to stop against. A figure
with no mountain in it must bring its own stop, and that is why the lines keep supplying a fence: 羝羊觸藩，
羸其角 (*dī yáng chù fān, léi qí jiǎo*, "the ram butts the fence and tangles its horns"), and at the top,
不能退，不能遂 (*bù néng tuì, bù néng suì*, "it cannot go back, it cannot go through"). The 大象's 非禮弗履
(*fēi lǐ fú lǚ*, "not treading off the rites") arrives here from the other side — the rite is the fence
a strong thing chooses before it hits one. 雜卦 says it outright: 大壯則止 (*dà zhuàng zé zhǐ*, "great
vigour means stopping"). 33 holds within and lets the sky go; 34 has the sky within and must learn to
hold.

**For the name.** **Great Vigour** — the signature supports the sense but strains the word: *vigour* is
this project's English for 乾's own action, 健 (*jiàn*), and 34's signature contains 乾, so the name
reuses the reading's vocabulary for a figure that carries it — the case the principle's "not as a source
of vocabulary" clause warns about. **Candidates the signature suggests:** *Great Force*, *Great Strength* —
*Great Force* survives the lines: 壯于趾 (*zhuàng yú zhǐ*, "force in the toes"), 小人用壯 (*xiǎorén yòng
zhuàng*, "the small person uses force"), 壯于大輿之輹 (*zhuàng yú dà yú zhī fù*, "force in the axle-block of
the great cart"); *Great Strength* also survives those three lines, but sits against the lock that gives
強 (*qiáng*) *strong*, so it needs a seam in the glossary entry.

## The heart of it

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-25 — an interpretive reading, not a translation. Each claim is marked by
layer: the core text (the judgment and the six lines, Western Zhou), the Wings (Warring States to
Han), 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*, 3rd century), and echoes of the Tao Te Ching in this project's own translation.
The formula words — 吉, 凶, 无咎, 亨, 孚, 貞 — carry working glosses until WORKLIST A4 and A6 rule on
them ([`hexagram-names.md`](../hexagram-names.md)).*

### Essence

**Strength at its full is not the problem; where it is put is the whole question.** 說文
(*shuōwén*) gives 壯 (*zhuàng*) as 大也 (*dà yě*, "great"), a grown man, 士 (*shì*), with 爿 (*qiáng*)
for the sound — so 大壯 (*dà zhuàng*, "great vigour", proposed) is greatness said twice. The core text
never doubts that the vigour is real. It asks where it sits: in the toes, where it marches out and
fails; in a ram's horns, where it tangles in a hedge; in the lashing of a great cart's axle, where it
carries a load and the hedge gives way. The judgment has only one thing to say, and it is not *more*:
it is *hold*.

### The polarities in it

The sky within is vigour; the thunder without is moving out. Four firm lines climb unbroken from
the base, and above them there is nothing firm at all — the force is already on its way out, and
nothing in the figure stops it (see *The signature*, above). So the text brings the stop. Every
failure in the lines is force meeting a limit it did not choose: the march that goes wrong, the
hedge, the ram that can go neither back nor through. And the two lines that go well are both firm
lines in a yielding seat — the second and the fourth, as 王弼 notices: 以陽居隂 (*yǐ yáng jū yīn*,
"yang dwelling in a yin place") at the one, 以陽處隂 (*yǐ yáng chǔ yīn*, the same) at the other. **Where the figure has no stop, the good lines carry their own.**

### In nature

Thunder in summer rolls across the whole sky and is gone; a storm that breaks at noon has spent
itself by evening. The 大象 sets it at the very top — 雷在天上 (*léi zài tiān shàng*, "thunder above
the sky").

The creature of this hexagram is the ram, 羝羊 (*dī yáng*), and 說文 is exact: 羝 (*dī*) is 牡羊也
(*mǔ yáng yě*, "a male of the flock"). Rams butt. In the rut they drive at each other and at
anything in the way, and a ram with curling horns that thrusts into a hedge or a fence can get his
head through and then cannot pull it back: the curl that slid in going forward hooks coming out. That
is the top line to the letter — 不能退，不能遂 (*bù néng tuì, bù néng suì*, "it cannot go back, it
cannot go through"). 藩 (*fān*) is in 說文 屏也 (*píng yě*, "a screen"): a hedge of brush, the fence of
a fold. And 羸 (*léi*), the word for what happens to the horns, 說文 gives as 瘦也 (*shòu yě*, "lean,
worn thin"); the line is usually read as the horns caught fast. Either way the ram comes out the
worse.

Then look under a farm cart. 輹 (*fù*) is in 說文 車軸縛也 (*chē zhóu fù yě*, "the lashing that binds
the axle"): the strap that ties axle to bed and takes the whole weight of the load. Nobody sees it.
Everything rides on it.

### The movement of the lines

**The judgment.** 大壯：利貞 (*dà zhuàng: lì zhēn*) — "Great vigour: advantageous to hold firm." No
亨 (*hēng*, "gets through"), no 元 (*yuán*, "from the source") — the strength needs no promise of
success, only a stance. The 彖 reads 大者正也 (*dà zhě zhèng yě*, "the great is upright").

**Line 1** is vigour at the bottom. 壯于趾，征凶，有孚 (*zhuàng yú zhǐ, zhēng xiōng, yǒu fú*, "vigour
in the toes; marching out — ominous; there is confidence"). The confidence is real and it is not
enough; the 小象 says 其孚窮也 (*qí fú qióng yě*, "its confidence runs out").

**Line 2** is two characters long. 貞吉 (*zhēn jí*, "holding firm: auspicious"). At the centre, strength
needs no image and no warning.

**Line 3** is the sky at its peak, firm in a firm seat. 小人用壯，君子用罔，貞厲。羝羊觸藩，羸其角
(*xiǎorén yòng zhuàng, jūnzǐ yòng wǎng, zhēn lì. dī yáng chù fān, léi qí jiǎo*, "small people use
vigour; the noble one uses 罔 — holding firm, peril. The ram butts the hedge and wears its horns").
罔 (*wǎng*) is a net, or it is *not*: the noble one either uses a net or uses nothing. 王弼 reads the
net as one that snares its user. The core text leaves it open, and so should the English.

**Line 4** is the seam, and here the hedge breaks. 貞吉悔亡，藩決不羸，壯于大輿之輹 (*zhēn jí huǐ wáng,
fān jué bù léi, zhuàng yú dà yú zhī fù*, "holding firm, auspicious, regret goes; the hedge bursts, the
horns come through whole — vigour in the axle-lashing of the great cart"). The same force that
tangled at line 3 goes through at line 4, because it has moved from the horns to the load.

**Line 5** lets it go. 喪羊于易，无悔 (*sàng yáng yú yì, wú huǐ*, "loses the ram at 易 — no regret").
王弼 takes 易 (*yì*) as *ease*: to lose one's force in easy times, not in hardship. It may also be a
place. Either way the ruler's seat is the one where the ram is given up.

**Line 6** is the ram again, and stuck. 羝羊觸藩，不能退，不能遂，无攸利，艱則吉 (*dī yáng chù fān, bù
néng tuì, bù néng suì, wú yōu lì, jiān zé jí*, "the ram butts the hedge — cannot go back, cannot go
through; nothing advantageous; hardship, then auspicious"). The 小象 adds 咎不長也 (*jiù bù cháng
yě*, "the blame does not last"). The ram stops only when it is held fast, and that is where the
line turns.

### In a life now

This is the figure for the first year of a promotion, a campaign that is winning, a body recovered
and wanting everything at once, a young person who has just found out how strong they are, a company
in a boom. The text never tells anyone to be less strong. It tells them where not to put it: not in
the toes (marching out on vigour alone), not in the horns (butting what fences them), but in the
axle — under the load, where no one watches and everything depends on it. And it tells them the
ruler's move: to let the ram go easily, before losing it hard. When the horns are already caught,
the text offers one plain way out: accept the hardship; the blame does not last.

The 大象 turns this into practice: 君子以非禮弗履 (*jūnzǐ yǐ fēi lǐ fú lǚ*, "the noble one does not
tread where the rites do not lead").

### An echo from the Laozi

**The two books share this graph, and part over it.** Chapters 30 and 55 both say: *"When things reach
their prime, they decay. / This is called not-Tao. Not-Tao comes to an early end."* The Chinese is
物壯 (*wù zhuàng*, "things in their prime"), and *reach their prime* answers to 壯 (*zhuàng*) — the same graph as this
hexagram's name and as three of its lines, in the core text itself. So the echo passes both
questions.

But the verdicts differ. For the Laozi, 壯 is already the beginning of the end; it takes the side of
the soft and yielding. The Zhou text does not. Its judgment is *advantageous to hold firm*; its
fourth line puts vigour in the axle and calls it auspicious. It does not condemn strength; it tells
strength where to stand. **The difference is the point, and this reading leaves it open**
([`tao-te-ching-relation.md`](../tao-te-ching-relation.md) § 5, on 剛 (*gāng*, "firm") and 柔 (*róu*,
"soft")): read through the Laozi, this hexagram would become a warning against its own name.

### Words kept

- 大壯 (*dà zhuàng*) — *great vigour*, the proposed name (not yet approved); the signature notes the strain — *vigour* is also this project's word for 乾's 健 (*jiàn*) — and *force* is the live alternative
- 壯 (*zhuàng*) — *vigour* throughout here; never *strong*, which is locked to 強 (*qiáng*)
- 大 (*dà*) — *great*, never *mighty*, never *grand* (locked)
- 天 (*tiān*) — *sky*, as a tier: 雷在天上 (*léi zài tiān shàng*), "thunder above the sky" (locked)
- 羝羊 · 藩 · 輹 (*dī yáng · fān · fù*) — *the ram · the hedge · the axle-lashing*
- 罔 (*wǎng*) — left open: *a net* or *not*
- 禮 (*lǐ*) — *the rites*
- 貞 · 孚 (*zhēn · fú*) — *holding firm · confidence*, working glosses (A6)
- 凶 · 厲 · 悔亡 · 无悔 (*xiōng · lì · huǐ wáng · wú huǐ*) — *ominous · peril · regret goes · no regret*, working glosses (A4)
