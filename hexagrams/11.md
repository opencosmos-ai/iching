## The signature — a reading

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-24. This reads the figure through the Wings — the declared lens
([`README.md`](../README.md#the-lens)) — and it is a reading, not a ruling. The name still comes from
the lines ([`read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums`](../principles/read-a-figure-through-its-spectrums.md)).*

**The signature.** Within: **sky** (乾 · yang · vigour). Without: **earth** (坤 · yin · going along).
**Crossed** — sky ↔ earth, the rising pole below and the sinking pole above, so they move into each other.

**What the tradition reads.** The 彖 (*tuàn*) reads 泰 (*tài*) as communication:
天地交而萬物通也 (*tiān dì jiāo ér wàn wù tōng yě*, "sky and earth meet, and the countless things get
through"), 上下交而其志同也 (*shàng xià jiāo ér qí zhì tóng yě*, "above and below meet, and their
aims are the same"). It names the signature itself: 內陽而外陰，內健而外順 (*nèi yáng ér wài yīn,
nèi jiàn ér wài shùn*, "yang within and yin without; vigour within and going along without"). The
大象 (*dà xiàng*) says 天地交，泰 (*tiān dì jiāo, tài*, "sky and earth meet: 泰"), and the sovereign
以財成天地之道 (*yǐ cái chéng tiān dì zhī dào*, "cuts to measure and completes the Tao of sky and
earth") — a ruler who shapes what the exchange provides.

**What the polarity implies.** The figure is the world upside down. 說卦's (*shuōguà*) 天地定位
(*tiān dì dìng wèi*, "sky and earth set the positions") puts sky above; 11 puts it underneath, and
the wrong placement is the good one. Each trigram, moving by its own nature, travels toward the
other: vigour rises into the earth, the earth settles into the sky. Every line is in transit, and the
judgment says so in the figure's own terms — 小往大來 (*xiǎo wǎng dà lái*, "the small goes, the great
comes"): the yielding lines are outward and leaving, the firm inward and arriving. But a crossing in
motion does not stay crossed. Once the sky has risen through the earth it stands above it, and that
is 12. The lines know it: 无平不陂，无往不復 (*wú píng bù bēi, wú wǎng bù fù*, "no level without a
slope, no going without a return", 11.3), and the top line ends with 城復于隍 (*chéng fù yú huáng*,
"the city wall falls back into its moat") — earth returning to the ditch it was dug from. The
structure suggests 泰 (*tài*, the figure's name) is a passage, not a condition: good for exactly as long as its parts are still
moving through one another. That is the strongest structural argument against *Peace*, which names
a rest.

**For the name.** **Flow** — strains slightly: the signature is two bodies passing into each other
from opposite directions, and *flow* is one current going one way; it keeps the motion and loses the
exchange. **Candidates the signature suggests:** *Meeting* — survives: 帝乙歸妹 (*dì yǐ guī mèi*,
"Di Yi gives his younger sister in marriage", 11.5) is a meeting of two houses, and 无往不復 (*wú wǎng bù fù*, "no going without a return") is its
two-way grammar; *Exchange* — survives, thinly: 翩翩，不富以其鄰 (*piān piān, bù fù yǐ qí lín*,
"fluttering down, not rich, along with its neighbours", 11.4) is earth coming down to the sky
holding nothing back, but no line names a thing given for a thing received. The name waits on 亨
(*hēng*, the judgment's last word, not yet ruled — A6).

## The heart of it

*Draft for Shalom, 2026-09-25 — an interpretive reading, not a translation. Each claim is marked by
layer: the core text (the judgment and the six lines, Western Zhou), the Wings (Warring States to
Han), 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*, 3rd century), and echoes of the Tao Te Ching in this project's own translation.
The formula words — 吉, 凶, 无咎, 亨, 孚, 貞 — carry working glosses until WORKLIST A4 and A6 rule on
them ([`hexagram-names.md`](../hexagram-names.md)).*

### Essence

**The small goes out and the great comes in: a meeting that holds only while it moves, and that
already carries its return.** 說文 (*shuōwén*) gives 泰 (*tài*) as 滑也 (*huá yě*, "slippery,
smooth") — two hands and water: water running through the hands. The core text's lines are all
passage — grass coming up together, a river waded, birds coming down, a sister given in marriage
— and in the middle of them, the plainest sentence in the figure: 无平不陂，无往不復 (*wú píng bù
bēi, wú wǎng bù fù*, "no level without a slope, no going without a return"). 泰 is the good time
that knows it is a time.

### The polarities in it

Sky within, earth without — the world upside down, and the wrong placement is the good one. The
sky, which rises, is underneath; the earth, which settles, is on top; so each, doing only what it
does, moves into the other (see *The signature*, above). Nothing has to be arranged. The meeting is
made by two natures going their own ways from the wrong starting places. The same motion that joins
them will, carried through, separate them, and the lines do not hide that: the last line's wall
falls into its moat.

### In nature

Look at the grass in the first line: 茅 (*máo*), 說文 菅也 (*jiān yě*), a coarse thatching grass.
Thatching grasses of this kind spread by runners under the soil, so that pulling one stem
brings up a string of shoots joined at the root. 拔茅茹以其彙 (*bá máo rú yǐ qí huì*, "pull up the couch grass,
roots and all, with its kind"): what comes up comes up together. 王弼 sees the same thing: 茹相牽引之貌也
(*rú xiāng qiān yǐn zhī mào yě*, "茹 is the look of things pulling one another along").

The 大象 (*dà xiàng*) gives 天地交 (*tiān dì jiāo*, "sky and earth meet"). 說文's 交 (*jiāo*) is
交脛也 (*jiāo jìng yě*, "crossed shins") — a standing figure with its legs crossed. The weather
shows it every day in a wet season: warmth draws water up off the fields as mist, and it comes
back as rain. The top line holds the other half of the observation. 隍 (*huáng*) is 說文's dry
moat — 有水曰池，無水曰隍 (*yǒu shuǐ yuē chí, wú shuǐ yuē huáng*, "with water it is a 池; without
water, a 隍"). A rammed-earth wall is built of the earth dug from its own ditch; left unmended, each
rain slumps a little of it back where it came from.

### The movement of the lines

**The judgment.** 泰：小往大來，吉亨 (*tài: xiǎo wǎng dà lái, jí hēng*) — "泰: the small goes, the great
comes — auspicious; it gets through." 亨 (*hēng*, "gets through") stands here in the judgment, and
nowhere in the six lines. Set that beside 12 (*below*), which withholds 亨 from its judgment and
gives it to its two lowest lines.

**Line 1** 拔茅茹以其彙，征吉 (*bá máo rú yǐ qí huì, zhēng jí*, "pull up the couch grass, roots and all,
with its kind; setting out — auspicious"). The first firm line rises, and the others come with it.

**Line 2** 包荒。用馮河，不遐遺；朋亡。得尚于中行 (*bāo huāng. yòng píng hé, bù xiá yí; péng wáng. dé
shàng yú zhōng xíng*, "wrapping the wasteland; wading the river; not leaving the distant behind;
companions gone; honoured for walking the middle"). 說文 makes 荒 (*huāng*) 蕪也 (*wú yě*, "overgrown").
The lower centre takes in the rough ground, crosses without a boat, forgets no one far off, and
belongs to no faction.

**Line 3** 无平不陂，无往不復，艱貞无咎。勿恤其孚，于食有福 (*wú píng bù bēi, wú wǎng bù fù, jiān zhēn
wú jiù. wù xù qí fú, yú shí yǒu fú*, "no level without a slope, no going without a return; hard
going, holding firm — no blame. Do not fret over its confidence; in eating there is blessing"). The
seam, where sky gives way to earth, is where the turn is named. 艱 (*jiān*) is 說文's 土難治也 (*tǔ nán
zhì yě*, "earth hard to work"). 王弼 places the line at 天地將各分復之際 (*tiān dì jiāng
gè fēn fù zhī jì*, "the moment when sky and earth are about to part, each back to its own place").
The counsel: hold firm, and eat what is on the table.

**Line 4** 翩翩，不富以其鄰；不戒以孚 (*piān piān, bù fù yǐ qí lín; bù jiè yǐ fú*, "fluttering,
fluttering — not rich, along with its neighbours; unguarded, with confidence"). 翩 (*piān*) is 疾飛也
(*jí fēi yě*, "quick flight"). The earth's first line comes down like a flock onto a field, all at
once, without being called. Compare 9.5, rich along with its neighbours: here the neighbours come
with nothing, and need no warning.

**Line 5** 帝乙歸妹，以祉，元吉 (*dì yǐ guī mèi, yǐ zhǐ, yuán jí*, "Dì Yǐ gives his younger sister in
marriage; with blessing — from the source, auspicious"). 說文's 歸 (*guī*) is 女嫁也 (*nǚ jià yě*, "a
woman marrying"). The upper centre, a yielding line in the ruler's place, goes down to meet the
firm centre below: the meeting of two houses is the meeting of sky and earth in a human form.

**Line 6** 城復于隍，勿用師，自邑告命，貞吝 (*chéng fù yú huáng, wù yòng shī, zì yì gào mìng, zhēn lìn*,
"the city wall falls back into its moat; do not use the army; from the town, make known the allotted
course; holding firm — stinting"). The turn line 3 named has come. The line does not counsel force;
it counsels speaking to one's own town, and even holding firm now comes up short.

### In a life now

This is the figure for the good season: a marriage in its early years, a team that has found its
rhythm, a recovery going well, a harvest coming in, a country at a moment of openness. The text
does not warn against it or tell anyone to enjoy it less. It asks for three things. Take in the
rough and the distant, and belong to no faction (line 2). Know that level ground slopes and what
goes comes back — then hold firm and eat (line 3). And when the wall does slump into its ditch, do
not send the army against it; speak to your own people (line 6).

The 大象 turns it toward work: 后以財成天地之道 (*hòu yǐ cái chéng tiān dì zhī dào*, "the sovereign
cuts to measure and completes the Tao of sky and earth"). What the meeting provides still has to be
shaped. 王弼 gives the reason: in a time of great getting-through 物失其節 (*wù shī qí jié*, "things lose
their measure").

### An echo from the Laozi

**The two books share 復 (*fù*, "return") — and part over what returning means.** In the Laozi's
chapter 16, *"I observe their return. / Things come to their fullest, / Each returns again to its
root."* 11.3's 无往不復, "no going without a return", has the same graph in the core text, and in
both books return is not an accident but the way things move.

In the Laozi, return is to the root and to stillness, and is welcomed. In 11 it is the level
turning to slope and the wall slumping into the moat; it is met with 艱貞 (*jiān zhēn*, "hard going,
holding firm"), not with repose. And a caution runs the other way: the Laozi's own 泰, in chapter
29, is something the sage *lets go of* — *"grandeur"*. **This book's 泰 is not that excess**, and
the Laozi's use is a reason not to render 泰 as abundance or prosperity.
